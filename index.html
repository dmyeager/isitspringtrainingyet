News Around the League

THE PROCESSION DESK: THREE-QUARTERS OF A MILLION TURN OUT FOR THE CHAMPIONS — THE POLICE CALL IT PERHAPS THE LARGEST GATHERING IN WISCONSIN'S HISTORY, YOUNT RIDES IN ON UECKER'S HARLEY, AND FRELICK PLEDGES A HUNDRED THOUSAND BEERS

The procession this paper trailed yesterday morning came off grander than even Milwaukee dared forecast. By the constabulary's count more than 750,000 souls turned out for the parade and the rally that followed — a figure the police say may stand, their preliminary arithmetic suggests, as the largest crowd for a single day's event in the state's history.2 Fans staked their places along Wisconsin Avenue before dawn; by 8 o'clock thousands had already filled the lakefront grounds at Henry Maier Festival Park, where the 23,000-seat amphitheater held the rally and every stage on the 75 acres carried it by screen. The only prior parade in the club's annals, in 1982, honored a team that had lost the seventh game of the World's Series in St. Louis. This one, 44 years later, honored winners.

The rostrum ran long and merry. Robin Yount arrived astride Bob Uecker's Harley-Davidson, precisely as he did at the rally of 1982, and Ted Simmons spoke for the men of that older club; Rollie Fingers praised the bullpen and the scoreless-innings man Aaron Ashby by name. The Series' most valuable player William Contreras thanked the multitude in English; Brice Turang confessed what the final out had done to his nerves; the captain Christian Yelich called the day worth its long wait; and the hitting coach Daniel Vogelbach sang karaoke. The manager Pat Murphy rode the route at the parade's rear in a lawn chair with his children and quoted Bruce Springsteen from the stage, the owner Mark Attanasio pronounced the state's 6 million residents "one family" for the day, and the president of baseball operations Matt Arnold addressed the crowd with a beer in hand — a detail the crowd took as a credential. As for Sal Frelick, whose leap into the Lambeau stands this paper reported yesterday, he surveyed the sea of humanity and vowed to drink 100,000 beers in its honor — a program this desk neither endorses nor entirely doubts.2

THE OPTIONS LEDGER, SECOND DAY: TAMPA BAY TURNS LOOSE THE ARM THAT WON FIFTEEN AND THE GLOVE THAT ROAMED CENTER — WHILE BALTIMORE PAYS EFLIN TWO MILLIONS TO MEND HIS ELBOW ON THE OPEN MARKET

The settling of accounts that opened Thursday, as this paper's ledger recorded yesterday, ran through its second day without pause. The Gulf Coast club declined its mutual options on the pitcher Nick Martinez and the center fielder Cedric Mullins on Friday, loosing both to the market.3 Martinez, 36, gave Tampa Bay everything $9 million could reasonably buy — 15 wins against 6 defeats, a 3.10 earned-run average across 180 innings and 31 starts, and 16 October innings of five-run ball atop it — but the club chose the $4 million buyout over a $20 million encore, and the pitcher is reported to have declined his own half of the bargain in any case. Mullins required less deliberation: the one-time Oriole batted .205 with 15 home runs and 21 stolen bases in 137 games, and the half-million-dollar buyout was always the likelier end of his $10 million clause. The Tampa men must still rule on Drew Rasmussen's $20 million figure and a smaller one on the shortstop Taylor Walls before Monday.3

In Baltimore the ledger closed on Zach Eflin the way all parties expected: the Baltimores declined his $25 million mutual option and will pay the 32-year-old right-hander a $2 million buyout to seek his fortune on a mending elbow.4 Eflin's season amounted to one start — he left his debut on March 31 with elbow trouble and underwent the Tommy John operation on April 8 — yet he kept close to the club through his rehabilitation and professes a wish to return, so a reunion is not foreclosed. He joins a general exodus from the Baltimore rotation, Trevor Rogers, Chris Bassitt, Keegan Akin and Andrew Kittredge all likewise free, though the bullpen at least holds firm: Ryan Helsley chose the warm hearth on Thursday, as this paper set down yesterday, and the club has decisions yet to make on its outfielder Luis Robert Jr. and first baseman Ryan Mountcastle.4

THE APPOINTMENTS DESK: HOUSTON GOES TO THE CARDINALS' SCHOOLHOUSE FOR ITS NEW GENERAL MANAGER — FLORES, A LEFT-HANDER OF THREE-HUNDRED-AND-FIFTY GAMES IN HIS PITCHING DAYS, MUST FIRST FIND THE CLUB A MANAGER

The Houston nine filled their long-vacant front chair Friday night, naming Randy Flores general manager — the fourth such hire of the owner Jim Crane's tenure, and the end of a search that began when the club parted with Dana Brown better than two months ago.5 Flores, 51, comes from ten seasons in the St. Louis office, where he arrived in 2015 as director of scouting and served as an assistant general manager from 2019 onward. His is a varied résumé even by the modern front office's standards: a left-handed reliever in 350 major-league games for the Cardinals, Rockies, Rangers and Twins; a pitching coach at Southern California; a television analyst; the founder of a sports-video concern. "Randy has had a huge impact on the Cardinals over the last 10 seasons," said Crane, praising his new man's eye for talent.5

The work will not wait for the introductions, which come Monday at Daikin Park. The Astros won the American League West at 81-81 — an eighth divisional flag in ten seasons, and surely the least ornamented of the lot — and then declined to renew the manager Joe Espada after three seasons. Flores' first labor is therefore to find the club a manager, with a bench coach and sundry staff positions to fill behind him.5

THE FAR PACIFIC DESK: THE LOWEST EARNED-RUN FIGURE IN JAPAN ASKS FOR PASSAGE — SEIBU WILL POST THE RIGHT-HANDER TAIRA AND HIS 1.36, AND ALL THIRTY CLUBS GET FIVE-AND-FORTY DAYS TO BID

The winter market gains an arm from across the Pacific. The Saitama Seibu Lions announced they will approve the posting of the right-hander Kaima Taira, who led all of Nippon Professional Baseball in earned-run average this season — a 1.36 figure with a 0.96 WHIP across 23 games in his return to the rotation, after years of distinguished relief work that included a 1.71 average and 31 saves in 2025. Across eight NPB seasons the man's ledger reads 1.78.6 He turns 27 on November 15; once the posting is official, all 30 clubs will have a 45-day window to treat with him, with no restriction on the terms since he is past 25, and Seibu collecting a release fee scaled to whatever deal he strikes.

The scouts' reports read warmly: a fastball run up to the mid-to-upper 90s from a frame of just 5-foot-8, with a cutter, sweeper, changeup and splitter behind it, and a studious turn of mind toward the analytics besides.6 The skeptic's note concerns the frame — only Marcus Stroman, since Fred Norman in 1979, has pitched 100 innings in a season at that height or shorter, and some clubs will fancy Taira a reliever for exactly that reason, though his preference for starting may steer where he signs. He joins Teruaki Sato, the Hanshin Tigers slugger who took the Central League's Triple Crown, among the first NPB men seeking passage to these shores this winter.6

THE COMMERCE DESK: THE WINTER'S SECOND SWAP IS STRUCK FOR PLAIN COIN — DETROIT BUYS THE FOUR-TIMES-CLAIMED CHENG FROM BOSTON, AND THE WAIVER WIRE'S GREAT WANDERER GETS A PERCH

The winter's second trade followed its first by a single day, and was more modest still. Detroit on Friday acquired the infielder Tsung-Che Cheng from the Fenway men for cash considerations — the Bengals' first move of the offseason.7 Cheng, 25, a left-handed-hitting shortstop and second baseman from Taiwan who stands 5-foot-8, knows the waiver wire as a commuter knows his streetcar: cast off by Pittsburgh last offseason after five years in that system, he was claimed by the Rays, the Mets, the Nationals and the Red Sox inside a single month. Boston needed the roster room for men returning from the long infirmary list; Detroit paid cash rather than wait and bid against the wire.

The season past gave the little infielder his first major-league hit, on June 26 in his Boston debut, and a .256 average over 14 games in the big club's employ, with a .241/.345/.390 line and 8 home runs across 94 games at Worcester beneath it. In Detroit he finds a familiar face: the infielder Hao-Yu Lee, his countryman and teammate on the Chinese Taipei side at the World Baseball Classic, where Cheng reached base eight times in four games and stole four bags. The Bengals' own arithmetic now presses — 36 men on the roster of 40, with eight more to be accommodated off the 60-day infirmary list by Monday — so the wanderer's perch, it must be said, is not yet a guaranteed one.7

All Hallows' Eve, and for once the calendar's costume holiday finds the game with nothing left to disguise. The schedule page stands white this morning, as it will for 144 mornings more, until Opening Night restores the genuine article on Wednesday, the 24th of March, 2027.1 The Herald's lamps stay lit regardless: the options ledger runs through Monday,3 the awards season draws near, and the first arms from Japan are already asking for harbor. Let the neighborhood's small goblins have their evening — this desk has watched a Milwaukee club that spent fifty-seven seasons dressed as a contender finally take the mask off, and no store-bought fright will top it. Until the next dispatch, keep your scorecards near and your enthusiasms nearer.
