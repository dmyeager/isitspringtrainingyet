Volume I · No. 125 · Tuesday, the Tenth of November, Two Thousand and Twenty-Six

No contests this day; the hot stove burns bright.

134 days until Opening Night of the 2027 season — the first pitch of the new campaign, the clubs yet to be named (Wednesday, the 24th of March, 2027).

News Around the League

THE LAURELS DESK: THE ROOKIES' PRIZES GO TO THE EVERYDAY MEN — McGONIGLE OF THE MOTOR CITY NINE TAKES THE AMERICAN HONOR ON EIGHT-AND-TWENTY FIRST-PLACE BALLOTS OF THIRTY, STEWART OF THE QUEEN CITY THE NATIONAL, AND THE CLEVELAND WAIT OUTLIVES ANOTHER NOVEMBER

The first verdicts of the writers' week are opened, and both prizes went to men who never took a day off the argument. The Baseball Writers' Association of America announced Monday evening that Kevin McGonigle, the Tigers' infielder of 22, is the American League's Rookie of the Year, and that Sal Stewart, the Reds' first baseman of the same age, is the National League's — the ballots, the reader is reminded, having been sealed before the postseason began, two writers voting in every city. The American League's two-man argument of the everyday against the every-fifth-day, as yesterday's columns framed it, resolved emphatically for the everyday: McGonigle took 28 of the 30 first-place votes and 204 points, against 122 for the Cleveland left-hander Parker Messick, who collected the other two firsts. The National League's was the more divided sitting, and Stewart won it all the same — 20 first-place votes and 170 points, over the Mets' outfielder Carson Benge (7 firsts, 107 points) and the Cardinals' second baseman JJ Wetherholt (3 firsts, 99).23

The case for McGonigle wanted no advocate. In his age-21 season the young man led the American League outright in plate appearances, with 707, and in Baseball-Reference's wins above replacement, with 7.1 — the first rookie to lead his league by that reckoning since Mike Trout in 2012 — while batting .278 with 18 home runs, 72 runs driven in and 12 stolen bases, and splitting his leather between shortstop and third base to the tune of 10 runs saved. The discipline is the signature: 95 walks against 100 strikeouts, a .376 on-base mark that stood sixth in the league, 97 runs scored, and a place in the top five in both walks and hits that no rookie had claimed since Ted Williams in 1939; since the award was struck in 1947, only Aaron Judge, Joe Morgan and Jim Gilliam had paired 95 walks with 95 runs as first-year men.24 He is the sixth Tiger to win it and the first Detroit position player since Lou Whitaker in 1978. "I don't really know how to put it into words," he said. "I'm just super grateful for how this season went. I worked my butt off to play and help this team win."2

Stewart's ledger reads like a dock manifest. He played all 162 games — "Being available for your teammates every single day — that's something that kind of goes unnoticed at times," he said, naming it his proudest line — stood at three infield stations, and struck 32 home runs, the first National League rookie past 30 since Pete Alonso in 2019 and only the second Reds rookie ever to do it, after Frank Robinson, himself the Rookie of the Year of 1956. His 112 runs driven in, second in the league to Alec Burleson's 116, made him the first Reds rookie and the fourteenth rookie anywhere to pair 30 home runs with 100 runs driven in, and with 15 stolen bases besides he became just the fifth rookie ever to pair 100 runs driven in with 15 steals, after Carlos Beltrán, Jose Canseco, Tony Lazzeri and Al Simmons.24 He is the ninth Cincinnati man to take the award and the second this century, after Jonathan India in 2021. Asked for the feeling, he chose one word: "Relieved. I told myself that this award wouldn't define me. That's how I was raised. I'm excited. I worked hard for this moment. A lot of people doubted me. I'm focused."2

And so the drought columns close unmoved. Messick's candidacy carried Cleveland's 36-year wait to the very threshold — this paper reckoned only yesterday what his victory would have ended — and the wait goes on: no Cleveland man has taken the rookie prize since Sandy Alomar in 1990, a vigil still second only to San Diego's. Boston's Payton Tolle finished third at 54 points on the strength of a dozen third-place votes. The two winners, both All-Stars this past summer, took the news in their hometowns at watch parties of roughly 70 friends and family apiece — which seems, for two men honored above all for showing up, exactly the right size of crowd.34

THE DUGOUT DESK: TONIGHT THE WRITERS JUDGE THE MEN AT THE WHEEL — MURPHY BIDS FOR A THIRD CROWN RUNNING, WHICH NONE HAS EVER WORN, AGAINST TWO APRIL INHERITORS, THE PALE HOSE'S RESURRECTIONIST AND THE PILOT OF THE EIGHT-AND-NINETY-WIN RAYS

The week's second sitting convenes this evening, when the writers read their Managers of the Year, and the six names on the bill share almost nothing but October: five were not finalists a year ago, and two were not even in their chairs on Opening Day. The ballots, as with the rookies', closed with the regular season, so no pennant and no champagne weighs in the scale.5

The American League offers three varieties of surprise. Kevin Cash's Rays were projected by the figuring house FanGraphs, on Opening Day itself, to finish 79-83 with about a one-in-four chance of October; they finished 98-64, took the East by four and a half games, and seeded first in their league — the longest-tenured manager in the game, twice honored already, making the familiar look miraculous on one of the game's thinnest payrolls. Chad Tracy was managing at Worcester on the 25th of April when Boston called; the club he inherited sat at 32-46 as late as the 25th of June, worst in the league by winning fraction, whereupon it ran off 32 wins in 37 games — a conflagration the town likened to Morgan's Magic of 1988, lit by another interim man — and went 55-29 down the stretch, the interim tag traded for a three-year contract in September. The father-and-son particular, should Tracy win, stands as yesterday's columns set it down. And Will Venable's White Sox, losers of a hundred games three seasons running and of a modern-record 121 as lately as 2024, won 84, took the league's sixth seed, swept Houston in the Wild Card round, and banked the franchise's first postseason series victory since the championship autumn of 2005.5

The National League's argument is between repetition and rescue. Pat Murphy, whose Brewers set a franchise record with 103 victories and led all the game for a second year running — this against projections of 81-odd wins, with 80 starts given to rookies, the most anywhere — bids to become the first man ever to take the award three years in succession; only four have ever won it twice running. Against him stand Don Mattingly, who inherited Philadelphia at 9-19 in late April and steered it to the fourth-best record in baseball thereafter and a fifth consecutive October; and Walt Weiss, whose first Atlanta season survived the springtime loss of four starting pitchers and a 162-game suspension besides, led the East from the 8th of April to the last day, and produced the franchise's seventh division flag in nine years — a campaign whose tone was set, the club's chroniclers insist, on the 7th of April, when the 62-year-old manager personally brought down Jorge Soler in a bench-clearing affray and his men answered with a 20-6 stretch. The Herald does not handicap the writers; it observes only that the evening must disappoint at least five clubhouses that have little else to regret.5

THE EXCHANGE DESK: THE WINTER'S SWEEPSTAKES OPENS ON THE MAN DETROIT COULD NOT KEEP — THE CORRIDORS AT LAS VEGAS MAKE THE FLUSHING NINE THE FAVORITES FOR SKUBAL, THE DODGERS WEIGH THEIR OWN SUMMER RENTAL, AND THE CHAMPIONS WAIT ON BAUERS' ANSWER

The General Managers Meetings opened in earnest at the Aria hotel Monday, the thirty front offices and the agents who farm them filing into the same corridors this paper mapped yesterday morning, and the talk has organized itself around a single left arm. Tarik Skubal — twice the American League's Cy Young man, dealt away at midseason by a sinking Detroit club in the very year its rookie infielder took the league's laurels, as the columns above attest4 — is expected to command the largest pitching contract in the history of the game, past the $325 million mark Yoshinobu Yamamoto presently holds. He turns 30 on the 20th of this month, and the question of how many years a prudent house gives a great arm with a surgical history is the question of the winter.6

The industry has already picked its favorite. "If the Mets want him, he's going to be a Met," one American League executive put it — the Flushing club having missed October two years running, with an owner of famous purse and a baseball-operations chief, David Stearns, whose whole career has preferred pitchers on short leashes; which appetite prevails will tell the tale. The Dodgers, who paid three prospects for Skubal's summer services, hear from rival quarters that they want hitting, not another arm — "The Dodgers need bats, not another arm," one executive said flatly, counting the grey in a lineup whose regulars run heavily past 30 — while the Blue Jays, who have lately bid on every great name and signed a $500 million extension besides, are nobody's afterthought, with the free outfielder Seiya Suzuki reckoned a natural fit for their order in any case. The Yankees, for once, are expected to sit the auction out: "I would be shocked if the Yankees made a real play for Skubal," a rival executive said, their wants running instead to second base, third base and a right-handed catcher, with the posted Japanese slugger Teruaki Sato — whose crossing the Far Pacific columns noted Sunday — a candidate for the Bronx corner.6

The champions, meanwhile, wait on a single signature. Milwaukee's winter hinges on whether Jake Bauers accepts his $23.15 million qualifying offer, an answer due with all the rest on the 17th, seven mornings hence; decline it and first base opens, accept it and the budget tightens. The harder arithmetic sits behind the plate: William Contreras, Most Valuable of the late World's Series, is now one season from the open market, and the front office that has made a practice of trading its stars a year early — the reader recalls Corbin Burnes — will at the least answer the telephone. All of it runs ahead of the same weather: the collective agreement has 21 mornings to live, and the trade expects a November of unusual commerce for exactly the reason Monday's columns gave — the last such winter signed away nearly a thousand millions before the freeze.6

THE ALMANAC DESK: THE CATCH IS GOING TO THE MAILS — MAYS IS NAMED THE FIFTIETH SUBJECT OF THE POST'S BLACK HERITAGE STAMPS, HIS BACK TO THE PLATE AND THE BALL DESCENDING, WITH SUMMITT OF THE LADY VOLS HONORED BESIDE HIM

The republic's mail will carry the game's finest outfielder. The United States Postal Service announced Monday that Willie Mays will be honored with a stamp in 2027, the fiftieth in its Black Heritage series, and the design chooses the one image that needs no caption: alongside the portrait runs the over-the-shoulder catch from the first game of the 1954 World's Series — back to the plate, glove out ahead of him in deepest center field, the New York Giants on their way to the title.7

The ledger beneath the picture is read from memory in most households: 22 seasons with the Giants of New York and San Francisco and the Mets at the close, two Most Valuable awards, a dozen Gold Gloves, and a manner of playing the game that made grown writers reach for their best adjectives for half a century. Mays died in 2024 at 93; the stamp arrives three years after the man departed, which is quick work as immortality goes.7

He travels in worthy company. The same announcement sets Pat Summitt upon a stamp of her own — the Tennessee basketball general who won 1,098 games and eight national championships for the Lady Vols before illness closed her bench in 2012, her portrait drawn after the cover of her book Reach for the Summit. The Herald's jurisdiction ends at the foul lines, but it knows a kindred record of showing up when it sees one, and it notes with satisfaction that a letter may soon travel under the two of them together. Some afternoons the mail will be worth watching for.7

A Tuesday with silver already on the table and more promised by nightfall. The rookies' prizes are bestowed, one to Detroit and one to Cincinnati, and the first item of the week's long bill is thereby discharged; the writers judge the managers this evening; Wednesday doubles the docket, the Cy Youngs and the writers' first reliever's prize read while the league stages its own awards theater at Las Vegas; and the Most Valuable verdicts close the books Thursday — the same day the courtroom at Brooklyn resumes, as Monday's columns noted. The forward ledger, ruled open before a word was set as the house requires, stands unmoved: nothing on the card before the spring exhibitions of Friday, the 19th of February, 101 mornings from this one, with Opening Night 134 mornings off on Wednesday, the 24th of March.1 The 17th keeps its double appointment seven mornings hence — qualifying answers and silver bats together — and the collective agreement has 21 mornings left to live, a figure the corridors of the Aria recite more fluently than any batting average. Until the morrow's dispatch, keep your scorecards near and your enthusiasms nearer.
