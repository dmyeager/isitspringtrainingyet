Volume I · No. 127 · Thursday, the Twelfth of November, Two Thousand and Twenty-Six

No contests this day; the hot stove burns bright.

132 days until Opening Night of the 2027 season — the first pitch of the new campaign, the clubs yet to be named (Wednesday, the 24th of March, 2027).

News Around the League

THE LAURELS DESK: SIXTY BALLOTS, SIXTY FIRSTS — MISIOROWSKI OF THE MILWAUKEES AND SCHLITTLER OF THE BRONX TAKE THE CY YOUNGS WITHOUT A SINGLE DISSENTING PEN, A DOUBLE UNANIMITY SEEN BUT TWICE BEFORE IN ALL THE AWARD'S HISTORY, AND THE WRITERS CROWN THEIR FIRST RELIEVERS BESIDES

The writers did not deliberate so much as transcribe. The Baseball Writers' Association of America read its Cy Young verdicts Wednesday night on the MLB Network — Roger Clemens, seven times the award's owner, opening both envelopes — and Jacob Misiorowski of the champion Brewers and Cam Schlittler of the Yankees were named on all 30 ballots apiece, first place on every one, 210 points a man. Two leagues unanimous in the same November is a thing the award has produced only twice before: Justin Verlander and Sandy Alcantara in 2022, and Denny McLain and Bob Gibson in the year of the pitcher itself, 1968. The reader of Monday's columns will recall that MLB.com's September poll of its own scribes called both races exactly so, and that this paper printed the result under its standing caution that a poll is a poll; the only electorate that counts has now ratified the straw vote to the last pen.243

The National League's trophy goes to the fastest arm the game has ever timed in a starting pitcher. Misiorowski, 24, led all the Majors in earned-run average at 1.80, in strikeouts with 252, in WHIP at 0.80 and in opponents' batting average at .157 — the lowest full-season figure on record, beneath even Pedro Martínez's .167 of 2000 — across 174 and two-thirds innings in 30 starts, and he did it throwing a four-seamer that averaged 100.6 mph and once reached 105.5, a record for a man in a rotation. Since 1950 only Sandy Koufax in 1965, J.R. Richard in 1979 and Mike Scott in 1986 had led the Majors in earned-run average, strikeouts and opponents' average together.2 He is the fourth Milwaukee man to take the award, after Rollie Fingers, Pete Vuckovich and Corbin Burnes, and the first to pair it with a World Series ring in the same autumn. The hero's own account of life since the parade runs to hunting with his father and a football game. "It's an honor to represent my city and my teammates," he said. "Just being in this situation is awesome."2

The American League's goes to the seventh-round pick who out-pitched every first-rounder alive. Schlittler, 25, taken in the seventh round out of Northeastern in 2022, led his league in earned-run average at 1.95 — the third-best by a Yankee qualifier in all the franchise's books, behind only Spud Chandler's 1.64 of 1943 and Ron Guidry's 1.74 of 1978 — and in opponents' on-base percentage at .244, this for a club that reached October with Aaron Judge long absent and three veteran starters short of 130 innings. The road ledger is the historical marvel: a 1.16 earned-run average away from home, the third-lowest of the live-ball era for a man with 100 road innings, behind only Gibson's 0.81 of 1968 and Greg Maddux's 1.12 of 1995.2 He is the seventh Yankee winner, after Turley, Ford, Lyle, Guidry, Clemens and Cole. "Surreal to be in this moment," he said, and then, with the champion Brewers evidently in mind: "The job isn't done yet."2

The margins behind the unanimity deserve their line in the ledger. In the National League, Cristopher Sánchez of the Phillies took second with 99 points and Chris Sale of the Braves third with 98 — a single point between them — while in the American, Dylan Cease of the Torontos stood second at 110, the rookie Parker Messick of Cleveland third at 84 and Drew Rasmussen of the Tampa men fourth at 72.3 Both winners claimed the prize in their second big-league season, a feat managed before only by Fernando Valenzuela (who did it as a rookie in 1981), by Gooden and Saberhagen sharing the stage in 1985, by Tim Lincecum in 2008, and by Paul Skenes only last year.2 The same sitting produced the writers' first Reliever of the Year honors, inaugurated this winter: Louis Varland of the Torontos in the American League, on a 1.27 earned-run average and 35 saves across 78 innings, and Mason Miller of the San Diego nine in the National — names the reader will meet again one item below, where the matter grows complicated.26

THE THEATER OF SILVER: THE LEAGUE STAGES ITS REVUE AT LAS VEGAS — ALVAREZ CARRIES THREE PRIZES FROM A SINGLE EVENING, THE AARON AWARDS GO TO THE TWO MEN FAVORED FOR TONIGHT'S VERDICTS, AND THE TWO HOUSES OF RELIEF AGREE ON ONE ARM AND SPLIT ON THE OTHER

While the writers read their ballots, the league threw its party. The MLB Awards played Wednesday night at the Chelsea Theater of the Cosmopolitan at Las Vegas, Mookie Betts and the comedian Roy Wood Jr. presiding for a second year — the hosts ran their jokes through their own version of the automated strike zone, to rule each one in or out of the joke zone — and the Hank Aaron Awards for the finest offensive performers went to Yordan Alvarez of the Houston nine and Pete Crow-Armstrong of the Cubs, with Ken Griffey Jr., the 2006 winner Ryan Howard and Mrs. Billye Aaron presenting. The pair arrive at tonight's Most Valuable announcements as the favorites, by the wire's reckoning, and the theater may thus have given the week's last secret a loud hint.5

Alvarez made the evening nearly his own. To the Aaron award he added the Edgar Martínez prize for the game's outstanding designated hitter — unseating Ohtani, the award's first new winner in six years — and the American League's Comeback Player honor besides, three trophies in one sitting for a season of .316, a .432 on-base mark, 42 home runs and 106 driven in, the league led in average, on-base, slugging and OPS alike. "Especially because I wear the No. 44," he said, naming the number he shares with Aaron. "It's very special for me to see my name on this award." Zack Wheeler of the Phillies took the National League's comeback honor.5

And the relief question this paper posed on Monday — whether the writers' new electors and the panel of old closers who award the Rivera and Hoffman medals could agree on the same arms — has its answer: in one league yes, in the other no. The National League spoke with one voice, the Trevor Hoffman Award going to Mason Miller of the San Diego nine, the same man the writers chose: a 1.67 earned-run average, the best among the league's qualified relievers, with 129 strikeouts in 70 innings — a 47.4 percent strikeout rate, the highest for any pitcher with 50 innings since Edwin Díaz in 2022 — on a fastball averaging 101.4 mph. He is the first Padre ever to win the medal struck in a Padre's name. The American League split its houses: the writers chose Varland, as the columns above record, while the panel gave the Mariano Rivera Award to Bryan Baker of the Tampa men, whose 41 saves tied Cleveland's Cade Smith for the most in the Majors and whose 1.44 earned-run average stood second among qualified relievers only to Varland himself — and who then went unscored upon through nine October appearances while his club took the pennant. The Herald, which keeps its subscriptions for exactly such disagreements, is satisfied on both counts.65

The night's remaining silver, briskly: Erik Neander of the Tampa men was voted Executive of the Year by his fellow executives, his second such honor, joining Matt Arnold of Milwaukee — whose club beat his in the World Series — as the award's only two-time winners. "Arnie, you were up here a year ago accepting this trophy and cashed it in for a championship," Neander said. "I can't believe I'm saying this — but I've never wanted to be you more." William Contreras, Most Valuable of the late Series, received the first Flow Mayor Award for style and self-expression, presented with a diamond-studded toothpick by his brother Willson; Colton Cowser's game-ending robbery of a home run was named the Premier Play of the Year; and the All-MLB First Team was read, its rotation — Misiorowski, Schlittler, Sale, Cease and Sánchez — amounting to the two Cy Young winners, the two runners-up and the National League's third man, with Miller and Varland its bullpen.5

THE DUGOUT DESK: HOUSTON CALLS ITS OLD CATCHER HOME — AUSMUS, SEVEN-AND-FIFTY, WHO CAUGHT MORE GAMES FOR THE CLUB THAN ANY MAN IN ITS BOOKS, LEAVES THE YANKEE COACHING ROOMS TO TAKE THE CHAIR LATELY ESPADA'S

The managerial carousel, which has turned all autumn, stopped Wednesday at a familiar face. The Astros named Brad Ausmus their manager, the club announced, with a formal introduction set for Friday at Daikin Park — and the appointment reads less like a hiring than a homecoming. Ausmus, 57, caught more games for Houston than any man in the franchise's history across two tours totaling 10 of his 18 playing seasons, won three Gold Gloves in the uniform, and caught the pitching staffs of the 1997 and 1998 division winners and the 2005 pennant club; his two-out, game-tying home run in the ninth inning of the 2005 Division Series' fourth game, ahead of Chris Burke's winner in the 18th, remains among the franchise's treasured strokes.7

The chair comes with a record already written. Ausmus managed five seasons before this engagement — the Tigers from 2014 through 2017, with a division title in his first year and 86 wins in his third, and the Angels of 2019 — to a combined 386-422, and spent the last three years as bench coach of the Yankees, whose own newly laureled ace the reader met two items above. He succeeds Joe Espada, whose contract was not renewed after three seasons and two division flags, and he arrives a fortnight behind the man who hired him: Randy Flores, whose removal from St. Louis to the head of Houston's baseball operations these columns chronicled last week, and whose Cardinals fought Ausmus's Astros through back-to-back league championship series as the two men played against each other in 2004 and 2005. The senior advisor Jeff Bagwell, Ausmus's teammate across both Houston tours, sat in on the hiring.7

"The Astros and the City of Houston have been a huge part of my life," the new manager said, "and I'm thrilled to create a new chapter as the team's manager." Flores pronounced himself "excited to work alongside Brad and happy to have him back in an Astros uniform." The work begins at once: a bench coach must be found, the club having parted with five members of the late staff, and a roster that was swept from October's opening round awaits instruction. The Herald observes that Houston has now restocked its front office and its dugout inside a single month, and that the winter's remaining question — the roster itself — is the one neither man can answer by introduction alone.7

THE INK DESK: THE POTOMAC NINE BUY FOUR YEARS OF THE FINEST GLOVE IN THEIR GARDEN — YOUNG, SEVEN-AND-TWENTY, SIGNS THROUGH 2030 WITH AN OPTION BESIDES, A WAGER ON LEATHER LAID DOWN WITH THE AGREEMENT'S EXPIRY IN PLAIN VIEW

The week's quietest signature may prove its most telling. The Nationals agreed to terms with their center fielder Jacob Young on a contract running four years through the 2030 season, with a club option for 2031, the president of baseball operations Paul Toboni announced Wednesday from the meetings at Las Vegas. Young, who turned 27 in late July, is a defense-first outfielder by anyone's ledger — third among the National League's center fielders this season in outs above average and in runs prevented, and a Gold Glove finalist in both 2024 and 2025 — whose bat has lately begun to answer: a .252 average with career highs of 10 home runs and 50 runs driven in, this from a man who had brought 5 home runs to the season in his first 1,006 big-league plate appearances.8

"Already one of the top defenders in Major League Baseball, we all saw the significant step forward Jacob took offensively in 2026," Toboni said, "and we anticipate continued development in the years to come." The timing is its own commentary: the ink dries 19 mornings before the collective agreement expires on the 1st of December, with the industry braced for the freeze these columns have charted all week — a club locking in its young glove while the locking-in can still be done. The Herald notes with approval that somebody in the capital is planning for 2031 while the rest of the sport cannot say with certainty what happens on the 2nd of December.8

THE EXCHANGE DESK: THE SUITORS' REGISTER LENGTHENS — THE TORONTOS BOOK THEIR OWN AUDIENCE WITH SKUBAL, THE YANKEES EYE BOHM FOR THE HOT CORNER, AND THE DESERT FRONT OFFICE OPENS THE MARTE QUESTION A SECOND WINTER RUNNING

The Skubal sweepstakes, which yesterday's columns left with the Mets booking passage to California, added a fourth confirmed suitor Wednesday: the Blue Jays will soon take their own meeting with the left-hander, joining the Mets, Dodgers and Padres in the audience chamber of the agent Scott Boras. The Toronto case writes itself in recent ink — a $500 million extension for Guerrero, $210 million for Dylan Cease — and the trade now talks of an annual wage in the neighborhood of $45 million, comfortably past the $43.3 million record Scherzer and Verlander share. The complication is arithmetic: the Torontos scored the fewest runs in all baseball this past season, and a purse emptied into one arm is a purse unavailable for bats, with the free outfielder Seiya Suzuki reckoned the market's neatest fit for their order. Pitching or hitting, but perhaps not both; it is the oldest sentence in the winter's grammar.9

In the Bronx, the shopping list runs to the infield. The Yankees have interest in Alec Bohm, seven years a Phillie and lately a free agent, as a right-handed partner for the left-swinging Ryan McMahon at third base — a station that gave New York a .629 OPS this season, 26th in the game. Bohm's own year ran crooked: a miserable first half dragged his line to .698, but he posted .794 after the All-Star interval, and his career-long habits — little given to the walk but little given to the strikeout either, his career strikeout rate of 17 percent sitting well under the league's 22, with an .853 OPS against left-handed pitching — describe exactly the platoon hand the Yankee ledger wants. The Post's reporting has him likely signing short, perhaps a single year, which suits a club that prefers its commitments at third base brief.10

And in Arizona, a name this paper listed Monday among those to be spoken of "chiefly by men who do not employ them" is now being spoken of by the men who do. The general manager Mike Hazen, three straight autumns short of October with the core that reached the 2023 World Series, allows that he must "be open to making adjustments to what the roster looks like" — and the roster's most valuable adjustable part remains Ketel Marte, owed a reasonable $87.5 million over five seasons but armed now with the ten-and-five rights that let him veto any destination, and trailing an August unexcused absence at Boston that obliges the Snakes to ask less than they once might have. The payroll, north of $200 million last season, is to come down; the middle relief has walked off in free agency; and Hazen says the Marte question must settle before the rest of the winter can be planned. The desert's offseason, in short, begins where last winter's ended — with the telephone ringing about the second baseman.11

A Thursday that all but closes the silver season. The Cy Youngs are read without a dissenting pen, the league's theater has struck its set at Las Vegas, and one envelope remains: the Most Valuable verdicts are announced this evening, closing the week's long bill exactly as Monday's columns scheduled it, with the Aaron-award pair of Alvarez and Crow-Armstrong installed as the favorites by the wire.5 The courtroom at Brooklyn resumes its proceedings this very day, as Monday's columns also noted. The forward ledger was ruled open before a word was set, per the house custom, and it reads as it has read all week: the next baseball on the card is the spring exhibition slate of Friday, the 19th of February — 99 mornings from this one — with Opening Night to follow 132 mornings off, on Wednesday, the 24th of March.1 The 17th keeps its double appointment five mornings hence, qualifying answers and silver bats together, and the collective agreement has 19 mornings left to live — a deadline the week's quietest signature already answers to, as the ink desk above attests. Until the morrow's dispatch, keep your scorecards near and your enthusiasms nearer.
