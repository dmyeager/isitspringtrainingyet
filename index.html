The Game of the Day

THREE OUTS FROM THE FIRST TITLE IN MILWAUKEE'S HISTORY, AND TAMPA BAY TEARS THE PAGE OUT — RAYS 4, BREWERS 3 IN THE TWELFTH, AND THE BROOM GOES BACK IN THE CLOSET

Pratt's Fourth Hit Is a Homer That Stands Half an Inning as the Championship Stroke; a Bounced Throw, Four Singles and the Season Is Saved; Ashby Runs His Spotless October to Eighteen Innings and Past Andrew Miller's Mark; Only the 1910 Cubs Ever Won in the Last At-Bat With a Sweep Overhead

Milwaukee stood three outs from the first championship in its history, and the man who had seemingly delivered it was a rookie of 22. Cooper Pratt — whose error-and-atonement these columns chronicled Sunday — led off the twelfth against Ian Seymour with his fourth hit of the night, a drive into the left-center seats that broke a tie standing since the fifth inning. His final line read 4-for-5 with a double besides, the finest evening of his young October life. Half an inning later the blow was a footnote.

The unmaking began, as so much of this Series has, with a throw. Joey Ortiz fielded Junior Caminero's leadoff grounder at third and bounced his long peg off Jake Bauers' glove, and the Rays' third baseman was aboard — limping, of which more below. Shane Drohan, a rookie left-hander summoned from the bullpen, never recorded an out: Liam Hicks ground a single through the left side, Caminero hobbling to third; Jonny DeLuca's single up the middle scored Cedric Mullins, running for the wounded man, to square it; Chandler Simpson's third hit of the night filled the bases; and Ben Williamson, sent up to pinch-hit, chopped a bounder into right field to score Nick Fortes with the run that ended it — 4-3, after four hours and twenty minutes, before 40,514 under the closed roof. "We pieced together a big inning," said the Tampa Bay manager Kevin Cash, "maybe the most important inning of our season."3

The eleven innings before had been the tight, low-scoring kind this Series keeps producing. Simpson doubled home DeLuca in the second; Pratt doubled home Sal Frelick in the fourth; Caminero crossed with the lead that same frame when David Hamilton missed a tag and threw wild; Hamilton atoned in the fifth with a fly over the right-center barrier, and there the score sat, 2-2, for seven innings of fine margins — Brice Turang turning two on a 101.8-mph line drive off Jonathan Aranda's bat, Simpson going up the left-field wall to rob Garrett Mitchell, Pratt ruled out on review stealing second in the ninth.3 And Aaron Ashby, arriving in the ninth of a tie game, retired all seven men he faced. His spotless October now runs to eighteen innings, past Andrew Miller's sixteen of 2016 for the longest by a relief man in any single postseason by the Elias Bureau's accounting — a march these columns have followed since he passed Gossage on Sunday.5

The record books required attention when it was over. Tampa Bay is the 44th club to fall three games behind in a best-of-seven and only the eleventh to dodge the sweep — the fifth to force a fifth game of a World's Series, after the 1910 Cubs, 1937 Giants, 1970 Reds and 2024 Yankees, and not one of those four ever forced a sixth. It was the seventeenth time a club facing elimination won a Series game in its last at-bat, the first since the 2011 Cardinals turned the trick on this very calendar date, and the 1910 Cubs remain the only other club to manage it with a sweep hanging overhead.4 Milwaukee's run of eight October victories in succession, whose record-tying length these columns noted only yesterday, ends at eight. "We've got one more day," said Seymour, who surrendered Pratt's homer and won the game anyway. The arithmetic still leans hard toward Wisconsin — three games to one, the best arm in baseball waiting tonight — but there has been no sweep of a World's Series since 2012, fourteen Octobers running, and this one has now joined the streak.4

News Around the League

THE ASSIGNMENT DESK: MILWAUKEE SENDS THE BEST ARM IN THE LEAGUE TO FINISH THE BUSINESS — MISIOROWSKI AND HIS 1.80 TONIGHT, WHILE TAMPA BAY, ITS STARTERS ALL SPENT, PREPARES A NIGHT OF COMMITTEE WORK

The fifth game belongs to Jacob Misiorowski, and the Cream City nine could ask for no better custodian. The right-hander led all baseball this season in earned-run average (1.80), strikeouts (252), WHIP (0.80) and opponents' batting average (.157), and his October has kept the pattern: three starts, a 2.70 average, and one run on four hits across five innings in the opening game of this Series. He takes the hill tonight on his full four days of rest.6

Tampa Bay, by contrast, opens the cupboard and finds it nearly bare. Five of its starters have pitched within the past four games; Drew Rasmussen is held unlikely to return on three days' rest; and Nick Martinez, who had been expected to start tonight, instead spent two scoreless innings keeping the twelfth-inning miracle possible.3 The likeliest course is a bullpen night — Cole Sulser, Steven Matz and Garrett Cleavinger, none of whom pitched Tuesday, are the freshest men in the house, while the closer Bryan Baker worked two innings and 21 pitches, his first such outing since 2024. The consolation of survival: should the Tampa men steal tonight's game, Rasmussen and Freddy Peralta stand rested and lined up for the sixth and seventh.6

THE INFIRMARY DESK: CAMINERO LIMPS THROUGH THE WINNING RALLY AND VOWS TO PLAY ON A BROKEN FOOT IF HE MUST — WHILE McCLANAHAN'S FOREARM CLOSES HIS OCTOBER FOR GOOD

Junior Caminero came up lame after reaching on Ortiz's twelfth-inning error, gimped from first to third on Hicks' single anyway, and yielded to a pinch-runner — and he does not propose to let any of it matter. "If my foot is broken," the 23-year-old third baseman said afterward, "I'll play tomorrow." He would not reveal the extent of the injury to his left foot. His manager settled the question from the other direction: "He'll play tomorrow on one leg. I'll make sure of it," said Kevin Cash, whose club is batting .195 with a single home run in this Series and can ill spare the man who hit 42 homers with 101 runs batted in while playing all 162 games this season — even an October version of him who has managed one homer and five runs batted in across 13 games.7

The same ward carries a harder verdict. Shane McClanahan, the Rays' oft-mended left-hander, has been ruled out for the remainder of the postseason with tightness in his forearm; the club expects him ready by spring. The spring is small comfort to a team whose season now lives one night at a time.6

THE INSTRUCTION DESK: THE FATSE ENGAGEMENT IS MADE OFFICIAL — PHILADELPHIA SEATS ITS NEW PROFESSOR OF HITTING, ADDS CRONIN TO THE FACULTY, AND KEEPS GONZALEZ ON STAFF

The hiring this paper reported yesterday on the word of a source is now official: the Phillies on Tuesday announced Peter Fatse as their hitting coach. "I am very excited to add Pete to our staff," said the manager Don Mattingly in the club's statement. "He has a track record of being a great communicator who prioritizes collaboration." The club also engaged Joe Cronin — six years in the Boston organization, lately a hitting-strategy man on its big-league staff — as an assistant hitting coach, and retained Edwar Gonzalez in the same capacity.8

The commission is as yesterday's columns described it: succeed Kevin Long, and raise an offense that slid from eighth in baseball in runs in 2025 (778) to nineteenth (713) this past season.8

THE STEWARDSHIP DESK: BALTIMORE'S NEW GENERAL MANAGER MEETS THE PRESS AND THE FRUSTRATION BOTH — BANNER, LATELY OF THE CUBS' FRONT OFFICE, PROMISES A WINNER AND PRACTICES THAT FEEL LIKE GAMES

The Orioles introduced Jared Banner as their general manager Tuesday, and the new man began by acknowledging the ledger. "To the fans, I want you to know: I know you're frustrated. It's been a rough couple of years," he told his introductory news conference. "We're frustrated too. But you have our commitment. We're going to build a winner here in Baltimore." Banner arrives from the Cubs, where he was an assistant general manager, and inherits a club that won the American League East in 2023, took a wild card in 2024, and has since posted two losing seasons running while its young cornerstones — Gunnar Henderson, Colton Cowser, Jackson Holliday — went backward.9

The division of labor is explicit. Mike Elias, elevated to the club presidency last year, keeps scouting and acquisition — professional, international, amateur, the contracts — while Banner takes everything internal: player development, performance, health, the coaching ranks. The new man's stated philosophy is a workman's one. "We have to make practice more like the game, so the game gets easier once you get in there," he said, promising to "challenge all of our processes" and to "get comfortable being uncomfortable."9

THE OLD GUARD DESK: POSADA, SEVENTEEN YEARS A YANKEE AND NEVER ANYTHING ELSE, SAYS HE NO LONGER FEELS WANTED IN THE BRONX — AND PRESCRIBES A STERNER VOICE FOR THE CLUBHOUSE HE LEFT

Jorge Posada says he stays away from the ballpark he helped fill because he no longer feels asked. "I mean, I could, but I don't think they want me there, to tell you the truth," the old catcher said in an interview on the New York Post's podcast The Show, published Tuesday. "I don't feel welcome when I go there, you know? ... I don't feel like they want me there, so I don't go." He traced part of the estrangement to his final season of 2011, when he was moved off the catching position and quarreled with the management over playing time: "It's tough to go back and feel that again."10

The grievance came wrapped in a prescription. Posada, a five-time champion, urged the Yankees to install a voice that holds men to account in June so the bill does not come due in October: "You didn't run to first base? Get him out of the game. ... It's not a game, it's really a job, and some guys are not doing their job." He was careful, even so, to endorse the stewardship of Brian Cashman and Aaron Boone as the right men for the leading of it.10

The Series stands three games to one, and the road ahead has been checked against the schedule ledger: the fifth game tonight at eight o'clock Eastern under the Tropicana roof, and then — should Tampa Bay keep the argument alive — a sixth in Milwaukee on Friday the 30th and a seventh on Saturday the 31st, All Hallows' Eve.2 The sum is simple. The Rays must now take three in a row from a club that needs one win in three tries, two of those tries at home with the best record in its league behind them; the one rope out of this hole, as this paper wrote yesterday, has been climbed to the top exactly once, by the 2004 Red Sox. But last night the 44th club to grab that rope gained a handhold, and tonight it sends a wounded third baseman and a patchwork pitching staff against the finest arm in the game, with nothing at stake except whether anyone in Wisconsin sleeps before Friday. Until the morrow's dispatch, keep your scorecards near and your enthusiasms nearer.
