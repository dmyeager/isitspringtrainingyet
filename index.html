News Around the League

THE TRIBUNAL DESK: THE MECHANICAL ARBITER COMES TO ITS FIRST WORLD'S SERIES — AND THE TWO CLUBS THAT QUESTION EVERYTHING ELSE IN BASEBALL WILL NOT QUESTION THE UMPIRE, SAVE FROM BEHIND THE PLATE, WHERE DÍAZ HAS LET NINE-AND-FIFTY BAD CALLS GO BY WITHOUT A WORD

The automated ball-strike challenge — the tap of the helmet that summons the machine to overrule the man — finished its first season this year, and Friday it plays its first World's Series. The delicious part is who brings it there. Milwaukee and Tampa Bay are the two most relentlessly analytical operations in the game, clubs that will interrogate any ledger for the thinnest edge, and both have arrived at the same conclusion about the new privilege: hoard it, and spend it through the catcher. Batting, the two clubs together have claimed just 4 of the 43 overturns October offered them; fielding, 21 of 40. Every one of the seven batting challenges between them belongs to Milwaukee — Tampa Bay's hitters have not tapped a helmet all fall.3

The abstention runs deeper than strategy; in two cases it approaches principle. Of the 139 batters this year who saw at least 25 wrongly called pitches with a challenge still in hand, 137 protested at least once. The two holdouts will both dress for the Series: Milwaukee's Andrew Vaughn, who let 36 such calls pass, and Tampa Bay's Yandy Díaz, who let a scarcely believable 59 go unprotested. Díaz explained himself to the Tampa Bay Times back in June: "When I go to home plate, I need a hit, not a challenge. Maybe in the World Series sometime, but now, no."3 It is the World Series now. His manager, Kevin Cash, calls the economy a habit rather than a commandment: "If we get the lead after the sixth or seventh inning, we are reminding guys to save them for the catcher... It's got to be kind of a run-scoring, game-changing at-bat."3

Milwaukee's reticence has an enforcement arm. The rookie outfielder Luis Lara, wrong on five of his seven tries, was relieved of the franchise entirely. "We took him offline," the manager Pat Murphy said late in the season. "He can't get any worse. We've got a reverse magnet so if his hand goes near his head, it goes away."3

Where the two clubs do spend, they collect. Tampa Bay and Milwaukee stand first and second in challenge value gained on the fielding side this postseason, and the catchers are the paymasters: William Contreras leads all of October with 12 overturns, while Liam Hicks owns three of the seven most consequential challenge moments of the month — among them the call in the sixth game of the American round that turned a would-be full count with two aboard into an inning-ending strikeout.3 So the question the Series will answer is not whether the machine matters but who dares consult it: if a championship game ends on a pitch nobody challenges, the sport will hear about it all winter.

THE LARCENY DESK: ONE-AND-TWENTY BASES ALREADY STOLEN THIS OCTOBER AND NOT A MAN THROWN OUT ON THE TAMPA SIDE — THE FASTEST CLUB IN BASEBALL MEETS THE SECOND-BOLTINGEST, AND ONLY ONE PENNANT WINNER OF THE WHOLE WILD CARD ERA EVER RAN MORE

If the arms decide nothing, the legs may decide everything. Milwaukee was the fastest club in baseball this season — first in average sprint speed and first in runs clocked at the elite 30-feet-per-second gait the measuring men call a bolt — and Tampa Bay, slower only on average, banked the second-most bolts in the game. The running followed the speed: 164 stolen bases for the Rays, tied for second in the majors, and 162 for the Brewers, fifth. Only one pennant winner of the entire Wild Card era, the 2023 Arizonans with 166, ever stole more in a regular season than either of these clubs, and this is just the fourth Series since 1969 to pair two of the year's five busiest thieving outfits. The last such meeting, Rays and Phillies in 2008, produced 14 steals in a five-game Series — the most of this century — and neither of these clubs has shown any inclination to let that record rest. Tampa Bay has stolen 11 bases in 11 tries across nine playoff games; Milwaukee is 10 for 12 in eight.4

The Tampa running trade is a one-man syndicate with branch offices. Chandler Simpson has never hit a home run in his major-league life and rarely accepts a walk, yet batted .306, led all of baseball with 42 infield hits, led the American League with 46 steals, and has added three more this October; behind him Taylor Walls and Cedric Mullins stole 21 apiece, Richie Palacios and Jonny DeLuca 17 each. Milwaukee counters with the committee: seven Brewers stole at least 11 bases, David Hamilton taking 30 in only 121 games — and 3 for 3 this October — while the rookie shortstop Cooper Pratt lifted 19 after a June debut and Jackson Chourio owns three straight seasons of 20 or more.4

Here is the asymmetry that may tilt the footrace: Milwaukee allowed the second-fewest steals in baseball this year, 71, while Tampa Bay surrendered the sixth-most, 131. The catchers' ledgers look lopsided — Contreras threw out 34 percent of basestealers to Hicks' 15.9 — though the measuring men rate their arms nearer even given what each was asked to stop; the true gap lives on the mound, where Milwaukee's pitchers rank among the game's best at holding runners and Tampa Bay's among its worst.4 The opening game states the whole thesis in miniature: Jacob Misiorowski, nearly impossible to run on, against Drew Rasmussen, on whom only three pitchers in baseball allowed more steals than his 23 — though Rasmussen has kept the question moot this October by the simple expedient of allowing almost nobody aboard: 13 hits and 3 walks in 21 innings, a 1.29 figure. Runners cannot steal what they cannot reach.4

THE REMEMBRANCE DESK: 'WHEREVER WE GATHER, WE KNOW HE'S RIGHT HERE CHEERING ALONGSIDE US' — ON THE EVE OF MILWAUKEE'S FIRST WORLD'S SERIES SINCE 1982, THE UECKER FAMILY WRITES A LETTER TO THE CITY

The most affecting dispatch of the idle day carried no statistics at all. On the eve of Milwaukee's first World's Series in 44 years, the family of Bob Uecker — the voice bound up with the Brewers beyond any untangling — addressed an open letter to the city, and it reads less like a press notice than like something passed across a kitchen table. "Some moments are worth waiting for," it begins, and arrives soon enough at the ache inside the celebration: "We are filled with elation and pride, but also longing. A wish that Bob was here to see it, too."5

The family writes that Milwaukee, the Brewers, and the fans "meant everything to Bob," and offers this certainty in place of his voice: "Wherever we gather — in a packed stadium, a neighborhood bar, or a living room with the radio turned all the way up — we know he's right here cheering alongside us." To the players: "Bob would have been so incredibly proud of what you've accomplished." There are thanks besides for the Attanasio family and Pat Murphy, for the broadcast booth that carries his tradition forward, and for the fans who have kept his spirit alive "through a laugh, a story, a cold beer, or a favorite call." The letter closes at full volume: "Milwaukee, let's get loud, let's do this together, let's win OUR way!"5

The Herald adds only this: a ballclub is finally a collection of the people who cared about it longest, and no franchise ever had a truer voice than the one Milwaukee will be listening for between the cheers on Friday night.

THE LONG ROAD DESK: THE SCOUT WENT TO TIJUANA FOR AROZARENA, WHO NEVER APPEARED — ELEVEN YEARS, EIGHT FARM CLUBS AND ONE ABANDONED CATCHING EXPERIMENT LATER, THE SHORTSTOP HE FOUND INSTEAD BATS IN THE MIDDLE OF A PENNANT WINNER

Every World's Series roster hides one story that explains the whole franchise, and Tampa Bay's is Jonathan Aranda's. In 2015 the club's international scouting director, Carlos Rodriguez, traveled to Tijuana to evaluate a Cuban outfielder named Randy Arozarena — whom the Rays could not then have afforded to sign in any case — and Arozarena never showed up for the workout. Rodriguez, holding to a scout's creed that "you're always dangerous at the ballpark," asked to see the academy's younger players so the trip would not be wasted, and came away intrigued by a 17-year-old shortstop of 160 pounds. The Rays signed him for $130,000 on July 2, 2015.6

What followed is the less accidental half of the story: eleven years of stubborn institutional faith. Aranda played for eight clubs in the Tampa Bay system, some at levels that no longer exist, and was bounced around the infield for a decade — there were men in the organization who doubted he would ever reach Double-A, and after the lost season of 2020 the club floated the idea of making him a catcher. He answered with his bat instead, hitting .330 with a .418 on-base figure and .543 slugging at Double-A Montgomery in 2021 without ever starting a game behind the plate. He battered Triple-A pitching at a .315 clip across three seasons while batting just .222 in scattered major-league chances, and the manager Kevin Cash kept repeating the creed of the patient: too good at Durham not to hit in the majors eventually.6

Eventually arrived this year. Healthy at last, Aranda started 148 games — only Junior Caminero and Yandy Díaz answered the bell more often — and batted .274 with a .792 on-base-plus-slugging, his left-handed swing wedged between Díaz and Caminero precisely so that no manager can cheaply spend a right-handed reliever on the pair of them. He is now the longest-tenured player in the organization. "Player development success story and scouting success story — both of those," says Jeff McLerran, the club's senior director of baseball development, recalling the scouts' promise down all those years: "This guy will hit. I promise you, this guy will hit."6 He did. Friday the promise reports to the World's Series.

The ledgers stand exactly as the pennant rounds left them — Milwaukee four games to one over Los Angeles, Tampa Bay four to two over Cleveland, and the Series line a pair of unblemished zeros waiting for Friday.1 The particulars hold as this paper has printed them all week: the first two games at Milwaukee, Friday the 23rd and Saturday the 24th at eight o'clock Eastern, the argument removing to St. Petersburg on Monday the 26th.2 Milwaukee hands the opener to Jacob Misiorowski, whose 1.80 earned-run average, 252 strikeouts and 0.80 walks-and-hits figure led the league this season, and Tampa Bay is expected to answer with Drew Rasmussen, unbeaten in 15 starts since the All-Star interval.8 The rosters come due Friday morning ahead of the first game, and with them the last suspense the off-days can offer.7 Until the morrow's dispatch, keep your scorecards near and your enthusiasms nearer.
