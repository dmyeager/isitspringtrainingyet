No contests this day; the hot stove burns bright.

141 days until Opening Night of the 2027 season — the first pitch of the new campaign, the clubs yet to be named (Wednesday, the 24th of March, 2027).

News Around the League

THE LAURELS DESK: THE WRITERS POST THEIR SHORT LISTS, THREE MEN TO A RACE IN TEN RACES — THE CREAM CITY NINE LAND FOUR FINALISTS, OHTANI STANDS FOR A FOURTH CROWN IN SUCCESSION, AND THE FIREMEN GET A PRIZE OF THEIR OWN AT LAST

The Baseball Writers' Association posted its finalists Monday evening — three names to a race, ten races across the two leagues, read out live on the MLB Network — and the verdicts follow in the week of November 9: the rookies first on the 9th, the managers on the 10th, the pitchers and the relief men on the 11th, and the Most Valuable Players on the 12th.2 The relief honor is brand new this year, the writers having at last hung a prize for the firemen, and the Herald's standing rule bears repeating before the speculating begins: these are short lists, not verdicts, and no trophy changes hands until the ballot says so. The widest grins this morning belong to Milwaukee, which placed a man in four of the five races — Jacob Misiorowski for the Cy Young, Pat Murphy for Manager of the Year, Trevor Megill for the relief prize, and Brice Turang, who adds a Most Valuable Player candidacy to the Gold Glove this paper recorded only yesterday.2

The valuable-player races first. In the junior circuit stand Yordan Alvarez of Houston, who led every qualified hitter in the game at .316, .432 and .601 with 42 home runs and 106 runs driven in, and chased the first Triple Crown since Miguel Cabrera in 2012 before falling just short; Junior Caminero of Tampa Bay, 23 years old and present for all 162 games with 42 homers of his own; and Bobby Witt Jr. of Kansas City, a finalist on the strength of his glove and a fourth consecutive year among the balloting's top seven. In the elder league the favorite is Pete Crow-Armstrong of the Cubs, the seventh man ever to post a 40-40 season — 45 home runs, 41 stolen bases — atop the best center-field defense in the game by every modern measure. Against him: Shohei Ohtani, who has taken the award three years running and four times in all — only Barry Bonds, with seven, holds more — a finalist again despite injuries of knee and biceps that halved his pitching year, though the half he gave read 1.79 by earned-run average; and Turang, a first-time finalist at .268 with 21 home runs and 100 runs driven in.2

The pitching short lists hold their own drama. Cam Schlittler of the Yankees led his league at 1.95 and drew comparisons in the Bronx to Guidry's 1978; Dylan Cease posted a 2.40 figure in his first Toronto season; and Parker Messick of Cleveland, a rookie of 186 1/3 innings — the heaviest rookie workload since 2019 — stands a finalist for the Cy Young and the Rookie of the Year alike. The National League's race pits Misiorowski, whose 252 strikeouts led all baseball and whose 1.80 earned-run average is the lowest in his franchise's books, with opponents batting a record-low .157 against him, against the ageless Chris Sale, a finalist for the ninth time, and Philadelphia's Cristopher Sánchez, a finalist for the second straight year. Among the managers, Kevin Cash, Chad Tracy and Will Venable will contest the junior circuit's honor, while Murphy — who would become the first man since the award's creation in 1983 to win it three years running — faces Don Mattingly and Walt Weiss. The rookie lists: Kevin McGonigle, Messick and Payton Tolle in the one league; Carson Benge, Sal Stewart — whose 112 runs driven in ranked third in all the game — and JJ Wetherholt in the other. And the inaugural relief ballots: Bryan Baker and Cade Smith, who tied atop the majors at 41 saves apiece, with Louis Varland in the junior circuit; Jhoan Duran, Megill and Mason Miller in the senior.2

THE BAZAAR DESK: EIGHT MEN ARE TENDERED THE QUALIFYING WAGE OF THREE-AND-TWENTY MILLIONS — MOREJON AMONG THEM AS PROMISED, SAN DIEGO BREAKING THE CLOSERS' MONOPOLY, AND EVERY ANSWER DUE BY THE SEVENTEENTH

The qualifying wage this winter is fixed at $23.15 million for a single season's service, and before Monday's deadline eight men were tendered it: Randy Arozarena of Seattle, owner of six consecutive 20-20 seasons and a career-best batting year in his walk season; Brandon Lowe, whose 35 home runs and 96 runs driven in repaid Pittsburgh's trade for him in full; Seiya Suzuki of the Cubs; the left-hander Trevor Rogers of Baltimore; Jake Bauers of Milwaukee; Adrian Morejon of San Diego; the catcher Ryan Jeffers of Minnesota; and David Bednar, who saved 35 games in 39 chances for the Yankees.3 Each has until 4 o'clock Eastern on the 17th of November to accept the year at that wage or decline it and carry a draft-pick tariff into the open market. The historical odds favor declining: of all the men so tendered since the system began in 2012, just 18 have accepted.3

Two entries deserve the longer look. San Diego's answer on Morejon, which this paper left pending at press time yesterday, came in the affirmative — and broke a precedent in the doing, for every reliever tendered the offer before him was a closer, and Morejon is a setup man by trade, albeit one with a 2.58 earned-run average across the past three seasons entire.3 And in Milwaukee the offer went to Bauers, the Championship Series' most valuable man, whose .266, .389 and .484 with 26 home runs amounted to a career year earned on a wage of $2.7 million — the offer would multiply his pay better than eightfold. Should he decline and sign elsewhere for $50 million or more, the champions collect a compensation pick near the first round's end, the same arrangement that turned Willy Adames's departure into the 32nd pick of last summer's draft.5

THE PANTHEON DESK: EIGHT NAMES GO BEFORE THE SIXTEEN FOR COOPERSTOWN'S WINTER BALLOT — BOCHY OF THE FOUR TITLES, BAKER OF THE TWO-THOUSAND WINS, PINIELLA TWICE DENIED BY A SINGLE VOTE, AND THE LATE TED TURNER BESIDES

The Hall of Fame on Monday unveiled its Contemporary Baseball Era ballot for managers, executives and umpires: eight candidates, to be weighed by a 16-member panel of Hall of Famers, executives, writers and historians, with 75 percent of the vote — twelve hands — required for election. The results come down Sunday, the 6th of December, and any man elected joins the Class of 2027.4 Four skippers lead the bill. Bruce Bochy, with four World Series titles and 2,252 wins, sixth on the all-time ledger, is newly eligible after retiring a second time a year ago. Dusty Baker, eighth all-time at 2,183 wins, remains the only manager ever to lead five different clubs into the postseason. Joe Maddon is the man who ended the Cubs' 108-year wait in 2016. And Lou Piniella returns to a ballot that has twice — in 2018 and 2023 — left him exactly one vote shy of the doors.4

The office men round out the eight. Sandy Alderson built the Oakland club that won three straight pennants from 1988 through 1990; Brian Sabean's long San Francisco stewardship produced three championships in five years. Ted Turner, the broadcasting titan who owned the Atlanta club from 1976 to 1996 and bankrolled its transformation from cellar fixture to the team of the nineties, stands for election posthumously, having died this past May. And Bill White — eight times an All-Star at first base, then the National League's president from 1989 to 1994 and the first Black man to head any major league in American sport — completes the slate.4 The Herald, which holds no vote and wants none, will observe only that a ballot wanting twelve hands from sixteen has broken harder hearts than Piniella's twice already, and will report the arithmetic faithfully come December.

THE OPTIONS LEDGER, FOURTH DAY: MILWAUKEE KEEPS THE CATCHER WHO CAUGHT A THOUSAND INNINGS THRICE RUNNING, DETROIT TURNS ITS OLD CLOSER LOOSE STILL SHORT OF FIVE HUNDRED SAVES, AND BALTIMORE PARTS WITH MOUNTCASTLE

The ledger's fourth day began with the least surprising entry of the winter: Milwaukee exercised its $15.5 million option on William Contreras, the World Series' most valuable man, who hit .270 with 18 home runs and 94 runs driven in while becoming the first catcher in the franchise's annals to log a thousand innings behind the plate in three consecutive seasons.5 The intrigue is in what follows, for Contreras now enters his final season before free agency, and the club's custom with men so situated has lately been the trade — Burnes dealt before 2024, Williams before 2025, Peralta before 2026 — though the Adames precedent, held to the last day and converted into a draft pick, shows the other road. The question will shadow the winter meetings.5

In Detroit the arithmetic ran the other way. The Tigers declined their $12 million option on Kenley Jansen, paying the $2 million buyout and sending the 39-year-old to market still short of the 500-save mark his career pursues; the season's surface figures — 22 saves, a 3.20 earned-run average — concealed six blown saves and 23 walks in 45 innings, and the club now enters the winter without an established closer.6 They kept the right-hander Drew Anderson at $10 million, his 3.67 figure across 108 innings having earned him rotation work by season's end, and re-signed the reliever Jacob Waguespack.6 In Baltimore, meanwhile, two long-tenured men reached the open market at once: Ryan Mountcastle's $7.5 million option was declined after an injury-wrecked year of just eight games — a foot fracture, then an oblique strain — his position long since crowded out by the five-year, $155 million signing of Pete Alonso last December; and Heston Kjerstad, the second overall pick of the 2020 draft, cleared waivers, was outrighted, and becomes a minor-league free agent at 27, his great promise still unredeemed. The outfielder Leody Taveras elected free agency besides.7

THE RUMOR DESK: THE NORTH SIDERS ARE SAID TO WANT THE FAIR'S FIRST LOT 'BADLY' — SIX OF CHICAGO'S STARTING ARMS WALK FREE AT ONCE, AND SKUBAL WOULD RESTOCK THE SHELF AT A STROKE

The winter market being one day old, the first great courtship is already whispered. ESPN's Jeff Passan reports that the Cubs "badly want" Tarik Skubal — the man this paper ranked the undisputed first lot of the fair only yesterday — and the arithmetic explains the ardor: six of Chicago's starting pitchers at once — Shota Imanaga, Matthew Boyd, Kevin Gausman, Clay Holmes, David Peterson and Colin Rea — are free agents, leaving the rotation shelf nearly bare behind the league's best offense. ESPN's Jesse Rogers adds that the club will "entertain almost anyone" where starting pitching is concerned, though the two-time Cy Young winner plainly heads the list.8

The price of such ardor is the rub. The trade's reckoning, carried in these columns since the market's eve, holds Skubal a near-lock to pass Yoshinobu Yamamoto's 12-year, $325 million standard for a pitching contract, and MLB.com's Mark Feinsand counts six clubs in the running with more expected to join.8 A club whose Most Valuable Player favorite patrols center field and whose winter purse has historically opened widest on the North Side's great occasions would seem a suitor of the first order; whether it opens $325 million wide, the Herald will learn alongside the reader.

This morning's consultation of the schedule ledger — performed daily, whatever the season — turned up a development of the first order: the page is white no longer. The first ink of 2027 has appeared upon it — sixteen spring exhibitions dated Friday, the 19th of February, the Rockies calling on Arizona and the Angels at Milwaukee among them — 108 mornings from this one, with Opening Night itself still 141 mornings off, on Wednesday, the 24th of March.1 Exhibitions turn no volume and settle no standings, but a dated page is a promise, and the Herald records it as such. Nearer at hand: the writers begin reading their verdicts Monday next, the rookies first,2 and the qualifying-offer men have a fortnight to answer their tenders. The winter desk, in short, has a fuller docket than many a July morning. Until the morrow's dispatch, keep your scorecards near and your enthusiasms nearer.
