Volume I · No. 104 · Tuesday, the Twentieth of October, Two Thousand and Twenty-Six

The Game of the Day

EIGHTEEN YEARS TO THE VERY NIGHT AFTER IWAMURA LEAPT, THE DOME ROARS AGAIN — CAMINERO RINGS THE FOUL POLE AT A WALKING PACE, DÍAZ PAYS HIS GUARANTEE IN FULL, AND THE RAYS TAKE THE AMERICAN PENNANT FOUR GAMES TO TWO: RAYS 7, GUARDIANS 3

Freddy Peralta carries one-run ball into the sixth and is proclaimed the most valuable man of the round; Richie Palacios, helpless against left-handers all season, wrings a two-run double from one anyway and howls at his own dugout; Junior Caminero's first postseason home run clangs off the left-field pole and is toured in 38.66 seconds, the second-slowest lap the measuring instruments have ever recorded — behind his own; Yandy Díaz closes the account at 111.9 miles the hour one night after declaring it closed; and the first pennant ever clinched on Tropicana Field's own carpet sends Tampa Bay to a World's Series that must crown a first-time champion

Eighteen years ago to the very night — the nineteenth of October, 2008 — Akinori Iwamura gathered a last grounder and leapt off second base beneath Tropicana Field's roof, and 40,473 watched the Rays claim their first pennant. On Monday, the nineteenth of October, 2026, before a sellout of 40,230 under the same roof, Bryan Baker spun a two-strike changeup, Chase DeLauter lifted it foul by the visitors' dugout, Liam Hicks squeezed it, and Baker flung his glove toward the catwalks: Rays 7, Guardians 3, and the American pennant settled four games to two.7 The evening was thick with its own history — B.J. Upton of the worst-to-first 2008 nine threw the ceremonial first pitch, and Tyler Glasnow and Randy Arozarena of the 2020 champions sat in the stands as paying witnesses — and it minted some besides: the 2020 flag having been clinched on neutral ground at San Diego, this was the first pennant ever won upon the Trop's own carpet, a vacancy this paper noted only yesterday morning.73

The contest itself followed the shape the ledgers promised. Cleveland, its rotation spent, stitched the night from eight pitchers beginning with Shawn Armstrong, who lasted the single inning and took the defeat; Tampa Bay handed the ball to Freddy Peralta, who gave them 5 1/3 innings of one-run ball on 84 pitches, 33 of them at 97 miles the hour or better — a career high for the 30-year-old.4 The first blow fell in the second, and it belonged to the unlikeliest man in the order: Richie Palacios, whom left-handers held to a .158 average this season, fought off Tim Herrin pitch after pitch and then lined a double into the right-center gap, his helmet tumbling behind him as Chandler Simpson and Victor Mesa Jr. crossed, and howled at his own dugout from second base.3 Jonny DeLuca's sacrifice fly made it three in the third, and Nathaniel Lowe's single off Peralta in the sixth — Cleveland's lone answer for seven innings — only trimmed the margin. Peralta's account for the round: three appearances, 12 innings, one earned run, an 0.75 average, and the Most Valuable laurels, in company with Matt Garza of 2008 and Arozarena of 2020 among Rays so honored.4

Then came the seventh, and the moment the month had been withholding. Junior Caminero, 42 home runs in the regular season and none in 36 postseason trips to the plate, caught Cade Smith's offering and sent it soaring down the left field line until it clanged off the foul pole — a two-run blow, Hicks scoring ahead of him, and the beginning of the slowest victory lap of the autumn. He stopped at the first-base coach's box and beat his chest. He skipped from first to second. He cupped a hand to his ear from second to third. And at the plate he turned to Arozarena, seated just behind home, crossed his arms in the elder showman's own defiant pose, and stepped on the dish: 38.66 seconds in all, the second-slowest uninterrupted tour the measuring instruments have recorded since 2015 — second only to a 40.65-second lap of Caminero's own, taken in September. "He called it," said the 23-year-old, reporting that Arozarena had demanded the home run from the stands before the at-bat began.5 Even Kevin Cash, the most composed man in the building by profession, threw both arms in the air and screamed.5

Cleveland loaded the eighth and pressed two runs across — both forced home, Patrick Bailey walking with the bases full and Angel Martínez struck by a pitch — and for a moment the thing tightened to 5-3. The answer required one swing. Yandy Díaz, who had stood before the writers Sunday night and guaranteed this very result — a proclamation this paper carried in yesterday's number — met Hunter Gaddis's changeup and sent it 416 feet into the left-field seats at 111.9 miles the hour, the second-hardest-struck postseason home run by any Ray in the instruments' ledger, Taylor Walls scoring ahead of him. He flexed the famous right arm, formed a heart with his hands at the plate, and was dragged back out of the dugout by Caminero for a curtain call as the chant of his name came down from the seats. The man Cleveland itself signed off the amateur market in 2013, who hit one home run in 88 games for them before the trade that remade him, had closed his old club's season personally. "I'm really glad he backed it up, that's for sure," said Cash. Asked about Milwaukee, Díaz consulted the same bank book: "It's going to be a great series, but we're winning."6

For the beaten there was no alibi and none sought. "We didn't beat ourselves, we got beat," said Steven Kwan. "They played defense better than us, they hit better than us, they pitched better than us. As a competitor, you can live with that."3 The Cleveland drought, the game's longest sorrow, now stretches into its 78th year — no title since 1948, though four World's Series and seven American pennant rounds have come and gone since — and the young architecture of this club, DeLauter and Travis Bazzana at the bat and Gavin Williams, Parker Messick and Cade Smith on the hill, is the consolation the coming weeks will have to serve.3 The victors fly north. The 122nd World's Series — guaranteed a first-time champion, as neither finalist has ever won one — opens Friday night at Milwaukee, and the particulars are set out at the Series Desk below.

News Around the League

THE SERIES DESK: ONE HUNDRED THREE WINS AGAINST EIGHT-AND-NINETY, THE TOP SEED OF EACH LEAGUE AND NEITHER WITH A TITLE TO ITS NAME — THE 122ND WORLD'S SERIES OPENS FRIDAY AT MILWAUKEE, RASMUSSEN RECKONED LIKELY AGAINST MISIOROWSKI

The bracket's long arithmetic has produced the rarest of finals: the best record in each league, and not a championship between them. Milwaukee, 103 victories and a franchise record, hosts Tampa Bay, 98 victories and the American League's best, beginning Friday night at eight o'clock Eastern at American Family Field; the second game follows Saturday at the same hour, and the argument removes to the Gulf shore dome Monday the 26th and Tuesday the 27th.29 Five franchises have never won the World's Series — these two, the Mariners, the Padres and the Rockies — so whatever else October does, it will strike one name from that list by month's end.9

The pitching question has a probable answer and a flamethrowing certainty. Tampa Bay has announced nothing, but the wire reckons Drew Rasmussen — 11-0 with a 1.73 earned-run average in 15 starts since the All-Star interlude, and the first-game man in both previous rounds — likely for the opener, on his accustomed rest; he would face the very club that drafted him and dealt him to the Rays five years ago.9 Milwaukee's answer is Jacob Misiorowski, who led the whole of the major leagues this season in earned-run average at 1.80, in strikeouts with 252, in walks-and-hits per inning at 0.80 and in opponents' batting at .157, and whose first pitch of the division round traveled 104.8 miles the hour — the swiftest delivery of any postseason since the measuring instruments began their count in 2008. The rest ledger is lopsided in the extreme: the Brewers will have sat eight days since their sweep concluded on the 15th, and their ace will have gone eleven full days between hostile innings — idleness that has cut both ways in Octobers past, as this paper's Sunday number set out at length.93

THE HOMECOMING DESK: BOTH MOST-VALUABLE MEN OF THE PENNANT ROUNDS WILL SPEND THE SERIES STARING AT THE CLUB THAT FIRST DRESSED THEM — PERALTA THE EX-BREWER AND BAUERS THE EX-RAY, CLASSMATES OF 2018, EACH DECORATED BY THE OTHER'S ALMA MATER

The Series arrives wrapped in a coincidence no scenarist would dare submit. Freddy Peralta, most valuable man of the American round, broke into the majors in 2018 with Milwaukee and spent eight seasons there — a 3.59 earned-run average, 1,153 strikeouts, two All-Star selections, and the first-game assignment in the Octobers of 2024 and 2025 — before a January trade sent him to the Mets, where the season soured to a 4.99 figure across 22 starts. Dealt again to Tampa Bay at the August deadline, he posted a 3.25 mark over his final ten outings and has spent this month repaying the gamble with interest.84 Jake Bauers, most valuable man of the National round for his part in Milwaukee's sweep of the Dodgers, debuted that same season of 2018 — with the Rays. After years of wandering he settled in Milwaukee in 2024 and produced the season of his life this year, a career-high 26 home runs.8

So each league's October laureate will open the World's Series opposite the club that first dressed him, both members of the class of 2018, each honored in the other's old colors.8 Peralta, for his part, declines to make it a grudge: "Milwaukee is the team and the city that gave me the opportunity to make my dreams come true, and Milwaukee is going to be always in my heart," he said Monday night, trophy in hand. "But it's time now to compete."4

THE APPOINTMENTS DESK: BOONE KEEPS THE BRONX CHAIR FOR A TENTH SEASON, THE ATHLETICS HAND THEIR BASEBALL AFFAIRS TO TAYLOR SMITH, AND BALTIMORE FETCHES JARED BANNER FROM THE CUBS' FRONT OFFICE

The hiring season opened on three fronts Monday. In New York, Aaron Boone will return as manager of the Yankees in 2027 — the final year of his contract and his tenth season at the helm — by the account of a source, the club itself declining comment. The case for retention is in the ledger: 93 wins this season and the top Wild Card post before the Rays swept the division round away, three American East flags and eight postseasons in nine years, and 790 victories in the span — second in all baseball to the Dodgers' 849.1011

Out west, the Athletics engaged Taylor Smith as president of baseball operations, entrusting the new man with a franchise entering its final year of temporary quarters at West Sacramento before the move to Las Vegas in 2028.12 And in Baltimore, the Orioles named Jared Banner, lately of the Cubs' front office, their new general manager, with the portfolio of player development, coaching and performance.13 Three chairs filled in a day; the winter's real bargaining, over the men who play, awaits the Series' last out.

THE PACIFIC DESK: SATO, TRIPLE CROWN MAN OF THE CENTRAL LEAGUE, WILL BE POSTED FOR THE AMERICAN MARKET — HANSHIN CONSENTS TO PART WITH A BAT OF .306, NINE-AND-THIRTY HOME RUNS AND 106 RUNS DRIVEN HOME

The winter market gained its headline lot before the Series has thrown a pitch. The Hanshin Tigers of Japan's professional league announced they will approve the posting of Teruaki Sato, the left-handed-hitting third baseman who won the Central League's Triple Crown this season — a .306 average, 39 home runs and 106 runs batted in — one year after taking the league's Most Valuable award on 40 home runs and a .924 mark of on-base-plus-slugging.14 Once posted, all 30 clubs will have a 45-day window to treat with him; being past 25 years of age he comes unrestricted, unlike the young Sasaki two winters ago, and Hanshin collects a release fee scaled to whatever contract results.14

The scouting ledger reads thus: 27 years old, turning 28 before next season; durable, never fewer than 120 games in any of six seasons; a strikeout rate of 27 percent over the past two years against a 10.3 percent walk rate; a Golden Glove at third base in 2025, with 262 games of outfield work besides should a new employer prefer him elsewhere; a Japan Series championship with Hanshin in 2023 and a quarterfinal run with Samurai Japan in this spring's World Baseball Classic.14 In a free-agent pool reckoned light on bats, the wire already counts the Mets, Yankees, Dodgers and Phillies among the interested — a queue the Herald expects to lengthen the moment the posting is official.14

THE LEDGER DESK: THE QUALIFYING OFFER RISES TO $23.15 MILLION, UP FIVE PERCENT AND A FRACTION ON THE YEAR — THE STANDING PRICE OF A FREE MAN'S YEAR CLIMBS AGAIN, AND THE HERALD NOTES WHO SET IT CLIMBING

The accountants have fixed this winter's qualifying offer — the one-year tender a club must extend to its departing free agent if it wishes draft compensation for losing him — at $23.15 million, a rise of 5.1 percent over last winter's $22,025,000. The figure is struck each year from the mean of the game's 125 richest salaries, and its steady climb is the cleanest instrument in the public ledger for what the top of the labor market actually pays.15

The Herald, whose sympathies on matters of the purse are a matter of record, offers the number without alarm: a market in which the standing price of one year's work rises five percent is not a market in distress, whatever may be pleaded across the bargaining table in the winter ahead. The offers go out in the days after the Series concludes; the men who receive them will have their own arithmetic to do.

The forward ledger grants everyone a breath: three days without baseball, and then the 122nd World's Series — Tampa Bay at Milwaukee, Friday the 23rd and Saturday the 24th at eight o'clock Eastern, the scene shifting to the dome at St. Petersburg Monday the 26th and Tuesday the 27th, with a seventh game possible as late as the 31st should the argument use every page allotted it.2 A first-time champion is now a mathematical certainty, which the Herald counts among the happiest sentences it has printed all year. The paper will spend the interval as the clubs will — sharpening its pencils and studying the other fellow. Until the morrow's dispatch, keep your scorecards near and your enthusiasms nearer.
