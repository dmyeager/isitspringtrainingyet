Volume I · No. 134 · Thursday, the Nineteenth of November, Two Thousand and Twenty-Six

No contests this day; the hot stove burns bright.

92 days until the first spring-training game (Friday, the 19th of February, 2027).

News Around the League

THE WINTER'S FIRST BLOCKBUSTER: SIX MEN CHANGE FLAGS IN ONE GREAT WEDNESDAY SWAP — GARCIA OF THE GOLD GLOVE GOES TO THE PUGET SOUND MEN FOR MILLER AND TWO ARMS BESIDE, AND DIPOTO DECLARES HIS ROTATION CLOSED FOR BUSINESS

The trade market, which had spent the month clearing its throat, found its full voice Wednesday morning. Kansas City and Seattle struck the biggest blow of the winter's business so far: a six-player affair that sends the third baseman Maikel Garcia — Gold Glove winner and first-time All-Star in 2025, and signed through the decade's end — to the Puget Sound men along with the right-hander James McArthur and the pitching prospect L.P. Langevin, while the Missourians carry off the starters Bryce Miller and Logan Evans and the left-handed reliever José A. Ferrer. The seed was planted when the Kansas City steward J.J. Picollo allowed early this offseason that Garcia could be had if Seattle would open its rotation; the talks took root at the Las Vegas meetings and, in the words of the Seattle president Jerry Dipoto, "picked up steam very, very quickly."3

What Seattle has bought is order at a station that had none. Ten different men started at third base for the club last season, Brendan Donovan's 36 turns the most of them; Garcia, 26, arrives as the settled answer, and on terms a bookkeeper would frame — the five-year, $57.5 million extension he signed only last December pays him $7 million in 2027, rising by steps to $19 million in 2030, with a club option for 2031. The 2025 season states his case: .286/.351/.449 with a career-high 16 home runs and 5.6 wins by FanGraphs' reckoning, second among all third basemen to José Ramírez's 6.3, though strains of the hand and hamstring cut his 2026 to 82 games and a leaner .268/.334/.381. The glove needs no advocate — since taking the position full-time in 2023 he stands second among third basemen in Statcast's fielding runs and in outs above average alike. "He's got range like a shortstop, but playing at third base," said Dipoto, penciling him into an infield of Emerson, Young and Naylor, with Donovan freed for the outfield grass.3

The reader will recall from Monday's columns that the wire's thirty-club exercise nominated George Kirby as the Seattle man likeliest to move, Dipoto having allowed that his rotation ran eight deep. The ledger answered otherwise: Miller, who battled bone spurs in 2025 and ailments of the oblique and lat in 2026, and Evans, who missed the whole of the past season after an internal-brace operation, are the arms that go, and Kirby stays — the depth now reads six, Gilbert, Woo, Kirby, Hancock and the rookie Kade Anderson among them, and Dipoto pronounced the shop shut: "the likelihood of us trading a starter just went from very high to zero." Kansas City, for its part, buys low on Miller's pedigree and takes two more arms besides, having — by ESPN's account — floated Garcia to rival clubs before the strike was made. One almanac note hangs over all of it: Seattle's roster now stands at 38 with less than two weeks to run on the collective agreement, and every front office in the land is working against that same clock.34

THE EXCHANGE DESK, CONTINUED: THE FENWAY MEN FETCH A CATCHER FROM THE PENNANT-WINNERS, THE SERAPHS BUY JUNG FOR CASH, AND THE HOUSTON NINE WEIGH SELLING WALKER TO BUY AN ARM

The day's other business ran brisk. Boston bolstered the position behind the plate, fetching Nick Fortes from the Sunshine nine — a division rival fresh off the pennant — for the 17-year-old minor-league right-hander Angel Roa; Fortes, 30, a strong defender who played 109 games for Tampa Bay this past season with a .258 average and the second-lowest strikeout rate of any regular American League catcher, will carry the backup's mitt behind Adley Rutschman, and the veteran Connor Wong was designated for assignment to make the room.5 The Seraphs, meanwhile, took the infielder Jace Jung off Detroit's hands for cash considerations — a first-round selection of 2022, handed a fresh start out west.6

The speculative shelf restocked besides. The insider Jon Paul Morosi reports the Houston nine gauging the market for Christian Walker, the four-time Gold Glove first baseman entering his final season before free agency at a wage of $20 million — moving him would shift Isaac Paredes across the diamond, hand third base to Carlos Correa, and free the purse for a rotation that ranked 27th in the game with a 4.81 earned-run average; Morosi reckons the club "could afford a Rogers or a Clay Holmes" if the deal is done. ESPN's Jeff Passan adds the Frisco nine to the sellers' column, naming the breakout utility man Casey Schmitt — 21 home runs in 98 games before a torn meniscus ended his season — as a possible chip in a search for pitching the correspondent calls desperate, and The Athletic counts multiple clubs interested in the Phillies' reliever Orion Kerkering, high-octane stuff and a rough 4.97 season notwithstanding.4

THE LABOR QUESTION: THE COMMISSIONER DRAWS HIS LINE — 'CAN'T IMAGINE' THE OWNERS RATIFYING A DEAL WITHOUT A CAP AND FLOOR, THE UNION ANSWERS IN KIND, AND A FRANCHISE MEANWHILE CHANGES HANDS AT FOUR THOUSAND MILLIONS

With 12 mornings left on the collective agreement's life, the commissioner put away the diplomatic phrasing. "The owners want a deal with a salary cap and floor," Rob Manfred said following the owners' meetings, declaring the thirty clubs "100% unanimous in support of the proposals that have been made" and adding that he "can't imagine the owners ratifying a deal that does not include a cap and floor." The league's latest proposal sets the cap at $258.2 million and the floor at $180.2 million, to take effect in 2028; by this season's closing payrolls, twelve clubs would be obliged to raise their wages by a combined $636 million to reach the floor, and eight to shed a combined $528 million to duck under the cap. The commissioner's case is that the system serves the players — a guaranteed half-share of revenue, a dramatic raise to the minimum wage, free agency opened early for men of 30 with five years' service — and that the fans demand it: "We are not prepared to tell 70% of our fans that they're wrong on this issue."7

The union's answer came within the hour, and gave no ground. "There can be no clearer example of the league's lack of seriousness," said the interim executive director Bruce Meyer, "than their insistence that they will not negotiate over the economics outside of a radical change to the system" — one that would, by his accounting, suppress pay across every class of player and strip the guaranteed contract itself. "They want to make more money in profits and franchise values by artificially suppressing player salaries," Meyer said.7 On the question of franchise values, the morning's own wire filed a supporting document: the league expects the sale of the Angels to Kroenke Sports and Entertainment, at a valuation of $4 billion, to close in January.8 The Herald, whose sympathies on matters of the purse are a matter of long record, merely sets the two dispatches side by side and invites the reader to read them in either order.

As for the balance the cap is said to cure, the commissioner met the obvious exhibit head-on — the season just ended crowned the small-market Brewers over the small-market Rays — by observing that Milwaukee is the first small-market champion since the 2015 Royals, and that such a thing "should not be a once-in-a-decade phenomenon." He professed his overriding object to be an agreement and an uninterrupted season of 162 games, while noting that the last negotiation came "within inches of losing games" with no cap on the table at all. The clock, in any case, is indifferent to the rhetoric: the agreement dies at one minute before midnight on the first of December, and nearly everything else in this morning's paper — the trade market's hurry, the Rule 5 drafting's fine print, the timetable of the winter's grandest courtship — is being conducted in its shadow.7

THE INK DESK: SONNY COMES HOME TO THE TWIN CITIES — GRAY SIGNS FOR TWO YEARS AND FIVE-AND-FIFTY MILLIONS, THE RICHEST PITCHER'S WAGE IN THE FRANCHISE'S BOOKS, AND THE NORTH COUNTRY ROTATION NOW RUNS NINE DEEP

Minnesota made the largest free-agent investment it has made in years on Wednesday, agreeing with the right-hander Sonny Gray on a contract of two years through 2028 — expected to be worth $55 million by the account of the insider Jon Heyman, with ESPN's Jeff Passan reporting an opt-out after the first season. The figure, which the club has not confirmed, would make Gray the highest-paid pitcher in the franchise's history, and the guarantee the third-largest it has ever extended, behind only two separate engagements of Carlos Correa. Taken together with the catcher Jeffers' acceptance of the qualifying tender — yesterday's lead in these columns — the week reads as the chairman Tom Pohlad promised the winter would: the Twin Cities nine are spending again.910

The man returns to a familiar address. Gray, 37, pitched two seasons in Minnesota in 2022 and 2023, a 2.90 earned-run average across 56 starts and a Cy Young runner-up finish to Gerrit Cole in the latter year; he comes back off a season in Boston that read 18-5 with a 2.72 figure over 169 innings — the most wins of any pitcher in the game, the best winning percentage in the American League, and a fifth-place Cy Young finish — having declined his half of a $30 million mutual option and taken the Fenway men's $10 million buyout to go to market. Among active pitchers he ranks fourth in both starts and wins, and sixth in innings, earned-run average and strikeouts.9

The arithmetic of the thing all but guarantees further business. The rotation account now holds six established veterans — López, Ryan, Bradley, Ober, Kremer and Gray — with the young arms Prielipp, Matthews and Abel behind them, nine names for five posts; López, Ryan, Ober and Kremer can all reach free agency after 2027, the lineup still wants a bat, and the simplest way to buy one may be to sell a starter. The wire expects multiple moves before the camps open, and the Herald will keep the account current.9

THE SKUBAL REGISTER: FOUR COURTS HELD AND TWO IN SHADOW — THE DODGERS AND THE TORONTOS TAKE THEIR AUDIENCES, THE FRIARS FOLLOW, AND THE WIRE EXPECTS A SIGNATURE BEFORE THE AGREEMENT DIES

The register these columns have kept on the winter's prize arm gains three entries in two days. After the Mets' Monday audience in Orange County, ESPN's Jeff Passan reports that the Dodgers and the Torontos each held court with Tarik Skubal on Tuesday, and the Friars were scheduled for Wednesday, per the insider Jon Heyman — four known suitors of the six clubs booked, with two mystery establishments still unnamed. The timetable sharpens too: Heyman's expectation is that the two-time Cy Young laureate signs before the collective agreement expires at one minute to midnight on December 1, though his colleague Jon Paul Morosi counsels that no decision is imminent and the weekend may pass without one.114

The handicapping, meanwhile, has narrowed to two. Passan writes that the Mets left their meeting impressed, "reinforcing their status as favorites," while ESPN's Buster Olney reports the expectation among those inside the negotiations that the matter comes down to the Flushing establishment versus Toronto — a club Passan describes as "flush with cash and prepared to spend it," and for whom, this offseason, "nothing is off-limits." The Canadian shopping list runs well past the great left-hander: Brandon Lowe is reckoned a possibility at second base, with Chisholm on the radar should Lowe sign elsewhere, the incoming Japanese bat Teruaki Sato and the outfielder Suzuki are both linked, and Ketel Marte remains the trade-market alternative. The Dodgers, for their part, would prefer the shorter engagement the man himself does not want, and the Friars may elect to spread their money over several additions rather than one grand one. The register stays open, but the candle is burning at both ends.411

THE LAW COURTS: 'SEND SOME OF THAT' — THE PROSECUTION PRODUCES THE CLOSER'S OWN WORDS IN BROOKLYN, A BETTOR'S TEN THOUSAND ON A FIRST PITCH AND A REQUEST THAT A SHARE GO HOME

The federal trial of Emmanuel Clase and Luis Ortiz, whose testimony this paper has followed through the week — the cooperating bettors first, the house of FanDuel after — turned Tuesday to the defendant's own correspondence. Prosecutors introduced evidence that Clase, upon learning an associate had won nearly $10,000 wagering on the closer's first pitch the previous night, asked the man to "send some of that to the [Dominican Republic]."12 Both pitchers, as these columns have recorded, have pleaded not guilty to the conspiracy charges against them, and the Herald will keep its seat in the gallery as the government's case proceeds.

THE WINTER ISLANDS: THE DREAM PUTS ON THE UNIFORM — GUERRERO TAKES HIS SWINGS IN LICEY BLUE AT THE QUISQUEYA, WHERE HIS FATHER TOOK HIS LAST AT-BATS, AND WAITS ONLY ON TORONTO'S BLESSING

Yesterday's columns reported the curiosity — Vladimir Guerrero Jr. traded, within the winter league, to the Tigres del Licey — and left open whether the man would ever appear for them. The answer took one day. On Wednesday Guerrero climbed the dugout steps at the Estadio Quisqueya Juan Marichal in Licey gear, took batting practice in a shade of blue uncannily near his Toronto colors, and sat before the Dominican press beside the Tigres' general manager. "It means a lot to me. Since I was a kid, I always admired this team and always dreamed about being here," he said in Spanish. "And today, one of my dreams is coming true." One formality stands between the man and the batter's box: "We're working on getting the approval," he said of his employers in Toronto. "They're the ones who will ultimately say yes or no."13

The appeal of the work is not hard to read. Guerrero is coming off the season he would soonest bury — a .688 on-base-plus-slugging and nine home runs, a year after his October heroics — and live pitching with something at stake is the cheapest medicine on sale; the romance, meanwhile, writes itself, his father having taken the final at-bats of a Hall of Fame career in the same Tigres colors in the winter of 2012-13. The roster he would join runs from Jesús Made, the 19-year-old reckoned the finest prospect in the game, to the old Blue Jay Emilio Bonifácio, still going at 41; the club holds Juan Soto's island rights besides, and it was Soto, the record should show, who texted Guerrero first when the trade was made. The island season runs to December 22, and the Herald's ear stays to the water.13

Six men swapped in a morning, the richest pitcher's wage in Minnesota's books by evening, and the commissioner and the union exchanging broadsides over it all — the stove is no longer merely lit but roaring, and the whole blaze is set down above. The forward ledger was opened before a word was written, per the house custom, and tells its unchanged story: the camps' reporting dates remain unposted, so the count runs to the first rung bearing a date — the spring exhibition openers of Friday, the 19th of February, 92 mornings from this one, with Opening Night to follow on Wednesday, the 24th of March, 125 mornings off.2 The nearer columns crowd close: the collective agreement has 12 mornings left to live, Cooperstown's committee of sixteen reports in 17, the Winter Meetings convene at San Diego with the Rule 5 drafting on December 9, 20 mornings from this one, and the writers' envelope waits for the 19th of January, 61 mornings off. Until the morrow's dispatch, keep your scorecards near and your enthusiasms nearer.
