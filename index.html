News Around the League

THE COOPERSTOWN DESK: SEVEN-AND-TWENTY NAMES GO TO THE WRITERS — POSEY AND LESTER HEAD FOURTEEN NEWCOMERS ON THE BALLOT FOR 2027, UTLEY LEADS THE RETURNING CLASS FROM JUST SHY OF SIXTY PERCENT, AND THE ENVELOPE IS APPOINTED FOR THE NINETEENTH OF JANUARY

The writers have been handed their examination paper. The Baseball Writers' Association unveiled its Hall of Fame ballot for 2027 on Monday — 27 names in all, 14 of them standing before the electorate for the first time — and Cooperstown thus has both of its winter questions on the table at once, for the reader will recall from Sunday's columns that a committee of sixteen already weighs eight managers and executives toward a December 6 verdict. The writers' timetable is longer: ballots must be cast by December 31, three-quarters of them spell election, and the results are to be announced on January 19 at 6 o'clock in the evening, with induction at Cooperstown next July 25 — a date the almanac at Baseball-Reference already carries.231

The newcomers are headed by a catcher with little left to prove. Buster Posey of the Giants was Rookie of the Year in 2010, Most Valuable in 2012 — when his .336 average made him the first National League catcher to win a batting title in 70 years — and a champion three times over, in 2010, 2012 and 2014; he batted .302 across twelve seasons with a 129 OPS+ that ties Mickey Cochrane for fourth among catchers of at least a thousand games, and he walked away in 2021 fresh from a .304 season for a Bay nine that won 107 games, retiring at the very top of his powers. Beside him stands Jon Lester, who beat lymphoma in 2007 and threw a no-hitter the following May, and who closed his books at 200 wins, 2,488 strikeouts and three rings — in the Modern Era, only Spahn, Johnson, Glavine and Kershaw among left-handers have matched that ledger of wins, innings and strikeouts at his quality. The supporting bill runs from Brett Gardner, whose 274 stolen bases as a Yankee trail only Jeter and Henderson in that club's books, to Ryan Zimmerman, Mr. National himself and author of 11 walk-off home runs, to Kyle Seager, a Mariner his whole career and nicknamed, with fraternal cruelty, "Corey's Brother."23

Among the holdovers the arithmetic favors Chase Utley, the only returning man above fifty percent: his support leapt 19.3 points last cycle to 59.1, and another such leap elects him. Andy Pettitte stands at 48.5 in his ninth year of ten; Félix Hernández more than doubled his backing in year two, to 46.1; Alex Rodriguez holds at 40; and Omar Vizquel, at 18.4, faces his tenth and final ballot. The Herald will keep the ledger open through the winter.3

THE SKUBAL REGISTER: THE METROPOLITANS COME CALLING IN PERSON — THE FLUSHING NINE HOLD THEIR FIRST AUDIENCE WITH THE WINTER'S PRIZE ARM IN CALIFORNIA, THE WIRE ALREADY CALLS QUEENS HIS EXPECTED DESTINATION, AND THE ASKING TERMS MAY RUN TO TEN YEARS

The courtship of Tarik Skubal has moved from the telephone to the parlor. On Monday the Mets made their first pitch to the two-time Cy Young laureate in person, sitting down with him in Orange County, California — so reports the insider Jon Heyman in the New York Post, by way of the wire's running register on the man.4 The development surprises no one who has read these columns since Las Vegas: the widespread expectation coming out of the general managers' meetings, as the wire's Mark Feinsand reported it, is that Skubal ends up in Queens, one National League executive putting it baldly — "I don't know who else is really even in the conversation."4

The conversation nonetheless has other voices. The Angelenos, who paid three prospects to rent the left-hander at the summer's trade deadline, remain in it, with the Jays and the Mission nine besides — the reader will recall from Sunday's columns that San Diego has a sitting of its own booked. But the shape of the asking favors the deepest purse and the longest patience: Skubal, 29, seeks a long engagement, the figure mentioned running to somewhere between seven and ten years, and both records on the pitchers' ledger — Yamamoto's twelve-year, $325 million guarantee, and the $43.33 million annual wage shared by Scherzer and Verlander — are reckoned to be in danger. The Herald files the Monday meeting under opening formalities, and keeps its register open.4

THE LAW COURTS: THE HOUSE ITSELF TAKES THE STAND IN BROOKLYN — FANDUEL'S MAN TESTIFIES THAT ACCOUNTS IN CLASE'S ORBIT WAGERED ON ORTIZ'S PITCHES TOO, ONE OF THEM COLLECTING TWELVE THOUSAND ON A SINGLE BALL THROWN WIDE

The federal trial of Emmanuel Clase and Luis Ortiz, whose opening testimony this paper's earlier dispatches from the Brooklyn courtroom set down, produced on Monday a witness from the counting-house side of the business. Joshua Mehta, director of legal and regulatory affairs for the house of FanDuel, testified that accounts registered to men linked to Clase during the trial placed heavy wagers on first pitches in two Cleveland games of June 2025 — on the 15th against Seattle and on the 27th against the Cardinals — and that Ortiz was the starting pitcher in both. The bettors risked thousands on the result of a specific pitch without knowing the batter, nor whether the starter would even still be on the hill.5

The particulars have a bookmaker's precision. Against Seattle, the house took a handful of wagers that the first pitch of the bottom of the third inning would be a ball or a hit batsman; several came from an account registered to Enoc Pena — the close associate of Clase's whom earlier testimony placed at the center of the scheme — and when Ortiz threw the pitch outside the strike zone, Pena's wagers collected approximately $12,000. Some of the same accounts bet big on Cleveland's first pitch of the third inning against the Cardinals twelve days later; that one, too, arrived wide. Mehta said at least five of the accounts were suspended in June 2025, the use of inside information being a violation of the house's terms — and the calendar duly records that Ortiz was placed on leave by the league on July 3, with Clase following roughly four weeks after.5

Earlier Monday the cooperating witness Jenner Olivo testified that he won bets on selected Clase pitches across the 2023, 2024 and 2025 seasons on information received from Pena; the government alleges the bettors took at least $400,000 on the result and speed of at least 18 Clase pitches. Both pitchers have pleaded not guilty to conspiracy charges touching wire fraud, sports bribery, honest services and money laundering. And the day closed on a stranger footnote: a FanDuel account registered to Clase himself, opened in May 2023, whose only recorded bets were on basketball — which, the record should note, no rule of the game forbids.5

THE EXCHANGE DESK: BOHM SITS DOWN WITH BOTH NEW YORKS, THE BIRDS DRAW A SHOPPING LIST WITH AROZARENA'S NAME AT ITS HEAD, AND THE WALKER PURCHASE SETS THE WIRE TO ASKING WHETHER MEGILL'S MILWAUKEE DAYS ARE NUMBERED

The corner infielder Alec Bohm has taken meetings with both New York establishments. The Athletic's Ken Rosenthal and Will Sammon report that the Yankees and the Metropolitans each sat with the former Philadelphia man at last week's meetings — and his season explains the double appeal. Bohm's season read a modest .698 in sum, but it concealed an .821 from August 4 onward, after the Quakers moved him across the diamond to first base; the Bronx men could platoon him at third with the left-handed McMahon, while the Mets, whose first basemen produced a .667 mark this past season, third-worst in the majors, might simply hand him the new position outright.6

In Baltimore the shopping list has grown long enough to publish. The Baltimore Banner's Andy Kostka reports the Birds interested in Randy Arozarena, the right-handed outfield bat their lefty-heavy order wants — though the man carries a qualifying offer, and signing him would cost a draft pick's toll to Seattle. Luis Robert Jr., whose $20 million option the club declined after his three-game, hamstring-shortened Baltimore tenure, remains a candidate to return at a friendlier wage; the club is operating with the intent of adding a left-handed starter, with Kris Bubic and Matthew Boyd of interest and a reunion with Trevor Rogers open — the same Rogers expected to decline his tender by this afternoon's deadline in search of a longer engagement. Calls have come in besides on the third baseman Jordan Westburg, and the Marte dialogue these columns recorded Sunday grinds on with no deal near.6

And in Milwaukee, Sunday's reclamation purchase is already casting its shadow. The wire asks plainly whether the acquisition of Ryan Walker — a man with ninth-inning experience and three years of control — makes Trevor Megill, entering his final year before free agency with 81 saves across the last three seasons, the more expendable; the Milwaukees have sold exactly this way before, sending Devin Williams to the Yankees ahead of his own walk year. The reader will note the wire's survey named Megill the club's likeliest man to go only yesterday in these columns; the drumbeat quickens. The left-hander A.J. Minter, meanwhile, has become one of the market's most pursued relievers, the insider Jon Paul Morosi counting the Jays, the Capital nine, the Phillies, the Rangers and the Royals among his suitors after a 2.56 season split between Queens and Minnesota; the Mets, who employed him last, have checked in as well.6

THE INK DESK: THE SPIDER-MAN OF THE CAPITAL GETS HIS FIGURES SET DOWN — YOUNG'S FOUR-YEAR ENGAGEMENT IS FINALIZED AT THREE-AND-TWENTY MILLIONS, AND IN THE DESERT, LAWLAR'S MENDED ELBOW IS EXPECTED BACK FOR THE SEASON'S OPENING

The quiet signature these columns recorded from Las Vegas on the twelfth now has its figures attached. The Nationals have finalized the four-year extension of their center fielder Jacob Young: $23 million guaranteed beginning in 2027, covering three of his arbitration years, with a club option at $8 million for 2031. It is the second extension of the young Toboni administration, following the four years and $47 million settled on the right-hander Cade Cavalli in September — brick by brick, the Capital nine are buying out the questions around their young core of Abrams, Wood, Crews and Lile.7

What the club has secured is the best wall-climber in the business. Young, 27, led all outfielders with 20 outs above average in 2024 and has followed with 14 and 11 in the two seasons since; the clubhouse calls him "Spider-Man," and the nickname earned its keep on August 23, when he covered 93 feet, scaled the wall at Miami, robbed Heriberto Hernández of a home run and threw back to the infield to finish a double play. The bat, long the asterisk on the profile, improved this past season to a .252 average with 10 home runs — doubling his career total at a stroke — a career-best 50 driven in, and 18 steals in 22 tries. A seventh-round selection paid like what he has become: a cornerstone of the game's third-highest-scoring offense.7

From the infirmary ward, one note out of the desert: Jordan Lawlar of the Desert nine underwent internal-brace surgery on his right elbow earlier this offseason, and the club expects the young man ready at or about the start of the regular season.8

THE ALMANAC: THE DAY OF ANSWERS IS AT HAND — SEVEN MEN MUST SPEAK FOR THREE-AND-TWENTY MILLIONS APIECE BY FOUR O'CLOCK THIS AFTERNOON, AND THE SILVER BATS ARE HANDED ROUND THIS EVENING

The day these columns have spent a week pointing toward has arrived. By 4 o'clock this afternoon, Eastern time, every man tendered the qualifying offer must answer: take the certain $23.15 million for 2027, or decline it and carry a draft pick's toll to any new employer. Eight were tendered; Jake Bauers answered early and in Boston's ink, as Saturday's lead recorded; seven answers remain outstanding. The roll deserves printing whole: Arozarena of Seattle, whose 148 OPS+ was the finest full-season figure of his career; Brandon Lowe of Pittsburgh, he of the 35 home runs and the remade glove; Seiya Suzuki of the Cubs, well above the league with the bat across his five American seasons; Rogers of the Birds, whose year away from one rough stretch read a 2.76; Morejon of the Mission nine, the first setup man ever so tendered; the catcher Jeffers of the Twins, quietly sixth among the position's regulars in OPS+ these past four seasons; and Bednar of the Bronx men, 35 saves in 39 chances. History counsels patience with predictions — since the system was instituted in 2012, just 18 men have ever accepted.9

The same evening brings the silver bats, whose finalists yesterday's columns arranged: Trout in pursuit of a tenth, a shelf only Piazza at ten and Bonds at twelve have reached.10 Answers and ornaments together, then — and the Herald will have the whole accounting, tenders and trophies alike, polished and arranged in tomorrow's columns.

A Tuesday that will answer more questions by nightfall than most Novembers answer in a week: seven tenders due by four o'clock, silver bats by evening, and a fresh Cooperstown ballot already circulating among the electors, as the lead column attests. The forward ledger was consulted before the first word, as the house requires, and shows what it showed: the camps' reporting dates await posting, so the count runs to the first rung bearing a date — the spring exhibition openers of Friday, the 19th of February, 94 mornings from this one, with Opening Night to follow on Wednesday, the 24th of March, 127 mornings off.1 The nearer columns of the almanac fill regardless: the collective agreement has 14 mornings left to live, the committee of sixteen reports from Cooperstown in 19, and the writers' envelope is opened in 63, on the 19th of January. The game plays nothing; the docket, meanwhile, has rarely been fuller. Until the morrow's dispatch, keep your scorecards near and your enthusiasms nearer.
