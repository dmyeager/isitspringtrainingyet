THE MORNING HORSEHIDE HERALD

"Every Score Set Down, No Deed Unsung"

~ Being a Faithful Daily Chronicle of the National Pastime ~

Volume I · No. 131 · Monday, the Sixteenth of November, Two Thousand and Twenty-Six

No contests this day; the hot stove burns bright.

95 days until the first spring-training game (Friday, the 19th of February, 2027).

News Around the League

THE INK DESK: THE LINE FOR LEFT-HANDED RELIEF NEVER SHORTENS — SO THE BRONX DECLINES TO STAND IN IT, KEEPING HILL THE SUBMARINER FOR A YEAR AT TWO MILLIONS AND A QUARTER, THE FIRST BRICK LAID IN A BULLPEN ITS STEWARD CALLS THE WINTER'S MAIN PRIORITY

The Yankees' winter of relief work has begun at home. Tim Hill — the left-hander who delivers from down under, whose name stood in three clubs' notebooks when yesterday's columns surveyed the southpaw market, his admirers numbering the Dodgers, the Mets and his own employers — will stand in no line at all: man and club are agreed on a contract of one year and $2.25 million, with a club option at $2.5 million for 2028, the terms allowed by a source Sunday though the club had not yet confirmed them.2 It is the first move upon the bullpen whose rebuilding Brian Cashman declared, in the quote these columns carried Saturday, "It's our main priority, to be honest" — and the Herald observes that a paper which wrote only yesterday that the line for left-handed relief never shortens has been answered within the day: the simplest way to beat the queue is to own the merchandise already.

What the Pinstripes are keeping is the game's foremost dealer in groundballs. Hill, who turns 37 in January, pitched to a 4.60 earned-run average over 67 appearances and 58 and two-thirds innings this past season — an ordinary figure concealing an extraordinary trade, for his 68.9 percent ground-ball rate led every reliever in the Majors, he stranded 33 of the 35 runners he inherited, and the 5.7 percent of inherited men who came home against him was the stingiest figure in the game. Since New York claimed him in 2024, after a journeyman's road through Kansas City, San Diego and the South Side, he has given the club a 3.34 earned-run average across 172 appearances; the wage is modest, the angle is singular, and the fit is proven.2

The larger rebuilding remains. The Yankee bullpen's 3.13 earned-run average stood second-best in the Majors this past season, and much of it is now at liberty: David Bednar weighs the $23.15 million qualifying tender whose answer falls due tomorrow, with Paul Blackburn, Ryan Yarbrough, John Schreiber and Michael Fulmer free besides, and the club has rung the representatives of Adrian Morejon and of Jonathan Loáisiga — the same Loáisiga yesterday's columns reported nearing a verdict with five clubs bidding.2 The consolation of the market is its stock: the wire's survey of the winter's relievers calls the class at large a lean one but the relief shelf its deepest, with Morejon at the head of it — so the shoppers, in the Bronx and everywhere else, will find the store open all winter.3

THE CHAMPIONS' LEDGER: THE WORLD'S CHAMPIONS SEND TWO ARMS WEST FOR ONE THAT LOST ITS WAY — WALKER, ONCE AMONG THE LEAGUE'S BEST FIREMEN AND LATELY OF THE GIANTS' CELLAR, IS BOOKED INTO THE SAME ESTABLISHMENT THAT MADE MEGILL, WHILE THE WIRE NAMES MEGILL HIMSELF THE WINTER'S LIKELIEST MAN TO GO

Milwaukee has taken in another reclamation. By Sunday's business the champions acquired the right-hander Ryan Walker from the Giants for two minor-league arms — Blake Holub, 28, who pitched to a 2.10 earned-run average with five saves across 50 games at Nashville, and Gavin Perry, 23, an 11th-round selection of the summer's draft who has yet to throw a professional pitch — and the manager made no secret of the method. Pat Murphy likens his pitching laboratory to the "H&H Car Wash," the initials belonging to the coaches Chris Hook and Jim Henderson, who take in arms that have lost their shine and return them gleaming; the establishment's famous customer is Trevor Megill, fetched from obscurity in a minor trade of 2023 after indifferent service with the Cubs and the Twins, and lately the man who recorded the final six outs of the World's Series.4

Walker arrives wanting exactly that treatment. In 2024 he was among the best relief pitchers alive — a 1.91 earned-run average over 76 appearances, ten wins to lead all National League relievers, ten saves, a 0.85 walks-and-hits figure — and in 2026 he was among the most lost, a 7.16 earned-run average in 33 appearances before San Francisco sent him down in May to shuttle between the Bay and the minors. He turns 31 ten mornings hence, on November 26, carries three years of club control, and cost two arms the champions could spare: a wager on the car wash, at the car wash's usual price.4 The bullpen book he joins holds Megill in his final year of arbitration alongside Abner Uribe, Aaron Ashby, DL Hall and Chad Patrick, with Antonio Senzatela and JoJo Romero at liberty — and the reader should mark that the wire's man, surveying trade candidates for all thirty clubs this morning, names Megill himself the champion likeliest to be moved, three mornings after the front office serenaded him in these columns from a Metallica concert at the Sphere.5 The telephone in Milwaukee, as this paper has observed of the catcher Contreras, does not distinguish between the men it rings for.

The rotation behind them poses the winter's quieter question: which Kyle Harrison reports to the spring. Through 11 starts the young left-hander owned a 1.57 earned-run average with 73 strikeouts in 57 and a third innings — second in the game in strikeouts per nine — before a forearm complaint and an August return that found the fastball dimmed; from August onward he pitched to a 7.31 figure over 32 innings and finished the season in the bullpen, unused through October. The ailment was reckoned mild, the underlying numbers kinder than the surface ones, and the house arithmetic is plain: the April version makes a front three with Misiorowski and Logan Henderson, while Woodruff and May sit among the free agents.6

THE PINSTRIPE VACANCY: CHISHOLM GOES TO MARKET UNTENDERED AND SECOND BASE STANDS OPEN IN THE BRONX — THE WIRE NOMINATES LOWE OF THE SMOKY CITY, WHOSE FIVE-AND-THIRTY HOME RUNS WERE DRAWN, NEARLY EVERY ONE, TOWARD A PORCH LIKE THE ONE AT THE STADIUM

The same Bronx front office keeping its left-hander has a vacancy up the middle. Jazz Chisholm Jr. has gone to the open market after two seasons and more in pinstripes, and the club declined to attach a qualifying offer to him — a quiet verdict of its own, for the tender it did extend went to the closer Bednar. Chisholm's season explains the hesitation: 19 home runs with a .399 slugging mark, the underlying measurements grading his contact at a .366 expected slug, down in the 24th percentile of the league, though his glovework at second — 15 outs above average, the best of his career — will be missed by a defense that can ill spare it. The internal answers are familiar: Anthony Volpe, who moved to second when young George Lombard Jr. took shortstop, with the utility men José Caballero and Luis García Jr. behind him.7

The wire's suggestion is Brandon Lowe, the left-handed second baseman who struck 35 home runs with an .824 on-base-plus-slugging for Pittsburgh this past season — a Ray for seven and a half years before the Smoky City nine acquired him in mid-2025.8 The case is a matter of architecture. Of his 35 home runs, 32 went to right field, pulled in the air at a career-high rate — and he compiled them in the yard the statisticians rank 27th of 30 for the long ball, while Yankee Stadium, with its short right porch, ranks among the friendliest; by Statcast's reckoning the same batted balls at the Stadium would have numbered 39. Nor is the glove the liability it was: from ten runs below average in 2025 he swung to twelve above in 2026, third-best at the position and a finalist for the National League's Gold Glove, tied for the largest year-over-year turnaround the measurers have recorded. For a lineup that hit 223 home runs, second-most in the game, yet batted ten percent below league average while Judge convalesced from the first of June to the seventh of September — only the Angels fared worse in that stretch — the appeal writes itself. The club enters its 18th year without a championship, tied for the longest such passage since Ruth came over in 1919; the winter's shopping list, bullpen first and second base close behind, is drawn up accordingly.7

THE EXCHANGE DESK: ONE NAME PINNED TO EVERY CLUBHOUSE DOOR — THE WIRE'S MAN NOMINATES A TRADE CANDIDATE FOR ALL THIRTY CLUBS, AND THE HERALD FINDS HALF ITS WINTER'S STORYLINES ALREADY ON THE LIST

The wire's senior correspondent has performed the winter's great parlor exercise — one plausible trade candidate named for every club in the land — and the Herald, reading down the list, keeps meeting its own back pages.5 Jarren Duran stands for Boston, the outfielder an evaluator called "clearly available" in Saturday's columns, his outfield crowded further by the Bauers purchase. Shane McClanahan stands for the Tampa men, confirming the sorting these pages recorded Saturday of the pennant-winners' rotation into the kept and the biddable. Ketel Marte stands for the Snakes — the twice-silvered second baseman whose dialogue with Baltimore filled yesterday's columns, his ten-and-five veto noted in the same breath — and for the Baltimores themselves the nominee is Jackson Holliday, the young infielder whose September wrist surgery helped open the very vacancy Marte would fill: the buyer's own candidate, in other words, is the price the seller may ask. George Kirby stands for the mariners of the Sound, one of the two names these columns reckoned likeliest to be had when Dipoto allowed on Friday that his rotation was eight deep and his lineup listing.5

The fresher entries concern the coasts. For the Dodgers the nominee is Teoscar Hernández, whose departure would clear a garden spot for the prospect Josue De Paula; for the Yankees it is Jasson Domínguez, the once-celebrated young outfielder still without a settled station, squeezed now by the arrival of Spencer Jones. Add the champions' Megill, treated in the column above, and the exercise reads less like speculation than like a timetable: the names the winter has been circling, now written down in one place.5

THE ALMANAC: THE SEVENTEENTH ARRIVES ON THE MORROW BEARING ANSWERS AND SILVERWARE TOGETHER — QUALIFYING VERDICTS FALL DUE AT LAST, AND THE SILVER BATS ARE HANDED ROUND WITH TROUT IN PURSUIT OF A TENTH, COMPANY ONLY PIAZZA AND BONDS HAVE KEPT

The appointment this paper's calendar has carried for a week arrives tomorrow, and it is a double one. The qualifying offers meet their deadline: Bednar of the Yankees and Morejon of the Padres are among the men still dragging the $23.15 million tender behind them, and by evening each must take the certain money or the open market — the reader will recall that Bauers answered his tender early, in Boston's ink, and that the compensation machinery has already begun to grind in Milwaukee.2

The same evening brings the silver bats. The Silver Slugger awards, whose finalists were posted on the fifth of the month, are to be revealed tomorrow, and the ornament of the slate is Mike Trout: the old master seeks his tenth silver bat, a shelf only Mike Piazza, with ten, and Barry Bonds, with twelve, have ever reached, and his case is honest — an .803 on-base-plus-slugging across 142 games, his best figure in ten years. Elsewhere among the finalists stand the newly laureled Alvarez, who led the game in each leg of the batting line; Alonso of the Baltimores and his league-best 43 home runs; and Bauers again, nominated at first base and in the utility class alike — while the team prize for the best offense in each league pits the Astros, Tampa men, Twins and White Sox in the American against the champions, the Cubs, the Boys in Blue and the Nationals in the National.9 The Herald will have the verdicts in Wednesday's columns, polished and arranged.

A Monday on which the winter kept its shops open through the weekend: a left-hander retained in the Bronx, a reclamation project carted into Milwaukee, and the morrow's docket already posted, as the almanac column attests — qualifying answers and silver bats together on the seventeenth. The forward ledger was ruled open before a word was written, per the house custom, and stands unchanged: the camps' reporting dates are still to be posted, so the paper counts to the first rung bearing a date — the spring exhibition openers of Friday, the 19th of February, 95 mornings from this one — with Opening Night to follow on Wednesday, the 24th of March, 128 mornings off.1 Nearer at hand, the collective agreement has 15 mornings left to live, and Cooperstown's sixteen electors report their verdict on December 6, 20 mornings from this one. The game plays nothing and decides everything, which is November's whole constitution. Until the morrow's dispatch, keep your scorecards near and your enthusiasms nearer.
