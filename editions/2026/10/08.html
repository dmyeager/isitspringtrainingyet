Reporting 4 contests from the day prior.

The Game of the Day

A SPECTATOR'S HAND RETURNS TO THE RIGHT-FIELD STANDS AND THIS TIME THE COURT COLLECTS — MESA'S TWO-RUN BROADSIDE OFF FRIED STANDS AS THE DIFFERENCE, VOLPE'S TYING BLOW IS RULED DOWN TO A DOUBLE, AND TAMPA BAY SWEEPS NEW YORK FROM OCTOBER: RAYS 4, YANKEES 3

Thirty Octobers less a single evening after a boy of twelve turned a fly ball into Bronx scripture, the replay court reverses the old miracle — Anthony Volpe's apparent game-tying two-run wallop in the sixth is found to have met a spectator's glove over the field of play, worth one run and second base and nothing more — while Victor Mesa Jr., who had already answered Ryan Vilade's first October four-bagger with a two-run drive of his own off Max Fried, supplies the margin; Ian Seymour wins in relief of the opener Martinez, Baker locks the ninth, and the Rays claim the first three-games-and-out sweep of a best-of-five in franchise annals, their third Championship Series berth, with the Guardians-White Sox survivor due at their dome Monday

On the ninth of October, 1996, a twelve-year-old named Jeffrey Maier reached over the right-field fence of the old Yankee Stadium and knocked a fly ball of Derek Jeter's into the seats. The umpires called it a home run rather than the interference it plainly was; the Yankees tied the Orioles that evening, won the game, won the series, and in time won everything, while the boy was toasted in Manhattan and cursed in Baltimore.5 The National Pastime keeps its ledgers open a long while, and on Wednesday night — thirty years later, less one evening, in the same right-field corner of the Bronx — the account came due.

The situation was composed with an archivist's care. Sixth inning, New York trailing 4-2, George Lombard Jr. aboard, and Volpe lined a sinker from Griffin Jax deep to left-center, where it carried to the wall, over the leap of Victor Mesa Jr., and was signaled a game-tying home run. Then the modern instrument did what 1996 could not: the replay court found that a spectator had met the ball with his glove over the field of play, that it would neither have cleared the barrier nor been caught, and wrote the entry down as it deserved — an RBI double, one run in, 4-3.4 Even the aggrieved manager filed no true grievance. “I thought they got it right,” said Aaron Boone, which may stand as the evening's epitaph.

The game around the famous call was a taut and worthy thing. Tampa Bay scratched first in the third, Jonathan Aranda singling home DeLuca while Taylor Walls was cut down at the plate on a relay that traveled right field to first base to shortstop to catcher. Ben Rice — the one Yankee bat October never cooled — answered at once. Vilade, a name new to the postseason register, put the visitors ahead with the first October home run of his life in the fourth; Spencer Jones doubled the score level again. And in the sixth Mesa turned on Fried for the two-run drive to right-center that settled the business, the left-hander's second surrender of the night and his last.

From there the Tampa relay — Seymour, Jax, Booser, Kelly, Baker — carried the one-run verdict home, the staff fanning twelve New York batsmen in all, and when Trent Grisham went down swinging to end the ninth, the visitors' clubhouse became a dance hall for the third time in a fortnight. The sweep is the first Tampa Bay has ever administered in a series of five, and only the second ever suffered by the Yankees in one, the other dating to the Championship Series of 1980. The Rays, champions of their division for the first time in five years, advance to the third Championship Series in the franchise's history, where the survivor of Cleveland and Chicago will call upon them Monday.3

The scrapbook this paper watched New York take down from the shelf on Tuesday — the flip of 2001, the rally of 2017 — stayed shut. The Bronx offense managed seven hits on the final night and never once held the lead; its season ends in the Division round for want of swinging, as its manager conceded, and the winter's accounting, which began within minutes of the final strike, is taken up elsewhere in this number.

“There's two more bites on the bone for us,” said the reliever Nick Martinez amid the champagne, quoting his manager's arithmetic — eleven wins wanted, eight remaining.3 The Herald observes only that a franchise long praised for doing much with little has now done the hardest thing October asks: it has made the Yankees watch.

The Postseason Card

RAMÍREZ EMPTIES EVERY POCKET OF THE TOOLBOX AND CLEVELAND DECLINES THE BROOM — TWO TWO-OUT TRIPLES, NINETEEN STRIKEOUTS BY THE VISITING RELAY, AND THE LAKE ERIE MEN DRAG THE QUARREL TO A FOURTH EVENING: GUARDIANS 9, WHITE SOX 3

The cold order this paper weighed yesterday — seven hits in sixty-one at-bats — wakes all at once on the South Side: Kwan singles home the tying run, Adell clears the sacks with a two-out triple in a four-run third, and Ramírez, wrist be hanged, files a double, a triple, two runs, two runs driven home, a stolen base and a diving stop at third into a single afternoon's work, while seven Cleveland pitchers fan nineteen Chicago batsmen and the bullpen-made hosts are left to answer at eight o'clock tonight, Messick against Hagen Smith, still one win from a first pennant-round berth since 2005

Cleveland arrived at Rate Field with its season reduced to a single afternoon and its batting order, as this paper measured only yesterday, carrying seven hits in sixty-one October at-bats. The response was a nine-run recital. The hosts struck first — Kyle Teel's double cashing Sam Antonacci in the opening inning — and then the third arrived and the famine ended: Steven Kwan lined the tying single, and Jo Adell, with two down, emptied the sacks with a triple past the right fielder — three runs on one swing and a 4-1 lead that was never seriously disputed again.

The afternoon belonged, as Cleveland afternoons will, to José Ramírez. The 34-year-old captain — he of the fractured summer and the aching wrist who told this paper's wire brethren that pain was no excuse — went 2-for-4 with a double, a two-run triple sliced on an 0-2 curveball in front of a diving Grichuk in the eighth, two runs scored, a stolen base, and a gloved robbery at third base that the Chicago crowd heard in silence.8 His teammates have ceased to be surprised. “Why are you surprised? This is what I do,” runs his standing answer, as catcher and outfield alike attested.

The pitching ledger deserves its own paragraph: seven Cleveland arms — Espino briefly, then Griffin brilliantly, seven strikeouts across two and two-thirds for the win, then Armstrong, Cantillo, Gaddis, Herrin and Smith — fanned nineteen White Sox batsmen in nine innings, a smothering the box score renders as six hits and three runs, one of them pushed across by a hit batsman. Chicago's own relay, assembled bullpen-fashion behind the opener Newcomb, was solvent for two innings and ruinous thereafter; Hudson was charged with the third-inning collapse.

So the series stands two games to one for Chicago, and the arithmetic is simple and loud. Game 4 is tonight at eight o'clock Eastern on the South Side, the left-hander Parker Messick (12-9, 2.56, with 193 strikeouts on the season's books) against the left-hander Hagen Smith (3-3, 1.29);10 a fifth game, should Cleveland force it, would be played Saturday by the lake in Cleveland.2 The White Sox remain one victory from a first Championship Series since 2005 — and, as this paper has noted, from a first home playoff clincher since the World Series of 1906. The ghost of that drought now has company in the building: all four of Chicago's October wins this fall were taken on the road, and the home crowd has yet to see one.

THE NINTH MAN IN THE ORDER SPEAKS LOUDEST FOR THE DEEPEST ORDER IN THE LAND — PAGES UNLOADS THE BASES IN THE SEVENTH, MUNCY APPENDS A PARTING SHOT, AND THE ANGELENOS PASS TO A THIRD STRAIGHT CHAMPIONSHIP SERIES: DODGERS 4, BRAVES 1

A game of errors, walks and one wild pitch waits all evening for a single clean swing, and it belongs to the No. 9 hitter — Andy Pages lining a bases-loaded single to center with two out in the seventh for the lead — before Muncy's ninth-inning drive to right, his third of this postseason, closes both the 4-1 verdict and Atlanta's season of eight runs in four October games; Glasnow survives five walks on one hit allowed, Henriquez wins in relief, Díaz seals it, and the Atlantas go to winter while Los Angeles packs for Milwaukee and Sunday

The clinching game at Truist Park was an untidy affair that waited seven innings for someone to put it in order, and it was fitting — perhaps ominous, if you bat leadoff for anyone remaining in this tournament — that the man who did so hits ninth for Los Angeles. With two out and the bases loaded in the seventh of a 1-1 game, Andy Pages lined a single to center that cashed two runs, and the Division Series was, in every sense that matters, concluded on the spot.1

What preceded it was attrition. Atlanta's lone run scored in the first on a wild pitch from Tyler Glasnow, who then walked five men across four and two-thirds innings while allowing precisely one hit — a tightrope act above a canyon that never quite became a plunge. Los Angeles leveled in the second when Max Muncy reached on a Dubón error that let Ohtani cross, one of two Atlanta misplays on the night. Mahle, the Atlanta starter, was excellent and unlucky: six and a third innings, three hits, one earned run, beaten by the seventh-inning inheritance his bullpen could not protect. Muncy supplied the coda in the ninth, a drive over the right-field barrier — his third circuit of this postseason — for the final 4-1.

The Atlanta epitaph is written in the hitting column: three hits on the night, two of them by the blameless Michael Harris II, and eight runs across the four games of the series. The pitching kept every defeat within reach; the batting declined, to the last, to reach. A franchise that pushed the titleholders to a fourth game will spend its winter asking why the lumber never arrived.

Los Angeles, by contrast, advances to a third consecutive Championship Series in pursuit of a third consecutive title, and does so with its October machinery humming in the late innings — Henriquez the winner, Scott the bridge, Díaz the lock for his second save of the round. The itinerary is fixed: Game 1 of the National League Championship Series is Sunday the 11th in Milwaukee, Game 2 Monday, against the Brewers outfit that eliminated San Diego hours later.2 The two clubs met in this very round last October; the rematch the whole league assumed all summer is now a fact of the schedule.

THE WALL AT THE MISSION SHORE GIVES NOTHING BACK — MITCHELL TRIPLES AND SCORES THE GO-AHEAD RUN, THEN LEAPS THE BARRIER'S EDGE TO STEAL MACHADO'S EQUALIZER, AND THE SUDS MEN TAKE THE SERIES WHILE AN OCTOBER ROAD FAMINE DIES AT A DOZEN: BREWERS 3, PADRES 1

One night after this paper measured Milwaukee's twelve straight October road defeats against a mark sixty-two years old, the streak is put to death in the clinching game — Garrett Mitchell authoring the whole verdict with an RBI single in the third, a fifth-inning triple and the tie-breaking run that followed it, and at the last a leaping, fan-jostled catch at the center-field wall that swallows Manny Machado's 406-foot bid to square the eighth; Gasser and Ashby split seven strong innings, Megill closes a three-hitter, the new father Miller throws a scoreless frame in a lost cause, and San Diego's season ends one swing short of extra arguments

Every October series ends on a handful of inches, and the National League's second Division Series ended on the few between Garrett Mitchell's glove and the top of the center-field wall. Eighth inning, Milwaukee ahead 3-1, a man aboard, and Machado met a sinker from Abner Uribe with the full fury of a proud season — 106.7 miles the hour off the bat, a projected 406 feet, Petco Park rising as one to call it even.9 Mitchell drifted back, leapt at the barrier's edge, took a nudge from the reaching crowd — “I'm not quite sure if I got grabbed,” he allowed afterward — and came down with the baseball and, for all practical purposes, the series. “Nice play,” was Machado's verdict. “Should have hit it harder.”

It was Mitchell's game long before the catch. His single cashed Frelick for the first run in the third; after Tatis answered with a drive to center to level it, Mitchell tripled in the fifth and came home on Chourio's single for the lead that never left. Frelick's sacrifice fly added the third run in the sixth. The pitching was a two-man argument settled early: the young Gasser gave four innings and the Tatis run, Aaron Ashby followed with three hitless, and Uribe and Megill — the latter for his second save of the round — carried the three-hitter home. San Diego's offense, so resourceful in the middle games of this series, finished with three hits and the lone Tatis blow.

And so fell a famine of genuine antiquity. As this paper set down only yesterday, Milwaukee had lost twelve consecutive October road games, a run tying a mark sixty-two years in the making; it ends in the most emphatic manner available, a clinching on foreign ground. The Brewers advance to a second consecutive Championship Series, where the Dodgers — their executioners of last autumn — arrive in Milwaukee for Game 1 on Sunday the 11th.2 The first seed in the league will at last play a round with the home advantage it spent six months earning.

For San Diego the ledger closes: a series lost three games to one, a closer restored from the paternity list in time to throw one spotless and irrelevant inning, and a winter of hard questions taken up, as all such questions are, in the colder columns of November.

News Around the League

THE RECKONING DESK: JUDGE BIDS THE COUNTING-HOUSE GO ALL IN — THE CAPTAIN, A SPECTATOR FOR ALL FIVE OCTOBER CONTESTS, SETS THE WINTER'S AGENDA WHILE BOONE KEEPS THE WHEEL

The last strike was scarcely called in the Bronx before the captain convened the winter. Aaron Judge, held out of all five of New York's postseason games by the right calf he had spent two weeks arguing with, delivered a pointed message to the front office: “We're the Yankees... I feel like they should go all in and do what they need to do,” naming no player and sparing no purse. Asked whether the club had gone all in heretofore, he hesitated — “It's tough to say” — before allowing that a winning team every year “is not good enough here in New York.”6

The seventeenth season has now passed since the organization's last championship, and the machinery is creaking audibly. General manager Brian Cashman's contract expires this offseason; manager Aaron Boone, nine years at the helm with a regular-season ledger of 790-565 and one pennant (the 2024 flag, surrendered to Los Angeles in five), has a year remaining, and the grandstands were chanting for his removal even as Grisham swung through the season's final pitch. Judge, for his part, wants Boone kept: “He's our manager. He's running the ship.” Among the prominent men whose papers now come due: Jazz Chisholm Jr., Trent Grisham, and the closer David Bednar.6

The Herald will note, as is its custom when a counting-house pleads its circumstances, that the captain's arithmetic is sound. The purse exists; the question, as ever, is the will to open it.

THE PENNANT DESK: THE FINAL FOUR TAKE THEIR MARKS — MILWAUKEE RECEIVES THE CHAMPIONS SUNDAY IN A SECOND STRAIGHT OCTOBER QUARREL, AND TAMPA BAY AWAITS A GUEST STILL TO BE NAMED

The bracket, so crowded a week ago, is nearly swept clean. In the National League the Championship Series is fixed: Los Angeles at Milwaukee, Game 1 Sunday the 11th, Game 2 Monday the 12th, both in Milwaukee.2 It is a rematch of last autumn's quarrel and a meeting of the league's first and second seeds — the Brewers reach a second consecutive Championship Series; the Dodgers, winners of the last two World Series, a third straight. Milwaukee will remember that its October road famine ended only Wednesday; Los Angeles will remember everything else.

In the American League, Tampa Bay's seat is reserved and the other chair stands empty. The Championship Series opens Monday the 12th at the Rays' dome, against whichever of Cleveland and Chicago survives a Division Series that Chicago leads two games to one with Game 4 on the South Side tonight.23 The Rays, having dispatched New York without the inconvenience of a defeat, are granted four days' rest and the pleasure of watching their next opponent spend its pitching.

The World Series is appointed to begin the 23rd of October, with ground held for a seventh game on the 31st should the quarrel require one.2 The Herald counsels against wasting an evening of it.

THE ALMANAC DESK: FOUR CONTESTS, FOUR VISITORS VICTORIOUS — A THING NEVER BEFORE SEEN IN OCTOBER'S LONG LEDGER

Wednesday's slate deserves a line in the annals apart from its clinchings. Four postseason games were played, and the road team won every one of them — Cleveland at Chicago, Tampa Bay at New York, Los Angeles at Atlanta, Milwaukee at San Diego — and the researchers confirm it had never happened before. Days of four or more postseason games have occurred fifty times, beginning once in the split season of 1981 and yearly since the Division Series arrived in 1995, and never until now had the visitors run the table.7

The opposite feat is on the books twice: the home nines swept four-game days on the 5th of October, 2018, and the 1st of October, 2020 — though the latter, played in the empty ballparks of that strange season, had no crowds to please.7 Wednesday's grandstands, by contrast, were full and went home disappointed in four cities at once, a melancholy quadruple the Herald does not expect to see matched soon.

The moral, if October owes us one: the modern game has made a fiction of the home advantage in its sternest month. Three of the four visitors clinched on foreign lawns; the fourth, Cleveland, saved its season there. The bunting, it appears, is purely decorative.

THE STORK DESK: THE NEW FATHER RETURNS TO A CAUSE PAST SAVING — MILLER, BACK FROM THE CRADLE-SIDE, THROWS A SPOTLESS INNING IN THE EVENING HIS CLUB EXPIRES

A coda to the week's most human story. Mason Miller, the hardest-throwing man in San Diego's employ, was restored from the paternity list Wednesday, his first child safely arrived, and took the ball in a game his club could not afford to lose.1 He gave it his customary best — an inning without a run, two singles stranded — and it mattered not at all, for the Milwaukee men had already banked the lead that ended the Friars' season.

The Herald's readers will recall the arc entire: Monday's number found Miller lined into heartbreak by young Chourio with the bases loaded; Tuesday's found the film men of San Diego declining, twice, to say whether the closer was showing his cards; yesterday's found him departing an elimination game for the birth of his first child, with Peralta summoned to his roster seat. Now the season he left is finished, and the fatherhood he left it for endures. Of the two ledgers, the Herald knows which it would rather hold.

San Diego's winter questions — the aging core, the thin purse, the closer's own approaching market — are questions for another number. Today the paper records only that a man kept both of his appointments in the same week, and that neither the ballclub nor the baby can claim he shorted them an inning.

A rare economy tonight: one contest only, Cleveland at Chicago at eight o'clock Eastern, with a season on one side of the scale and a pennant-round berth on the other. Friday the calendar rests entirely; Saturday holds a fifth game by the lake should Cleveland force it tonight; Sunday the National League's Championship Series opens in Milwaukee, and Monday the American's at Tampa Bay, with the World Series appointed for the 23rd.2 The Herald, having watched four road clubs strip four home crowds of their evening in a single day, offers the grandstands of the South Side its sincerest wishes and no guarantees. Until the morrow's dispatch, keep your scorecards near and your enthusiasms nearer.
