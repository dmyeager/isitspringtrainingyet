The Game of the Day

THIRTY YEARS OF WINNER-TAKE-ALL SORROW, STRUCK FROM THE LEDGER IN NINE INNINGS — HARRIS GOLFS A SINKER THIRTEEN INCHES OFF THE EARTH FOR THREE, KERR THE ONCE-FORGOTTEN JOURNEYMAN BLANKS THE ORDER, AND SALE WALKS OUT OF THE BULLPEN TO BAR THE DOOR: ATLANTAS 6, PHILADELPHIAS 2, AND THE SERIES WITH IT

Nola cannot find the plate and the hometown boy makes him pay the full tariff — a three-run thunderclap in the first on the lowest pitch ever hit for one of his hundred-and-one home runs — whereupon Albies and Olson add blasts of their own, three homers from the three men Philadelphia evicted from October in '22 and '23 alike; Kerr, told only Wednesday night he would open the decider, carves three and one-third one-hit innings out of the season's biggest game, and Sale, two days removed from ninety-five pitches, claims the final four outs to become the first man since his own 2018 self to start a series' first game and finish its last — Atlanta's first series won since the 2021 World Series, its first winner-take-all survived since the seventh game of the 1996 Championship Series, and a date with the twice-defending champion Dodgers on Saturday afternoon

Measure the decisive blow in inches, as the counting-men did. Aaron Nola's 2-0 sinker stood thirteen inches and a fraction above the Georgia clay — 1.11 feet, by the official instruments — when Michael Harris II reached down and golfed it on a line at a hundred miles the hour and a half more, Bryce Harper tracking it to the right-center wall only to watch it clear the brick by a hand's width. Three runs, first inning, and all the evening's scoring Atlanta would strictly require. The instruments pronounced it the lowest pitch ever converted into any of Harris's hundred-and-one home runs, the lowest Nola has ever had hit for one, and the third-lowest struck for a postseason homer since the tracking era began in 2008 — and it was hit by a son of the Atlanta soil, drafted by the home club out of its own suburbs, in the first winner-take-all October game of his life. Nola, who had walked Acuña and Olson on five pitches apiece to load the crisis and thrown but three of his first thirteen offerings for strikes, lasted five outs; Wheeler, the arm Philadelphia spent Sunday just to reach this round, gave four creditable innings of relief behind him, but the deficit had been dug.

The shutting of the door was a relay of improbables, and the first hand on it belonged to Ray Kerr. Told only Wednesday night that he would open the season's biggest game, the two-and-thirty-year-old left-hander — six weeks removed from Triple-A Gwinnett and the long road back from the 2024 elbow surgery — was handed the one assignment that mattered, the neutralizing of Schwarber and Harper atop the order, and he did that and more: three and one-third scoreless innings, a single hit, the Philadelphia bats carving at air. The man's history makes the arithmetic look small. A boyhood spent partly in foster homes while his father served time; a 2015 season pitching for a men's-league nine in Reno while clerking at a 7-Eleven and a movie house; and now the opener's ball in an October decider. "This was just another Thursday," said Kerr. "Growing up, I had a hard time. So, this was nothing." His manager Weiss put it otherwise: "Hopefully Ray Kerr became a bit of a household name tonight. He deserves it."

The middle chapters confirmed the verdict. Albies turned a Wheeler offering into the right-center seats with Riley aboard in the fourth, 5-0; Olson added a solo clout off Bowlan in the seventh — three home runs on the night, struck by the three Braves who were present to be evicted from October by these same Philadelphians in both 2022 and 2023, each man collecting a measure of his own account. The visitors' resistance came in polite installments: Schwarber came round on Bohm's sixth-inning groundout, and Derek Hill, having tripled in the eighth, scored on Turner's sacrifice fly — two runs, no more, against the procession of Holmes (two innings and the victory), Suarez, Fuentes, and Dodd.

And then the theater. Chris Sale — who had planted himself in Weiss's office chair in the afternoon before the manager could even raise the question, announcing "I feel amazing" — emerged from the bullpen in the eighth and struck out Harper to end it, then finished the ninth: four outs, a little more than eight-and-forty hours after throwing ninety-five pitches across six and one-third innings in Game One. The scribes certify him the first pitcher to start a series' opening game and close out that same series since one Chris Sale, Boston, World Series of 2018 — he and Kershaw now the only men to have done it twice. So fell a pair of droughts at a single stroke: Atlanta's first postseason series won since the 2021 World Series, and its first winner-take-all game survived since the seventh game of the 1996 Championship Series, six straight such defeats — the third-longest run of them in the annals — now closed. The application the Phils filed in these columns to become the fourth club of the best-of-three era to lose an opener and advance was denied without comment, before a sold-out Truist Park that stayed to watch the champagne. Next: the Division Series, Saturday at four o'clock Eastern at Dodger Stadium, on the Fox circuits, with triple-digit heat in the forecast and the twice-defending champions waiting — a club Atlanta beat five times in six meetings this summer, outscoring them 26 to 15, for whatever such summer arithmetic is worth in October.

News Around the League

THE OCTOBER DESK: THE EIGHT ARE NAMED AND THE CARD IS FULL — FOUR DIVISION SERIES OPEN SATURDAY FROM ONE O'CLOCK TO HALF PAST EIGHT, CLEVELAND HANDS ITS OPENER TO THE ROOKIE MESSICK, AND LOS ANGELES IS EXPECTED TO ANSWER ATLANTA WITH YAMAMOTO

The bracket's last blank is filled, and the schedule stands thus: Saturday the White Sox call at Cleveland at one o'clock, Atlanta at Los Angeles at four, the Yankees at Tampa Bay at half past six, and the Padres at Milwaukee at half past eight; the second games go Sunday at four in Milwaukee and eight in Los Angeles, Monday at five in Cleveland and eight in Tampa Bay, with Atlanta's series returning to Truist Park for a third game Tuesday evening. Friday carries not a contest anywhere in the land — the last quiet day before the four quarrels run concurrent.

Cleveland has shown its hand first. The manager Vogt names Parker Messick, the five-and-twenty-year-old rookie left-hander, for Game One against Chicago — a man who had to argue his way onto the roster in the spring and finished sixth in the game at a 2.56 figure, his three-and-ninety-and-a-hundred strikeouts the most by a Cleveland first-year man save Herb Score's 245 of 1955 — with Gavin Williams, whose 248 strikeouts led the American League and trailed only Misiorowski's 252 in all the game, to follow in Game Two. "They've been our two best pitchers," says Vogt. "They've earned it and then some." The Dodgers have made no formal announcement, but the expectation of the Los Angeles camp is Yamamoto for Game One — the only arrangement that returns their ace on regular rest for a fifth game, with Skubal, Snell, and Glasnow filling out a rotation of uncomfortable depth, and the Ohtani-Betts platoon atop the order sorting itself by the hand of the opposing starter. Atlanta, by contrast, arrives with its rotation depleted: Sale cannot go before Sunday's Game Two, Mahle not before Tuesday, and Pérez, on the invalid rolls since the first of September with the low back, not before Game Three — leaving Holmes, who threw but two innings Thursday, to be asked for the early innings Saturday, with young Ritchie an option and a left-handed opener under consideration besides.

THE PHILADELPHIA RECKONING: A FOURTH STRAIGHT OCTOBER SERIES SURRENDERED AND THE COSTLIEST CORE IN THE GAME AT A CROSSROADS — MATTINGLY DECLINES TO PLEAD HIS OWN CASE WHILE THE SHADOW OF CORA FALLS ACROSS THE INTERIM CHAIR

In the visitors' clubhouse Thursday night the beat men watched a group of Phillies — Schwarber, Realmuto, Turner, Stott, Nola, Stubbs — stand in a corner and talk quietly, and noted that it was not like the gatherings of a few years ago. The club has now surrendered four consecutive postseason series while carrying one of the heaviest payrolls in the game, and the Philadelphia correspondent pronounces changes not merely likely but overdue. "It's going to be a long offseason, obviously," said Harper. "I obviously have trust in the organization and people that are in charge. We'll see where we go."

The first question on the docket is the manager's chair. Mattingly, who took the club at 9-19 and steered it to the fourth-best record in the game thereafter — 79 won, 55 lost, and likely votes for Manager of the Year to come — declined to argue his own retention: "It's not the time to talk about anything like that. My stuff doesn't really matter." He reports no conversations with the front office on the subject. But the shadow over the interim chair has a name: the beat men record that Dombrowski offered the position to Alex Cora on the 26th of April, two days before Thomson's dismissal, that Cora declined it then, and that so long as Cora sits available and the interim tag stays unaddressed, the industry expects the pursuit to resume. The players, for their part, spoke for the incumbent — "his demeanor was great for us," testified Schwarber — while Harper, asked directly, declined the question. The Herald observes that a man who inherits a shipwreck in April and has it racing by June has earned at least a hearing in November.

THE DUGOUT DESK: ESPADA DECLARES HE WOULD 'ABSOLUTELY' RETURN TO A HOUSTON THAT HAS NOT YET SAID SO — WHILE ARIZONA, WANTING NO SUSPENSE, RETAINS LOVULLO ON NEW PAPER

The succession question this paper opened Thursday has its first testimony: Joe Espada, whose contract expired with Houston's elimination, declares he would "absolutely" like to return to the Astros in 2027. The club, which must also seat a new general manager, has not yet said whether the feeling is mutual — and the Herald notes that a manager auditioning for his own job while the office above him stands vacant is a delicate position even by Texas standards.

Arizona, meanwhile, declined to let any such question linger: the Diamondbacks retain Torey Lovullo on a new contract, the winningest manager in the franchise's annals across his ten seasons, now bound for an eleventh. One chair filled, one chair wobbling, and the music only beginning — the autumn's managerial carousel is hereby declared open.

THE ALMANAC DESK: FALL BALL IN THE DESERT — THE ARIZONA AUTUMN CIRCUIT OPENS ITS FOUR-AND-THIRTIETH SEASON SATURDAY, FIFTEEN OF THE GAME'S HUNDRED FINEST PROSPECTS ON THE ROLLS

While eight clubs quarrel over the pennants, the future reports for duty in the desert: the Arizona Fall League opens its four-and-thirtieth season Saturday, carrying fifteen members of the game's Top Hundred prospect list — matching the richest enrollment of the past four years. The rolls run from a seventeen-year-old, Angeibel Gomez, ranked one-and-eightieth in the game, to a nine-and-twenty-year-old in Tyler LaPorte, four winters a veteran of the independent circuits, with a genuine All-Star among them in the rehabilitating San Francisco closer Randy Rodriguez. The circuit's pedigree wants no argument — Judge, Trout, and Harper all took their autumn cuts there, former Fall League Most Valuables include Arenado, Acuña, and Bryant, and ten of the eighteen position men who started this summer's All-Star Game are alumni. Last autumn's Most Valuable, the Detroit infielder McGonigle, hit .362 in the desert and proceeded to lead the whole American League in wins above replacement at one-and-twenty this summer. The games count for nothing and foretell a great deal; the Herald will keep an eye on the box scores from the cactus country.

The Friday tables, then — one contest entered, and the Wild Card round closed at three days' length: four series, three settled by sweep, and the one that went the distance settled by the club that had waited thirty years to win a game of the winner-take-all kind. The field for the Division Series is therefore complete — Chicago's White Sox, Cleveland, New York, and Tampa Bay carrying the American's colors; Atlanta, Los Angeles, Milwaukee, and San Diego the National's — and today the land is quiet, not a game from coast to coast, the calm before four quarrels run at once. Saturday the cannonade: Chicago at Cleveland at one o'clock, Atlanta at Los Angeles at four, New York at Tampa Bay at half past six, San Diego at Milwaukee at half past eight. Until the morrow's dispatch, keep your scorecards near and your enthusiasms nearer.
