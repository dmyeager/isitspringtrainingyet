Volume I · No. 85 · Thursday, the First of October, Two Thousand and Twenty-Six

Reporting 4 contests from the day prior.

The Game of the Day

TWO RUNS TO THE BAD AND SIX OUTS FROM WINTER, PHILADELPHIA ANSWERS WITH THREE THUNDERCLAPS — SCHWARBER AND HARPER BACK-TO-BACK IN THE EIGHTH, BOHM ALONE IN THE TENTH, AND THE MACHINE ITSELF CALLS THE FINAL STRIKE: PHILS 4, TOMAHAWK NINE 3, IN TEN INNINGS

Mahle gives Atlanta seven innings that deserved a kinder ledger and Harper's misplayed fly hands the hosts the lead besides — whereupon the Phils, measured for the coffin at 3-1, strike twice against Game One's winning pitcher and once against Fuentes in the tenth, young Painter takes his first taste of October and the victory with it, and Realmuto ends the night with the first game-ending ABS challenge in postseason history, sending the quarrel to a winner-take-all third chapter this very evening

Two runs to the bad and six outs from winter, Kyle Schwarber stood in against Dylan Lee — the man who had won Game One in relief — and swung Philadelphia's season back onto its hinges. One fly ball into the right-center seats; then Bryce Harper, the very next man, lifted another to nearly the same address; and a 3-1 deficit that had the undertakers out measuring was a 3-3 tie before Lee could record a second out. Two innings later Alec Bohm, two down in the tenth and nobody aboard, turned on Fuentes's offering and wrote the verdict into the right-field stands: 4-3, Phils, the series squared at a game apiece, and the club that stole the opener on Riley's eighth-inning thunder now made to play a third.

For seven innings it had belonged to Atlanta. Harper's first-inning single cashed Turner for 1-0, Olson's answered in the home half, and thereafter Tyler Mahle — 7-10 on the summer, as this paper's armory had him — gave seven innings of one-run, six-strikeout pitching that deserved a kinder ledger than the one he got. Sánchez, Philadelphia's eighteen-game winner, labored by comparison, five walks salted among his six and two-thirds, and the seventh inning cost him twice: Baldwin's catchable fly fell past Harper's misjudgment in right for a double, Murphy scoring, and Olson's sacrifice fly cashed Kim for 3-1 — the precise score from which the eighth-inning resurrection was conducted.

The last three innings belonged to a man playing his first October game. Andrew Painter, the young right-hander, took the ball with the season in his palm and gave three scoreless innings — three hits scattered, three strikeouts, not a run permitted — and claimed the victory for his trouble. The final pitch made history of its own denomination: Painter's two-out delivery to the pinch-hitter Smith, with two Braves aboard, was called a ball — whereupon the pitcher tapped his head, Realmuto tapped his in answer, and the automated arbiter reversed the call to strike three. The first game-ending challenge of a postseason, the league's scribes certify, and a ballgame concluded by nerve and machinery in equal parts.

So to this evening's decider — eight o'clock at Truist Park, on the NBC and Peacock circuits — and for once the probables ledger offers no names at all. "All hands on deck," declares the interim manager Mattingly of his pitching plans, while Atlanta weighs Holmes, who played coy on whether he starts or follows an opener, with Kerr and Suter among the arms behind him. The winner proceeds to the Division Series and the hundred-win Dodgers; and the Phils, should they prevail, would become but the fourth club of this best-of-three era to lose the opener and live to advance — by the very ledger this paper set out Wednesday.

The Postseason Card

ONE-AND-TWENTY YEARS BETWEEN OCTOBER HANDSHAKES — THE SOUTH SIDERS FINISH THE SAME HOUSTON CLUB THEY LAST BEAT IN THE 2005 WORLD SERIES, AND THE LONGEST WAIT IN THEIR LEDGER ENDS IN A SWEEP: SOUTH SIDERS 7, ASTROS 3

A four-run first inning batters the erstwhile invalid Brown before he can record a second out, Houston's three answers arrive politely one runner at a time, and Meidroth's three-run thunderclap in the sixth closes the account — whereupon Hicks and Fedde carry the hosts out hitless across the final four frames, fifteen Texans go down on strikes, and a club that lost one-and-twenty-and-a-hundred games two summers ago advances to meet Cleveland in the first October quarrel the Central neighbors have ever staged

The last time the White Sox won a postseason series, they were shaking hands on this same Houston club's infield — the World Series of 2005, swept in four. One-and-twenty years later they have done it again, in the same city, by the same emphatic method: a two-games-to-none sweep, sealed 7-3 Wednesday night beneath the closed roof at Daikin Park, and the South Siders — two summers removed from a season of one-and-twenty-and-a-hundred defeats, the worst in the modern annals — advance to a Division Series no spring prophecy dared sketch for them.

The verdict was effectively rendered in the first inning. Brown, the erstwhile invalid this paper's armory had opposite Burke, faced nine men and retired but two: Teel's single cashed Antonacci, Benintendi's line drive brought home Teel and Vargas both — the second run a gift of Spence's throwing error from center — and Peters's single made it 4-0 before Houston had drawn a clean breath. The hosts answered in polite installments, a solo blast apiece from Peña, Smith, and Altuve off Burke, who otherwise struck out eight in his four innings; and at 4-3 the issue stood until the sixth, when Chase Meidroth — whose series closes at four runs driven home — met a Lambert offering with two aboard, Luisangel Acuña and Peters scampering home ahead of him, 7-3. Thereafter the Chicago relay simply closed the yard: Kay for the win, then Hicks and Fedde two hitless innings apiece, fifteen Houston strikeouts entered in the final accounting, and not a run permitted across the last five frames.

The chairman Reinsdorf watched his improbable club celebrate on the road — the beat men report him savoring a run not a soul saw coming, the Vatican's correspondence excepted, as this paper recorded Wednesday. Now comes Cleveland: Game One of the Division Series goes Saturday at one o'clock on Lake Erie's shore, the first postseason meeting between these Central neighbors in the whole of their long acquaintance, and the form sheet is no comfort to the division champions — Chicago took seven of the thirteen between them this summer, six of the thirteen settled by a single run. Game Two follows Monday at five o'clock.

THE OLDEST QUARREL ENDS IN TWO SHORT CHAPTERS, AGAIN — FRIED CARVES SIX LEAN INNINGS, THE SIXTH POURS SIX MORE, AND BOSTON GOES HOME BY THE SAME OCTOBER DOOR AS LAST YEAR: PINSTRIPES 9, BOSTONS 2

García strikes the go-ahead blast in his first postseason start and Bellinger — on the night his father throws the ceremonial first pitch — drives home four, while the unsealed Crochet faces four men and retires none, Gray departs at seventy pitches wishing to 'live or die' with his manager's trust, and the Bombers advance on an aggregate of eighteen runs to two, bound for Tampa Bay and the top seed Saturday evening

For the second October running, the game's oldest quarrel was adjourned in the minimum two chapters, and by verdicts that brooked no appeal: 9-0 Tuesday, 9-2 Wednesday, eighteen runs to two across the series entire. Max Fried supplied the craftsmanship this time — six innings, three hits, a single run, six strikeouts — and the Yankee rotation, the beat men observe, now squares its shoulders with some confidence toward the rounds ahead.

The decisive blows were struck by the supporting cast. Luis García Jr., making his first postseason start, brought the Bronx to its feet with the game's first run — a fourth-inning blast, his first in October — and finished with three hits; Cody Bellinger, on the night his father Clay, once a Yankee himself, threw the ceremonial first pitch, drove home four, the last three aboard the home run that crowned a six-run sixth. That inning turned on Boston's most awaited arm: Crochet, the ace unsealed from the sixty-day rolls only on the series' eve — he may be needed at once, this paper wrote Wednesday, and so he was — faced four men without recording an out, three hits and four runs the toll. The visitors' griefs did not end there. Gray, the eighteen-game winner, was lifted at seventy pitches with but two runs against him, and did not disguise his feelings afterward — he wished his manager Tracy had let the game "live or die with me," said the right-hander, and the quarrel over the hook will season Boston's winter. The Hub nine's whole offense came to two solo blasts, Contreras's in the sixth — upheld by the review men — and Sogard's in the seventh; Chisholm, for good measure, stole two bases and scored twice for the hosts.

So New York proceeds, and the captain's calendar comes due: the Division Series date Judge's rehabilitation has had circled since the calf gave way now arrives Saturday at half past six in Tampa Bay, where the top-seeded Rays — the American's best by the season-long ledger — await a club they have met in October once before, the 2020 Division Series, settled in five for the Floridians. Game Two goes Monday at eight o'clock; whether the captain's name appears on the roster, the morrow's dispatches will tell.

FIVE ARMS BRAID A FIVE-HITTER AND THE WALKING BOOT COMES OFF SWINGING — SHEETS'S PINCH-HIT THUNDER SETTLES LAST OCTOBER'S ACCOUNT IN FULL: MISSION NINE 4, CUBS 1

Pivetta hands a lead to a bullpen that never gives it back, Salas at twenty becomes the youngest man ever to catch a postseason game and drives home a run for good measure, and when the Petco lights cut out for Miller's ninth the sweep is a formality — San Diego, ejected from this very round by these very Cubs a year ago, advances to Milwaukee and the hundred-three-win Cream City nine

San Diego closed out its revenge with the weapon it trusts most: the bullpen gate. Five Friar arms braided a five-hitter Wednesday night — Pivetta for three and a third, then Rodriguez for the win, Adam and Morejon holding, Miller sealing — and the Cubs, who ejected this club from the Wild Card round a year ago, were swept from it in two games flat, 4-1 the final, a single run their whole October's produce against eighteen innings of San Diego pitching.

The runs came in businesslike pairs. In the second, Bogaerts lined a single to cash France, and Ethan Salas — twenty years of age, and Wednesday the youngest man ever to catch a big-league postseason game — lifted a sacrifice fly for 2-0, a run driven home to garnish his history. Hoerner's single halved the lead in the fourth, Suzuki scoring; and in the home half Gavin Sheets, who passed most of September in a walking boot and entered now as a pinch-hitter, met the reliever Palencia's offering for a two-run blast, Bogaerts ahead of him, 4-1. Gausman, Chicago's starter, battled through three and a third and wore the loss. The ninth belonged to theater as much as to baseball: the Petco lights cut to black, the crowd rose roaring, and Mason Miller — the game's most feared closer, by the league's own estimate — struck out two around a harmless single to end the series, the sellout house never resuming its seats.

The form chart now sends the Friars north: Game One at Milwaukee Saturday at half past eight, Game Two Sunday at four o'clock, against the top-seeded, hundred-three-win Cream City nine — the first postseason meeting of two franchises born in the same expansion spring of 1969. The season's small ledger favors the visitors, four of six, including an August sweep at Petco Park; the Herald notes merely that the Friars arrive hot, rested by Friday's idle day, and carrying a bullpen that has yet to surrender a run this October.

News Around the League

THE OCTOBER DESK: THREE SWEEPS ENGRAVE THREE-QUARTERS OF THE DIVISION-SERIES CARD — CLEVELAND AND CHICAGO TO MEET IN OCTOBER FOR THE FIRST TIME IN THEIR LONG ACQUAINTANCE, NEW YORK RETURNS TO TAMPA BAY, AND MILWAUKEE AWAITS THE FRIARS

The bracket wants but one name. Three of the four Wild Card quarrels ended in two straight, and the league has published the consequences: the second-seeded Guardians entertain the sixth-seeded White Sox at Cleveland, Game One Saturday at one o'clock — the first postseason meeting between the Central neighbors in all their shared history, and a delicate matter for the hosts, who won the division yet dropped seven of thirteen to Chicago on the summer; Game Two goes Monday at five. The top-seeded Rays, the American's best by the full-season ledger, receive the Yankees Saturday at half past six, with Monday's Game Two at eight — those clubs having met once before in October, the 2020 Division Series, which Tampa Bay took in five. And the top-seeded Brewers host the Padres Saturday at half past eight, Game Two Sunday at four — the first postseason meeting of two franchises born together in 1969, the Friars having taken four of six between them this season.

The fourth line of the card is written tonight at Truist Park, its winner bound for the hundred-win Dodgers. Friday the second carries not a game anywhere in the land — a travel day for the fortunate, a first day of winter for the rest — and then Saturday the October proper begins.

THE BASEPATHS DESK: TWO BROTHERS, FOUR THEFTS, ONE AFTERNOON AND EVENING — RONALD AND LUISANGEL ACUÑA EACH STEAL TWICE IN A SINGLE INNING, AND THE WIRE PRONOUNCES IT POSTSEASON HISTORY

Wednesday's basepaths belonged to a single surname. In Atlanta, Ronald Acuña Jr. — held hitless by Philadelphia's pitching but twice issued passes — stole two bases in one inning against the Phils; in Houston, his younger brother Luisangel, running for Chicago as a pinch man, stole two in one inning against the Astros before coming round to score on Meidroth's home run. Two steals apiece, each pair inside a single frame, on the same day of the same October — a family feat the league's own wire pronounces postseason history, and this paper is pleased to enter in the annals. Pitchers of the surviving clubs are advised that one of the brothers remains at large in the bracket.

THE FRONT OFFICE DESK: HOUSTON'S OCTOBER LASTS TWO DAYS AND THE RECKONING BEGINS AT ONCE — A GENERAL MANAGER TO BE FOUND, AND THE MANAGER ESPADA'S STANDING THE OPEN QUESTION OF THE TEXAS WINTER

The Astros' season closed Wednesday night in their own yard, and the club walks into winter carrying an unusual vacancy: the general managership itself, which Houston must now fill even as it weighs the standing of the field manager Espada. The ledger the beat men recite is a sobering one for a once-imperial house — four years now since the World Series championship, three years since the club so much as won a postseason game, the great run of seven consecutive Championship Series appearances, four pennants, and two titles receding steadily into the rearview. The Herald observes that dynasties are dismantled on exactly such quiet Thursdays as this, one vacancy at a time, and will report the succession as it is decided.

THE WINTER LEDGER OPENS EARLY ON THE NORTH SIDE: THE CUBS DEPART OCTOBER WITH ONE RUN IN TWO GAMES AND A 'SOUR TASTE' FOR PROVISIONS — THE LOOMING FREE AGENCIES OF HAPP AND SUZUKI STAND FIRST ON THE WINTER'S DOCKET

For Chicago's National League tenants the abruptness is the wound: two October games, a single run scored between them, and a season of genuine promise closed before the calendar's first weekend. The beat men report the clubhouse tasting of what one of its number called the "sour taste" of the finish, and the front office already staring at the winter's first-order business — the looming free agencies of Ian Happ and Seiya Suzuki, two fixtures of the order now at the ends of their papers. The Herald notes that a lineup held to a single tally across eighteen October innings will spend four months hearing about it, and that the answers the North Side assembles by spring will say much about how long this particular window stays propped open.

The Thursday tables, then — four contests entered, and the Wild Card round all but spent: three series closed by sweep, the brooms wielded by the sixth seed in Houston, the fourth in the Bronx, and the fourth again in San Diego, while the lone survivor of the trailing clubs plays on. Tonight the round concludes where it began, Philadelphia at Atlanta at eight o'clock, winner to Los Angeles; Friday the schedule stands empty from coast to coast; and Saturday the Division Series open in three yards at once — Chicago at Cleveland at one, New York at Tampa Bay at half past six, San Diego at Milwaukee at half past eight. The Herald's Wednesday ledger bears repeating at the brink: in all the brief history of this best-of-three fashion, but three clubs have dropped the opener and lived to advance, and the Phils tonight apply to be the fourth. Until the morrow's dispatch, keep your scorecards near and your enthusiasms nearer.
