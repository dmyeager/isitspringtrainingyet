The Game of the Day

A RUN IN THE FIRST, A RUN IN THE SECOND, A RUN IN THE THIRD, AND SEVEN INNINGS OF RASMUSSEN TO KEEP THEM — TWELVE HITS WITHOUT ONE OVER THE WALL MUFFLE THE FOREST CITY NINE, AND THE RAYS CARRY THE AMERICAN QUESTION HOME STANDING ONE VICTORY FROM THE WORLD'S SERIES: RAYS 3, GUARDIANS 1

Drew Rasmussen holds Cleveland to one run across seven innings on 95 pitches, feeding the left-handed hitters a changeup he did not own a year ago and retiring 14 of 15 men through the middle of the evening; Yandy Díaz opens the proceedings with a double and collects three hits; Jonny DeLuca forces in the first run with a bases-loaded walk that survives Cleveland's appeal to the electronic arbiter, and Jonathan Aranda and Liam Hicks single home the second and third; Steven Kwan throws out one runner at the plate and robs another at the center-field wall in a losing cause; and Bryan Baker closes his sixth spotless errand of the month — the pennant round returns to St. Petersburg with Tampa Bay ahead three games to two

Nobody in a gray uniform struck a ball over a wall at Progressive Field on Sunday night, and nobody needed to. Tampa Bay assembled its three runs the way a clerk assembles a ledger — one entry at a time, each in its proper column — scoring once in each of the first three innings, a thing the franchise had managed in only three previous October contests.5 In the first, Yandy Díaz led off with a double and walked home when Jonny DeLuca, the bases full, declined ball four; Cleveland's catcher Patrick Bailey appealed the call to the league's electronic arbiter, and the machine sided with the walk. In the second, Jonathan Aranda's single to left cashed Ben Williamson; in the third, Liam Hicks dropped a single into center to cash Chandler Simpson. Twelve hits the visitors collected, and only two — doubles by Díaz and Simpson — advanced a man past first base on their own authority; the beaten starter himself certified the method afterward, describing an unselfish nine that shoots the ball through holes rather than swinging for the fences.3 Tyler Wells, who contributed one strikeout to the cause, put it in the house style: "That was Rays baseball at its finest."5

The man guarding the entries was Drew Rasmussen, on the five days' rest this paper noted yesterday he has kept nearly all season: seven innings, four hits, one walk, six strikeouts, 95 pitches. After Chase DeLauter's two-out single in the first he retired nine men running and 14 of the next 15; only six Cleveland balls escaped the infield all evening, a mere three were hard-struck by the measuring instruments' reckoning, and the lone run arrived in the sixth by way of two singles and DeLauter's groundout.4 The quiet work was done by the newest tool in the box — a changeup built only this year, thrown 27 times to Cleveland's left-handed hitters, 28 percent of his pitches and the heaviest he has ever leaned upon it, without surrendering a hit. "It's probably been the single greatest addition I've made to my repertoire in the entirety of my career," said its author; "Three fastballs and a really good changeup," summarized Stephen Vogt, who found no answer to any of the four.4 The October account now reads 21 innings and three runs across three starts: no Tampa Bay pitcher before him had twice gone seven innings allowing one run or none in a postseason, none had strung three quality starts together, and the last man anywhere to bank two such starts before a World's Series was Logan Webb in 2021. Since the All-Star interlude Rasmussen stands 11-0 with a 1.73 earned-run average, 99 strikeouts against 18 walks in 93 2/3 innings, his club winning 14 of those 15 starts — figures that moved Díaz, through the club's interpreter, to a flat verdict: "I really do think he's the best pitcher in MLB."43 Behind him Cam Booser took two outs, Wells one, and Bryan Baker worked a clean ninth for his fifth save — six errands this October, no runs, one hit allowed in the whole of them, company kept only by Jason Motte and Mark Wohlers among pitchers who finished each of their first six postseason appearances.5

The defeat was not for want of gallantry. Steven Kwan, having judged his own second-inning throw insufficient, atoned in the fourth: he gathered Aranda's single in left and cut down Taylor Walls at the plate on one true bounce to Bailey — "off the bat, I thought there was no chance of a play," admitted Kevin Cash — and in the ninth, shifted to center field, he leapt at the wall to strip Victor Mesa Jr. of extra bases. "I was glad to save some runs there, but I left some meat on the bone, I felt like, with that first one," was the Gold Glove man's own accounting. José Ramírez added a barehanded pickup of Simpson's eighth-inning bunt, and Vogt spoke the epitaph: "That game could have looked a lot different without our defense tonight."6 True, and insufficient — for the deeper wound was the start. Parker Messick, moved up a game by the rainout's arithmetic as this paper set out Sunday, lasted two innings, the shortest start of his career, on 57 laboring pitches; his season's account now stands at exactly 200 innings against a previous professional high of 138 1/3, and the workload question the Herald carried yesterday morning answered itself by the third inning.7 Tampa Bay's leather answered Cleveland's besides: Williamson dove to rob Ramírez in the fourth, and Walls — "one of the best shortstops on the planet," per the opposing manager — spun in the grass behind second to end the fifth.5

So the scholars' arithmetic this paper offered Cleveland as comfort only yesterday changes its allegiance: in series knotted at two games apiece, the fifth game's winner has taken the argument in 46 cases of 69, and road winners of such fifth games in 21 of 28.3 Tampa Bay flies home wanting one victory for the third World's Series in franchise history, the visits of 2008 and 2020 having both ended in defeat — and should the Rays take it, the 122nd Series is guaranteed a first-time champion, for neither they nor the waiting Brewers have ever won one. Cleveland, whose 77 seasons without a title since 1948 stand as the game's longest sorrow, must now win twice in two nights on foreign carpet; "We need to rebound tomorrow," said Vogt, master of the available options.3 The 36,303 by the lake filed out quietly after 2 hours and 50 minutes of clear, 63-degree October. The dome tonight will be louder — the particulars of the sixth game are set out at the Elimination Desk below.

News Around the League

THE ELIMINATION DESK: PERALTA, SIX VICTORIES WITHOUT A DEFEAT AND AN 0.79 FIGURE SINCE THE SIX-AND-TWENTIETH OF AUGUST, TAKES THE BALL UNDER THE DOME TONIGHT — CLEVELAND ANSWERS BY COMMITTEE WITH WILLIAMS HELD FOR A SEVENTH GAME IT MUST FIRST EARN, AND THE TROP AWAITS THE FIRST PENNANT EVER CLINCHED ON ITS CARPET

The sixth game goes tonight at 8:08 at Tropicana Field, and the pitching ledgers could scarcely sit further apart. Tampa Bay hands the ball to Freddy Peralta — 10-11 with a 4.42 for the whole of his itinerant season, but 6-0 with a 0.79 earned-run average over nine appearances since August 26, including a 0.99 figure with 26 strikeouts across five starts under this very dome — fresh from six shutout innings in the second game and the two borrowed relief outs in the fourth that this paper recorded in its Sunday number.8 Cleveland must stitch its evening from the committee: Slade Cecconi may open, with Tanner Bibee — one-third of an inning pitched all month — and Joey Cantillo expected to carry roughly two innings apiece, the high-leverage men behind them. Gavin Williams, the round's great strikeout engine, is barred on short rest — "No, that's not an option," said Stephen Vogt, whose ace's fastball sagged from 99.2 to 95.4 miles the hour across five innings of the third game — and Foster Griffin and Parker Messick are both spent, so Williams waits instead upon a Tuesday seventh game his club must first earn.78 The committee's spokesman kept it short: "It's crunch time," said Cecconi. "Whatever they need, I'm ready for."7

The stage carries its own story. Tropicana Field, shuttered by Hurricane Milton two Octobers ago while the club kept house elsewhere, returned this season to harbor a 55-26 home campaign — the best in the game, jointly with Milwaukee's — and has yielded three victories in four postseason sittings; no Tampa Bay pennant has ever been clinched upon its carpet, the 2020 flag having been won as the home club of a neutral yard.10 Cleveland, for its part, has been in this hole before and climbed out — down two games to none against the White Sox only a round ago — and its manager declines all despair: "This is when this team thrives," says Vogt, and "if there's a group I'm betting on to get this done, it's our guys."78

THE PROCLAMATION DESK: 'WE'RE GOING TO WIN TOMORROW' — DÍAZ, FIVE HITS IN TWO NIGHTS AND THE LAST MAN STANDING FROM THE 2020 PENNANT-WINNERS, DECLARES THE ROUND CONCLUDED, WHILE THE CLEVELAND BATS HAVE MANAGED FOUR RUNS SINCE HALPIN'S WALLOP

Guarantees are rare currency in October, where the game collects its debts promptly, but Yandy Díaz issued one Sunday night with the serenity of a man reading from a bank book. "It's a great opportunity. All of us are thinking of it," the 35-year-old said of the pennant through the club's interpreter — "But it all ends tomorrow. We're going to win tomorrow."9 The affidavit behind the boast is in order: three hits Sunday atop two Saturday, safe arrival in seven of his eight October contests, and a leadoff post from which, in his manager Kevin Cash's four words, "He goes, we go." The longer ledger is in order too — the only man on the active roster remaining from the 2020 pennant-winners has now played 33 postseason games in a Tampa Bay uniform, more than any man in franchise history, and his 25 postseason hits trail only Randy Arozarena's 38 and B.J. Upton's 27 on the club's all-time roll, all atop a season of .293, 22 home runs and 85 driven in across a career-high 154 games.9

The men he proposes to dismiss are, at present, struggling to furnish a rebuttal. Since Petey Halpin's bat-flipping blow turned the third game — the high-water mark of Cleveland's October, as readers of this paper's Friday number will recall — the Cleveland bats have produced four runs in two games, yesterday's lone tally arriving on a groundout; the club neither leaves the yard nor manufactures by other means, and the round has tilted accordingly.10 Their manager's answer to the guarantee was not a counter-boast but a position report: "We don't have another choice."9

THE PROPRIETOR'S DESK: 'YOU WON'T BE SO CHEERY WHEN YOU LOSE FOR ANOTHER TWO-AND-TWENTY YEARS,' A RETIRED SCRIBE TOLD ATTANASIO AT HIS FIRST MILWAUKEE INTERVIEW IN 2004 — TWO-AND-TWENTY YEARS LATER, TO THE AUTUMN, THE MAN HOLDS A PENNANT

When Mark Attanasio was introduced as the Brewers' new owner on October 4, 2004, a long-retired reporter in the front row of his first media scrum offered a prophecy: "You won't be so cheery when you lose for another 22 years!" Twenty-two years had then passed since Milwaukee's only World's Series appearance, in 1982. Twenty-two years later, to the very autumn, Attanasio stood on the Dodger Stadium grass while his club celebrated a pennant, and permitted himself the obvious arithmetic: "Here we are, 22 years later."11

The intervening ledger rewards inspection. The front office he purchased counted about 130 full-time souls; it now counts more than 400. His first club played to .500 — Milwaukee's first non-losing season in 13 years — a 26-year postseason famine ended in 2008, and the last nine seasons have brought eight Octobers, six division flags and three pennant rounds, culminating in this year's franchise-record 103 victories and an 18-6 September.11 The owner confesses the summit frightened him more than the climb: "I was more nervous than I have ever been because this could actually happen," he said of the fourth game. "It's the first time I felt pressure — real pressure. And fortunately, our guys delivered." His president of business operations, the Milwaukee-born Rick Schlesinger, supplied the creed of the regime — ownership as "a public trust," the club "really, effectively, owned by the fans" and Attanasio its steward — and the Herald, which keeps a skeptical eye on the proprietor class as a body, notes without irony that this is precisely the manner of stewardship it recommends to the other 29.11

THE APPRENTICE DESK: 'WE HIT UNTIL OUR HANDS WERE BLEEDING' — PRATT, A SHORTSTOP OF TWO-AND-TWENTY RAISED ON NIGHTLY BATTING IN A MISSISSIPPI YARD, CARRIES A .310 OCTOBER INTO THE WORLD'S SERIES

The pennant-winners' shortstop is a rookie who turned 22 during his first big-league season and plays the position as though he has held it for a decade. Cooper Pratt batted .281 with a .350 on-base mark across 74 games after his summons to Milwaukee, and has raised the figure to .310 through the club's October — an apprenticeship conducted in the loudest classrooms the game offers.12

The making of him was nightly and unglamorous. His father Russell, a college player at Utah who runs a youth baseball concern in northern Mississippi, had Cooper and his elder brother Ozzie — later of Ole Miss — hitting nearly every evening, no days off granted or requested. "We hit until our hands were bleeding," the young man reports, adding with candor, "I hated it for the longest time." His mother Heidi played college softball before becoming a family physician; the household's verdict on his temperament is unanimous. "He's so level, so steady. Never too up, never too down," says the father. Milwaukee took him in the sixth round of the 2023 draft and paid above the slot to sign him, and its infield coach Matt Erickson now suspects "a really high ceiling as a defender that maybe he didn't even know about." The apprentice's own summation wants no ornament: "This has always been my dream."12

The forward ledger is one line with a continent of weight in it: the sixth game of the American round tonight at 8:08 under the dome at St. Petersburg, a seventh there Tuesday only if Cleveland insists, and the World's Series opening Friday, the 23rd, at Milwaukee against whichever club survives.2 One side has promised the matter concluded; the other climbed out of this same hole only a round ago. The Herald ventures no guarantee of its own save the standing one — that tomorrow's number will carry the full account, pennant or stay of execution. Until the morrow's dispatch, keep your scorecards near and your enthusiasms nearer.
