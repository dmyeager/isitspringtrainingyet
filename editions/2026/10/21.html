Volume I · No. 105 · Wednesday, the Twenty-First of October, Two Thousand and Twenty-Six

No contests this day; the pennant races rest.

2 days until the first game of the 122nd World's Series — the Rays at the Brewers, eight o'clock Friday evening at Milwaukee (Friday, the 23rd of October, 2026).

News Around the League

THE ALIGNMENT DESK: WINNING IN SIX PAYS ITS DIVIDEND IN REST — RASMUSSEN POINTED TOWARD A THIRD STRAIGHT FIRST GAME, PERALTA'S DAY THE ONE RIDDLE LEFT ON THE TAMPA SLATE, AND EVERY ARM OF BOTH STAFFS REPORTS FRESH FOR FRIDAY

Yesterday's number set out how fat with rest Milwaukee would come to the opener — a full week off since its sweep of the Dodgers concluded last Thursday, its ace eleven days between hostile innings. The fuller ledger shows Tampa Bay scarcely behind. By settling the pennant in six games the Rays were spared the all-hands seventh that empties a pitching staff, and bought themselves an extra day besides.3

Drew Rasmussen last worked Sunday evening in Cleveland, which leaves him his accustomed four days to Friday night; he opened the division round and the pennant round alike, and nothing in the club's conduct suggests the World's Series will begin any differently. Asked after Monday's clinching for his program, the right-hander offered a working man's itinerary: "Throw a bullpen and figure out what game I get to start."3 The genuine riddle is Freddy Peralta, most valuable man of the American round. Four days' rest would serve him perfectly well for Saturday's second game — in Milwaukee, against the club that raised him — but the Rays have made a quiet habit this month of starting him under their own roof, and a third-game assignment Monday at St. Petersburg would still permit him two starts should the argument run long. "We'll think about that in a few days," said the president of baseball operations, Erik Neander.3

The bookkeeping behind them is equally kind. Bryan Baker, who has finished every one of Tampa Bay's seven October victories with seven scoreless innings and worked three straight nights to close out Cleveland, now collects a holiday; Griffin Jax has not pitched since Thursday and Nick Martinez since Friday, so both report rested for the early-series bullpen errands the Rays favor before the rotation comes back around.34 Milwaukee's late innings are no less fortified: its relief corps stands 7 wins against 1 defeat with a 1.33 figure this postseason — every decision in the club's eight games has belonged to the bullpen — and Trevor Megill, Aaron Ashby and DL Hall have thrown 23 1/3 scoreless innings between them while holding batters to a .096 average.4 Two staffs, fully wound. Friday releases the spring.

THE INFIRMARY DESK: THREE-AND-TWENTY LIVE PITCHES AFTER FOUR SILENT MONTHS — WOODRUFF PRESSES HIS CLAIM ON A ROSTER DUE FRIDAY MORNING, WHILE ACROSS THE FIELD McCLANAHAN'S OCTOBER IS OVER BEFORE IT BEGAN

Brandon Woodruff, the longest-tenured man on the Milwaukee roll, threw 23 live pitches at American Family Field on Tuesday to the first string itself — Jackson Chourio, Brice Turang, William Contreras and Christian Yelich, with Jake Bauers stepping in when the count wanted filling — and sat at 90 to 92 miles the hour, facing hitters for the first time in nearly four months.5 He has not pitched in a game since the 4th of July, when the surgically repaired shoulder complained a second time and season-ending surgery was fixed for August. Then the unexpected: the shoulder healed, the surgeon blessed a comeback, and five weeks of throwing carried him to bullpen mounds between division-round games and again at Los Angeles during the pennant round. The schedule cooperated at every step, and now the last door stands ajar: the Series roster is due Friday morning.5

The decision is thornier than sentiment. By the league's rules a man injured during a postseason series may be replaced only for "an acute, non-chronic injury or ailment," as a report from Ken Rosenthal of The Athletic set out Tuesday — and whether a fresh complaint from a twice-angry shoulder would qualify is exactly the apprehension in Milwaukee's councils. A wrong guess could leave the club a man short for the length of the Series. "Still working through our meetings," said the president of baseball operations, Matt Arnold.5 The pitcher himself pleads his case and grants the other side of it in the same breath: "I'm not going to be mad either way... All I could do was put myself in a position to be an option." And the larger ledger, in his own words: "Obviously, the competitor in me wants to be suited up for our first World Series in 44 years... This city is buzzing, man. This is about this organization, this city, and trying to win its first World Series."5

Tampa Bay's infirmary page is shorter and sadder: Shane McClanahan is ruled out for the postseason entirely, though the club expects him ready when spring comes. Otherwise both wards stand empty — a Series between the two best records in baseball, played at something close to full strength.4

THE GAUNTLET DESK: CLEVELAND DREW MILWAUKEE A MAP — FASTBALLS UPSTAIRS FOR CAMINERO, ELEVEN STRIKEOUTS IN FIVE GAMES — BUT THE MOST HEATER-PROUD ROTATION THE INSTRUMENTS HAVE EVER COUNTED MUST TEST IT AGAINST THE MAN WHO SLUGGED .882 ON HIGH FIRE ALL SUMMER

The central duel of this World's Series was drafted in the one just ended. Cleveland's pitchers attacked the Rays with four-seam fastballs 37.8 percent of the time in the pennant round, and fed Junior Caminero in particular a diet of them pitched higher than in any month of his career. For five games it worked handsomely: 4 hits in 20 trips with 11 strikeouts — he had never before struck out more than 8 in any five-game span — and of 26 four-seamers shown him out of the strike zone he chased fully half, at an average height of 3.70 feet off the ground. His swing-and-miss rate on pitches outside the zone, 37.5 percent across the season, has jumped to 53.5 this October.6 The answer, when it came, rang off the left-field foul pole in Game 6 and carried the Rays to the Series, as yesterday's number told at length.

Here is the paradox Milwaukee must now price: by the season's full ledger the high fastball is precisely the pitch Caminero punishes best. His flat, level stroke and the speed of his bat keep him on plane with elevated heat; against four-seamers at the belt or above he hit .368 and slugged .882, damage exceeded by one man in baseball, and his .641 slugging against the pitch overall stood among the ten best in the game.6 The Guardians bet that October nerves would beat the summer's arithmetic, and for five games they were right.

And no club is better armed to re-run the experiment — or more committed by temperament. Milwaukee's starters threw 41 percent four-seamers this season, the highest allotment in the majors (Tampa Bay's own starters, by instructive contrast, threw fewer than 25), and struck out more batters with the pitch than any rotation in the whole of the pitch-tracking record, which reaches back to 2008 — nearly a hundred clear of the next club. Jacob Misiorowski alone threw the four-seamer 63 percent of the time at an average of 100.6 miles the hour, and 166 of his 252 strikeouts finished on it.7 The club's pitching instructor, Chris Hook, states the doctrine without embroidery: "It's either the pitcher's in charge, or the hitter's in charge... 'Hey man, I'm going to bully you.' That's that edge we're looking for."7 High fire against the game's best high-fire hitter, with the championship of the world on the table: the Series could be decided a letter's width above the strike zone.

THE CHALK DESK: NOT ONE UPSET ALL OCTOBER — THE BETTER RECORD HAS CARRIED EVERY SERIES OF THE BRACKET, AND A MILWAUKEE TITLE WOULD CLOSE A POSTSEASON TO FORM AS NO AMERICAN SPORT HAS MANAGED UNDER ITS PRESENT RULES

The bracket has behaved all month with a discipline October rarely shows. Every series so far has gone to the club with the better regular-season record; the one lower seed to advance — the sixth-seeded White Sox over the third-seeded Astros in the wild-card round — in fact owned the better ledger, 84 wins against 81. The final it has produced, each league's best record with 103 and 98 wins respectively, is only the sixth such meeting of the Wild Card era, which dates from 1995.8

The wider books make the run rarer still. Since the four major American leagues settled into their present playoff shapes — this game in 2022, the basketball league in 2021, the football league in 2020, the hockey league in 2014 — not one postseason among them has gone entirely to the better record. Some underdog always collects: a year ago it was the Dodgers, with one win fewer, over Toronto in the Series.8 Should Milwaukee finish what its 103 wins began, the 2026 bracket would be the first in any of the four sports to run true from first pitch to last.

The Herald, which has spent a full summer arguing that 162 games constitute the sounder tribunal, declines to call this dullness. When the best teams of April through September are also the last two standing in October, the proper word is not chalk but justice — and the two clubs in question, it happens, are the two that have never once been champion. Form and novelty, for one week at least, pull the same oar.

THE TIMELESS TICKET DESK: A THOUSAND DOLLARS IN 2015 BOUGHT 'THE BEST AVAILABLE SEAT' AT ANY GAME TO COME — SEVERAL HUNDRED OF THE FAITHFUL CALLED THE NOTE FOR THE SERIES, WERE SHOWN THE FOURTH DECK, AND HAVE NOW BEEN MADE WHOLE

In 2015, with the club going nowhere in particular, Milwaukee sold a promotion called the Timeless Ticket: one thousand dollars for admission to any single game in all the future, redeemable per the offer's own language "for the best available seat locations, subject to ticket availability," with the right to buy three further seats at retail alongside. The purest buyers were buying exactly one thing — this week. Now the Series has come to Milwaukee for the first time since 1982, several hundred holders have presented their notes, and the redemption did not match the prospectus: a form offering only 400-level perches or standing room, with no saying even which game.9

Danny Fochesato of Milwaukee, who bought his on his 27th birthday against precisely this occasion, recalls the selling pitch — priority just behind the season-ticket holders, "anything the season-ticket holders don't have, you'll have dibs at it for the World Series" — and notes that in 2018, when the club came within a game of the Series, the same ticket would have fetched him seats in the 100 level behind the plate. This time he bargained his way to three fourth-deck seats for Saturday and one lower seat in the right-field corner for Friday, then stood in the public sale and watched better locations offered to all comers.9 "I immediately was like, 'No, that's bogus,'" he said, and this paper, whose sympathies in any contest between the ledger and the bleacherite are a matter of record, finds the verdict sound: a promise sold to the faithful in 2015 ought to trade at full face in 2026.

To the club's credit, the business president Rick Schlesinger conceded the error in plain words — "we did not live up to the spirit of the program" — and the amends go past apology: every holder who applied by the deadline keeps the World's Series tickets and retains the Timeless Ticket itself, to be redeemed for another game in any season to come.9 The note, in other words, pays out twice. The Herald records the resolution with approval and a moral: never sell the future short to the people who believed in it earliest.

Two idle days stand between the pennants and the proof, and the paper intends to spend them exactly as the clubs will: studying the other fellow. The particulars hold as published — Friday the 23rd and Saturday the 24th at Milwaukee, eight o'clock Eastern both evenings, before the argument removes to St. Petersburg on Monday the 26th.2 The rosters are due Friday morning, the rotations remain officially unspoken, and somewhere in Milwaukee a man with a thousand-dollar ticket eleven years old is deciding what to wear. Until the next dispatch, keep your scorecards near and your enthusiasms nearer.
