Volume I · No. 110 · Monday, the Twenty-Sixth of October, Two Thousand and Twenty-Six

No contests this day; the pennant races rest.

0 days until the third game of the 122nd World's Series — Gasser against Peralta under the Tropicana roof, eight o'clock this very evening (Monday, the 26th of October, 2026).

News Around the League

THE RESUMPTION DESK: AN IDLE SUNDAY SPENT AT ALTITUDE, AND THE ARGUMENT RECONVENES TONIGHT UNDER THE ST. PETERSBURG DOME — OF THE SEVEN-AND-FIFTY CLUBS THAT EVER LED A SERIES TWO GAMES TO NONE, SIX-AND-FORTY FINISHED THE BUSINESS

No baseball was played yesterday, and none was missed: Sunday belonged to the airlines, the Brewers flying south under family colors (as this paper reported) and the Rays returning home to reckon what went wrong in Milwaukee. The reckoning resumes tonight at eight o'clock Eastern at Tropicana Field, and the arithmetic the Rays carry into it is unfriendly. Of the 57 clubs that ever took a World's Series lead of two games to none, 46 went on to take the championship3 — though the optimists of Tampa Bay may answer that 16 clubs have climbed out of that very hole6, and that both of Milwaukee's wins were had by a single run.

The pitching assignment is the one this paper set out Sunday: Freddy Peralta, the exile, against the club that raised him — and opposite him Robert Gasser, Milwaukee's left-hander, whose own story is told below. Peralta's late-season ledger remains the best argument the Rays have: 7-0 with a 0.89 earned-run average over his last ten appearances, and a 1.45 mark in seven starts under his home roof since the July trade that brought him.3 Gasser answers with 2-0 and a 1.51 figure across eight appearances since late August, and a single run allowed in nine October innings.3

One Milwaukee footnote persists: Brandon Woodruff, whose 50-50 case this paper followed onto the roster on Friday, has yet to throw a pitch in the Series — his last appearance in any game remains July 4.3

THE ROOF DESK: THE HURRICANE TORE IT OFF, THE CITY PAID TO PUT IT BACK — AFTER EIGHTEEN YEARS THE WORLD'S SERIES RETURNS TO TROPICANA FIELD, A BALLPARK LATELY REBUILT AND LATELY FULL

Tonight's game is the first World's Series baseball played at Tropicana Field since 2008, and that the building stands ready to receive it is a civic feat worth a paragraph of its own. In the fall of 2024 the hurricane Milton tore the roof clean off the dome. The city of St. Petersburg, bound by contract to repair what the storm had wrecked, voted nearly 60 million dollars to the work — over the doubts of the club's own leadership, who questioned whether it could be done at that price or in time — and baseball returned under the new roof on April 6 of this year.4 The mayor, Ken Welch, calls the building "a great symbol of resilience"; the club's chief executive, Ken Babby, pronounced it "a great American comeback story" when the gates reopened.4

The comeback has found its audience. The Rays have opened the upper deck for October and drawn an average of 37,697 across five postseason dates, winning four of the five4 — and the house records one debt still outstanding: in the only other Series the building ever held, Tampa Bay won but a single home game, the second of the 2008 set against Philadelphia.4 The tenants would consider three wins in three nights a handsome way to square the account.

THE PATRIMONY DESK: MILWAUKEE DRAFTED A GASSER IN 1984 AND NEVER GOT HIM TO A MOUND — TWO-AND-FORTY YEARS LATER HIS SON TAKES THE BALL FOR THE SAME CLUB IN THE WORLD'S SERIES

The Brewers drafted a Gasser once before. In 1984 they selected Jim Gasser, a hitter by trade whom the club proposed to convert to pitching; he never threw for them, was drafted again by the Giants in 1986, and an elbow injury closed the book before a professional chapter could be written.5 Tonight, 42 years after Milwaukee first called the family name, his son Robert starts the third game of the World's Series for that same club.

The son's road this season was its own trial: left off the Opening Day roster, sent down to Nashville with a balky arm and no feel for his pitches, and recalled only after the feel returned. It has returned emphatically. His two October starts came in the enemy's parks at San Diego and Los Angeles, and across those nine innings he has surrendered one run — a Fernando Tatis Jr. home run — and nothing else.5

Gasser's parents will be in the seats tonight, stationed by agreement at a prudent remove from the bullpen rail, having found the proximity of earlier rounds hard on the nerves. His scouting report on himself: "I wasn't sure I was going to survive the LCS."5

THE SALVAGE DESK: PLAY CLEAN, BUILD A BRIDGE, AND REMEMBER CLEVELAND — THE RAYS' PRESCRIPTION FOR CLIMBING OUT OF A TWO-GAME HOLE

The Rays spent the idle day writing prescriptions, and the first is the simplest. "We've got to play clean versus those guys," said the left fielder Chandler Simpson6 — an echo of the sermon Junior Caminero delivered on his feet in the Game 2 dugout, which this paper recorded yesterday. The club that beat itself least from April to September has beaten itself twice in October, and knows it.

The second prescription concerns the middle innings. The ends of the bullpen are sound — Bryan Baker closing, Cam Booser from the left side — but the bridge between starter and finisher has leaked badly: Kevin Kelly has allowed eight hits in four October innings, Tyler Wells stands charged with five runs on six hits, and Ian Seymour threw the pitch Sal Frelick carried to dead center Saturday.6 Unless Peralta works deep tonight, somebody in that middle company must become dependable. The manager Kevin Cash may hold Griffin Jax back to start or carry the bulk of Game 4, which would line up Nick Martinez, Drew Rasmussen and Peralta again for whatever games follow6 — a plan drawn without Shane McClanahan, lost to the postseason with forearm tightness.3

The third prescription is memory. These same Rays trailed Cleveland two games to one in the league series and won it6; the hole is deeper now, but the shovel is familiar.

THE HAGGLER'S DESK: A BOY OF EIGHTEEN ASKS WHETHER A MILLION DOLLARS SHOULD STAND BETWEEN THE BREWERS AND A HALL OF FAMER — THE CLUB PAID UP, THE MAN NOW DENIES SAYING IT, AND EIGHT YEARS ON THE LEDGER LEANS HIS WAY

Every World's Series surfaces an origin story, and Milwaukee's second baseman owns the best-disputed one of this edition. In 2018 the Brewers drafted Brice Turang 21st overall out of Santiago High School in Corona, California, and the two sides haggled over the signing bonus with the boy holding a scholarship to Louisiana State as his leverage. By the account Bob Nightengale published in USA Today last May, Turang asked the general manager David Stearns whether the club would truly let a million dollars stand between it and a Hall of Famer.7

Here the testimony divides. Matt Arnold, then on the call and now president of the club's baseball operations, swears he heard the line. Turang remembers asking about the college money and nothing grander — and when Arnold suggested the agent had fed his client the flourish, Turang denied that too: "I never said that, either!"7

Whatever was said, the investment has matured. Turang's batting line has climbed from a .585 OPS in 2023 to .816 this season, with 21 home runs and 100 runs batted in; he took home both the Gold Glove and the National League's Platinum Glove in 2024 and is a finalist again.7 The Hall of Fame withholds judgment, as is its custom, but the haggler has long since covered the million.

A day of rest in October is a contradiction the calendar insists upon, and yesterday was one: no pitch thrown, no run scored, the whole argument suspended at two games to none while the principals changed addresses. Tonight it resumes indoors in Florida, and the particulars ahead are verified and as follows: the third, fourth and fifth games at Tropicana Field tonight, Tuesday and Wednesday, the Series returning to Milwaukee on Friday should a sixth game be required, and the seventh — should the argument run its full length — falling on Saturday, the 31st of October, All Hallows' Eve.2 The Rays need win only enough to get the trunks repacked; the Brewers, two wins from the first championship in the whole of their franchise's history, would prefer the luggage stay where it is.3 Until the morrow's dispatch, keep your scorecards near and your enthusiasms nearer.
