News Around the League

THE INK DESK: QUEENS SIGNS ITS SUNRISE — YOUNG EWING, BUT TWO-AND-TWENTY, TAKES EIGHT YEARS AND FIVE-AND-NINETY MILLIONS, THE GRANDEST WAGER THE METS HAVE EVER LAID ON A MAN NOT YET ENTITLED TO ARGUE HIS WAGE

The quietest Saturday of the winter produced its loudest signature in Queens. By the account of a source, the Mets agreed yesterday with the center fielder A.J. Ewing on an extension of eight years and $95 million, with a club option for a ninth year, 2035, at $25 million — the club not yet confirming. It is the largest contract the franchise has ever written for a man still shy of arbitration, surpassing the six years and $55 million David Wright signed in 2006, and it binds to the organization, through at least 2034, a player who one year ago had yet to take a big-league at-bat.3

The season that earned it was no masterpiece, which is rather the point. Ewing, 22, an Ohio boy taken in the fourth round of the 2023 draft, came up in May, prospered, then fell into a midsummer slump deep enough to swallow a lesser temperament; he climbed out to the tune of an .879 on-base-plus-slugging over his final 25 games, and the club's evaluators marked the adjustment more admiringly than they would have marked an unbroken ascent. The full ledger reads .252/.324/.376 with 11 home runs and 25 stolen bases — a club rookie record — beside center-field work good enough to stand among the season's best arguments for him, and a ninth-place finish in the league's Rookie of the Year balloting on a club that placed three men among the vote-getters, his closest friend Carson Benge the runner-up. One official allowed that "all the arrows pointed in the right direction," and called the young man "the type of person you want to invest in"; the player, for his part, is said to have expressed a sincere wish to live and play his years in New York.3

The instrument itself deserves a paragraph, for it is the small-market club's tool in the big-market club's hand. The pre-arbitration extension — the Rays' and Brewers' habitual economy, the method by which Atlanta bound its whole core a decade ago — trades ceiling for certainty in both directions: the club buys a possible star's twenties at a discount, and the man guarantees his fortune, about $11.9 million a year, against every cruelty the game can inflict. The Mets have rarely bothered, Wright and Reyes in the mid-2000s and Niese and Lagares in the decade following being nearly the whole of the file; that they bothered now says what they think of Ewing. The outfield of Ewing, Benge and Juan Soto stands under club control through 2031 at the least, Ewing and Soto far longer, and the wire already wonders whether Benge, Nolan McLean or Christian Scott is handed the next pen.34

THE EXCHANGE DESK: THE BAY MEN WILL LISTEN ON THEIR WORKHORSE — WEBB, THIRTY AS OF WEDNESDAY, IS PLACED IN THE SHOP WINDOW ONE DAY AFTER THESE COLUMNS HAD SAN FRANCISCO SHOPPING FOR ARMS TO STAND BEHIND HIM, AND THE AMERICANS TAKE CALLS ON TWO YOUNG OUTFIELDERS

The reader of yesterday's columns met the Giants hunting rotation help behind Logan Webb and Landen Roupp, their only two certainties. This morning one of the certainties is in the window. By the account of FanSided's Robert Murray, San Francisco is now "open to listening" to trade offers for Webb — a reversal from the summer, when the club would entertain no such conversation at the deadline. The right-hander turned 30 this past Wednesday and is midway through a five-year, $90 million extension that runs to 2028; what a buyer would be buying is the steadiest freight-horse in the business, no man in the game having thrown more innings these past five seasons — 980 and two-thirds of them — at a 3.40 earned-run average with three top-six finishes in the Cy Young balloting, the runner-up's place of 2023 among them. The past season was the lightest of his prime — knee bursitis and a blistered finger held him to a 4.31 figure over 160 and two-thirds innings — but the signature virtues held, the walks still scarce and the ground balls still better than half of all contact for a seventh consecutive year. He is, moreover, the home product entire, a Northern California boy drafted by the club in the fourth round of 2014, which is precisely the kind of man a fan base does not forgive a front office for selling.4

In the Bronx the telephone rings on the young rather than the old. The steward Brian Cashman, by way of The Athletic, allows that rival clubs "are asking a lot about" the outfielder Spencer Jones, with inquiries on Jasson Domínguez besides — whether the Americans are merely listening or actively selling, the wire cannot say. Jones, 25, is penciled toward the center-field post after a rookie year that closed at .254/.312/.459 with nine home runs over his final 55 games, the ball leaving his bat with an authority — a 52.6 percent hard-hit rate, a barrel rate of 19.8 — that the league's instruments rank among the elite. Domínguez, 23, is the harder case: a shoulder-marred season spent largely in the minors, a .225 average in 57 big-league games, and no evident path past Jones, Bellinger and Judge. But the Herald's island correspondence supplies the counter-argument, for the switch-hitter is at this hour batting .348/.439/.609 through 57 plate appearances for the Águilas Cibaeñas — the selfsame Eagles whose command Francisco Cervelli assumed on Thursday, as these columns recorded Friday — and a man restoring his value under a new manager's eye is exactly the sort the callers keep calling about.4

THE ANGELENO QUESTION: A PHENOM OF ONE-AND-TWENTY PRESSES AT THE GARDEN GATE — DE PAULA'S SEPTEMBER HAS LOS ANGELES WEIGHING WHETHER HERNÁNDEZ'S LAST GUARANTEED YEAR IS BETTER SPENT IN SOMEBODY ELSE'S OUTFIELD

The Dodgers' winter has an examination beneath its grand pursuit, and the wire's Los Angeles desk set it out Saturday: what to do with left field. Under the standing arrangement Teoscar Hernández keeps the post through the final guaranteed season of his three-year, $66 million contract — $14.5 million for 2027, with a $15 million club option or a $6.5 million buyout to follow — but Josue De Paula, reckoned by MLB Pipeline the fourth-best prospect in the game, spent September making the arrangement look less than settled. Called up directly from Double-A Tulsa at 21 to stand in for an ailing Shohei Ohtani, the young man went 10 for 31 and reached base in half his plate appearances across 11 games, hardly blinking at the altitude; the bat, the club is persuaded, plays now. The glove is the open question — he logged all of six big-league innings in left, and the front office wants another winter's progress from it.5

The arguments stack neatly on either side. For a sale: the open market is thin on right-handed-hitting outfielders, Hernández closed the season batting .343 with a .980 on-base-plus-slugging over his final 27 games, and a club that means to play its young men — the general manager Brandon Gomes professing himself uncompelled but adding, "as far as due diligence, we'll always listen" — cannot also pay $14.5 million for the post the young man wants. Against it: the lineup leans left-handed without him, the glove behind De Paula is unproven, and the clubhouse has already lost Miguel Rojas to retirement — his utility post filled only Friday by the Lewis purchase, as yesterday's columns recorded — and Kiké Hernández to the open market, which makes a veteran of Teoscar's standing worth more than his slash line. The Herald files the question where the wire leaves it: open, and worth watching through the Winter Meetings.5

THE SKUBAL REGISTER: THE QUEUE BEHIND THE GREAT LEFT-HANDER GROWS RESTLESS — SEVEN CLUBS PRESS UPON PERALTA, WHO MAY NOT WAIT FOR THE VERDICT AFTER ALL, WHILE THE METS ARE RUNG ON VIENTOS AND THE FRANCE INTEREST ACQUIRES A TERM

The register these columns keep on the winter's grandest courtship stands this morning where yesterday's edition left it — the Mets' reported offer of nine years and very nearly $400 million still the only hard figure in the file — but the wire allows that the biggest free-agent story of the offseason "could soon have a resolution," and it is the queue behind the great man that supplies the Sunday news. The Athletic's Ken Rosenthal and Will Sammon count seven clubs now pursuing Freddy Peralta — the Rays, who "remain seriously engaged" on the man they remade, with the Pale Hose, the Cubs, the Friars, the Canadians and two mystery establishments besides — and report that Peralta may sign before Skubal decides at all, which would upend the orderly procession the insider Jon Paul Morosi has described, with Peralta and Michael King waiting politely upon the verdict. The right-hander's 2026 explains the crowd: a 5.01 figure across 21 starts in a Mets uniform, a midsummer trade to Tampa Bay, then an 0.79 over his final six starts of the regular season and the Most Valuable Player's honors of the league championship series — as thorough a restoration as the year produced.4

The Metropolitans' periphery, meanwhile, keeps its own column moving. The club tendered Mark Vientos his contract Friday, but Sammon reports rival clubs have been calling on the first baseman all the same, his 81 OPS+ across 87 games having dimmed his claim on the post; and the Ty France interest these columns carried yesterday now has a term attached, The Athletic reporting that France — a 139 OPS+ this past season, and one of the finest gloves at the position — seeks a contract of three years. A club that extended its center fielder Saturday, covets the market's best pitcher, and shops for a first baseman besides is a club conducting its winter at full orchestra, and the register stays open.4

THE PACIFIC DESK: THE SAWAMURA MAN CALLS ON THE SECOND CITY — ITOH, LATELY OF THE NIPPON-HAM FIGHTERS, IS SEEN IN CHICAGO WITH HIS POSTING CLOCK RUNNING, AND BOTH LOCAL ESTABLISHMENTS HAVE REASON TO HAVE SET THE KETTLE ON

The eastern shelf these columns have watched all week gains a destination city. Hiromi Itoh, the right-hander posted by the Hokkaido Nippon-Ham Fighters on the first of November, paid a visit to Chicago on Friday, by the account of ESPN's Jesse Rogers and Jorge Castillo — and the town offers two plausible hosts, though the wire cannot say whether the man sat with the Cubs, the White Sox, or the pair of them. The reporter Francys Romero has lately placed the South Siders' interest on the record, and their motive is written in these columns' own file: a rotation remade by the May signing and then thinned again by the Castillo sale, with the steward Getz promising continued pursuit. The North Side's motive is no fainter, the club's winter list of departed arms — Imanaga, Boyd, Gausman and the rest — having been set down in Friday's edition.4

The credentials travel well. Itoh, 29, pitched to a 3.18 earned-run average with 159 strikeouts across 172 and two-thirds innings this past season, one year after taking the Sawamura Award, the Japanese game's highest honor for a pitcher. The clock is the story's spring: a posted player has 45 days from his posting date to come to terms, so the window wound on the first of November runs only into mid-December, and every audience now is an audience under the glass. The Herald notes for the completist that this makes two arms of the Japanese leagues under active courtship in these pages — the Seibu right-hander Taira, his planned Mets video audience and his South Side admirers alike, having been recorded Friday — and the stove wants only a signature from either to open the market entire.4

THE HERALD LOWERS ITS COLORS: LANCE BROADWAY, FIRST-ROUND MAN OF 2005 WHO TRADED THE MOUND FOR THE PICTURES, IS DEAD AT THREE-AND-FORTY

The wire from Dallas brings the paper's saddest item. Lance Broadway, right-handed pitcher of the White Sox and the Mets, died Tuesday in that city at the age of 43; the county medical examiner's report, posted Thursday, gave no immediate cause. Broadway pitched his college ball at Dallas Baptist and at TCU, was taken by the White Sox in the first round of the 2005 draft, and appeared in 27 big-league games between 2007 and 2009 — and when the game was done with him, he declined to be done with performing, making his picture debut in Olympus Has Fallen in 2013 and appearing in several television series besides.6

Most men are allotted one improbable career; Broadway built himself two, and gave his name's marquee promise an honest second act. The Herald dips its masthead to a man who understood that the show goes on, and sends its sympathies to all who loved him.

A Sunday paper of signatures and shop windows: a Met of 22 signed into the next decade, the steadiest Giant listed for inquiry, and a posting clock ticking in two Chicago anterooms — the whole of it set down above. The forward ledger was opened before a word was written, per the house custom, and reads as it read yesterday: the camps' reporting dates remain unposted, so the count runs to the first rung bearing a date — the spring exhibition openers of Friday, the 19th of February, 89 mornings from this one, with Opening Night to follow on Wednesday, the 24th of March, 122 mornings off.2 The nearer columns allow no dozing: the collective agreement has 9 mornings left to live, and the bargainers return to the table on the morrow, Monday, their first sitting since Friday's rare day of motion;7 Cooperstown's committee of sixteen reports in 14; the Winter Meetings convene at San Diego with the Rule 5 drafting on December 9, 17 mornings from this one; and the writers' envelope waits for the 19th of January, 58 mornings off. Until the morrow's dispatch, keep your scorecards near and your enthusiasms nearer.
