News Around the League

THE COURTROOM DESK: THE MAN WHO CAUGHT THE PITCHES IN QUESTION CALLS THEM ORDINARY SLIDERS — NAYLOR TAKES THE STAND FOR THE DEFENSE, A COOPERATING BETTOR TELLS OF 'FOR-SURE BETS,' AND THE BOOKMAKER'S OWN DETECTIVE COUNTS EIGHT-SCORE PINGS FROM THE CLOSER'S HOUSE

The second day of witness testimony in the pitch-rigging trial of Emmanuel Clase and Luis Ortiz brought to the stand, of all people, the man who caught the pitches in question. Bo Naylor — five seasons a Guardians catcher before an August trade carried him to Milwaukee — appeared Thursday as a defense witness in federal court at New York, where the two Cleveland pitchers stand charged with conspiracy to commit wire and honest-services fraud, sports bribery and money laundering, a year on from their indictment.2 Shown the replays of pitches the government alleges were predetermined for the benefit of bettors, Naylor pronounced them common sliders off the plate. He was behind the bat for 14 of Ortiz's 16 starts in 2025, among them the June 27 game against St. Louis in which the government says the first pitch of the home third, to the Cardinals' catcher Pedro Pages, was thrown for a ball on purpose; the scouting report on the hitter, Naylor testified, called a slider low and away a "weapon." He caught besides 7 of the 18 pitches Clase is accused of rigging across 17 games from May of 2023 to May of 2025, found none of them remarkable, allowed that carrying word outside the clubhouse walls would be a "betrayal," and said he suspected no such breach by either man — indeed, that he did not know the betting houses offered wagers on single pitches until the case became public.2

The government's afternoon told the other half of the story. Edward Canario, a cooperating witness, testified that in May and June of 2023 he bet on Clase's pitches on the word of the co-defendant Luis Arismendy Paca Méndez, who was then living in Clase's house in Cleveland — wagers running upwards of $600 apiece that a given pitch would arrive slower than 94.5 mph or go for a ball or a hit batsman, placed heaviest when the word said to take Clase's first pitch of the ninth inning, whoever stood in the box. Canario paid Paca Méndez half the winnings and swallowed the losses himself; under cross-examination he allowed that his man never claimed inside information in so many words, only "for-sure bets."2 Earlier, Joel Trella, who directs the special investigations unit at the house of DraftKings, told the court his firm had counted 160 geolocation pings from betting accounts inside Clase's residence, where a government lawyer put the number of persons geolocated at 14.2

The ledger, for the reader keeping score at home: Clase is a three-time All-Star and twice his league's reliever of the year; Paca Méndez, extradited from the Dominican Republic on the 19th of October, is not a party to the present trial; and Judge Kiyo A. Matsumoto expects the proceedings to run until the 3rd of December. The Herald reports the allegations as allegations, the testimony as testimony, and reserves its verdict for the jury, which is the one panel in this story nobody has figured out how to wager on.2

THE PENSION DESK: FIVE UMPIRES OF LONG SERVICE HAUL THE LEAGUE'S RETIREMENT PLAN BEFORE THE BENCH — WEST OF THE FIVE THOUSAND GAMES SAYS HE WAS TOLD 'SUE US,' AND HAS OBLIGED

The other courtroom of the morning belongs to the arbiters. Five retired umpires — Joe West, Tom Hallion, Gerry Davis, Larry Vanover and Jerry Layne — have sued their retirement plan and its governing committee in federal court at New York, alleging their benefits were wrongly calculated and claiming additional monies under the pension law of 1974; their lawyer, Paul B. Holmes, said Thursday the sum owed the five runs in excess of $2 million.3 West, who worked a record 5,460 regular-season games between 1976 and 2021, says that when he raised the matter with the committee he was offered a modest figure and told by the league's chief employment counsel, Steven Gonzalez, "If you don't like the number, sue us." The invitation was perhaps imprudently extended. "Does Steve Gonzalez not know my history with dealing with lawsuits?" said West, who in 2021 took a $500,000 judgment for defamation off the former catcher Paul Lo Duca.3

The suit, filed the 29th of September, names the umpires' retirement plan and its committee; the umpires' union is not a party, their lawyer explaining that the organization is small and no house of pension experts. Holmes allows that some of the disputed items could be argued either way, "but in a couple of the issues it was pretty clear that they were just shorting the umpires," and says settlement talks foundered on offers that were "not very competitive." The league declines comment, citing the litigation; Judge Ronnie Abrams has set a pretrial conference for the 11th of December.3 The Herald's sympathies on matters of the purse are a matter of long record, and they extend without strain from the men who play the games to the men who called them: five officials who spent their working lives counting to four balls and three strikes now ask only that the league's arithmetic be held to the same standard.

THE LAURELS DESK: THE SILVER BAT SHORT LISTS ARE POSTED, AND AMONG THE NINE OUTFIELD NAMES STANDS A MAN OF NO HOME RUNS WHATEVER — SIMPSON OF THE .306 AVERAGE AND SIX-AND-FORTY THEFTS CHASES COMPANY WITH ROSE AND THE WIZARD

Louisville Slugger posted its Silver Slugger finalists Thursday — the honor for the best bat at each position in each league, balloted by the game's managers and coaches, who may not vote for their own men — with the winners to be read on the 17th of November, the very day the eight qualifying-offer men must answer their tenders.5 The Herald's standing rule wants no repetition by now: these are short lists, not verdicts. What wants attention is a single entry among the American League's nine outfield finalists: Chandler Simpson of the Tampa men, who this season hit no home runs at all — the only qualified hitter in the game without one — and made the list anyway, on a .306 average that stood fourth among all qualified batters behind Alvarez, Gabriel Moreno and Luis Arraez, a strikeout rate of 7.8 percent bettered by one man only, and 46 stolen bases, second-most in the game.4

Should the voters seat him, the company is select and the precedent nearly bare. Two position players have taken a silver bat in a season without a home run — Pete Rose in 1981 and Ozzie Smith in 1987 — but Rose carried 155 career home runs to the ceremony and the Wizard 13, while Simpson has never hit one in the major leagues at any date. He would be the first non-pitcher ever to win the award with no career home run whatever, joining a fraternity otherwise composed entirely of pitchers — Valenzuela, Glavine, Hershiser, Mike Hampton twice over, and lately Max Fried — and he is already better than halfway to the live-ball record for the most plate appearances in a career without a single round-tripper.4 The slap, the sprint and the silver: the deadball desk confesses a rooting interest it will not bother to conceal.

The rest of the junior circuit's lists hold steadier names. Pete Alonso, whose first Baltimore season led the league with 43 home runs and 113 runs driven in; Junior Caminero and Bobby Witt Jr., both of whom the reader met on Tuesday's Most Valuable short lists, Witt now seeking a third silver bat in succession; the rookie Kevin McGonigle at shortstop; and, among Simpson's eight outfield rivals, the ageless Mike Trout.5

THE WAGES DESK: EIGHT-AND-TWENTY MEN OF TWO YEARS' STANDING WIN THE RIGHT TO ARGUE THEIR PAY — THE SUPER TWOS ARE NAMED TO THE ARBITRATION ROLLS, A SPRING SUSPENSION COSTS ROJAS THE CUT BY TWO DAYS, AND NOBODY CAN SAY WHEN THE HEARINGS CONVENE

The arbitration rolls gained their junior class Thursday: 28 players with between two and three years of major-league service qualified as so-called Super 2s, the top 22 percent of that cohort by service time, each with at least 86 days logged this season, now entitled to argue their wages alongside the three-to-six-year men.6 The cutoff fell at 2 years, 133 days, and the last men aboard — Boston's Tyron Guerrero and Colorado's Jordan Beck — sat upon it exactly, with Milwaukee's Quinn Priester clearing by a single day. The names of note: Louis Varland, closer for the Toronto men and a finalist besides for the writers' new relief prize, as Tuesday's columns recorded; Andy Pages of the Dodgers; the left-hander Jacob Latz of the Rangers; Evan Carter, the same Texas center fielder whose mended hip this paper logged Wednesday; and Pedro Pages of the Cardinals, whose name the reader will have met already this morning in the courtroom columns, standing innocently in the batter's box of the government's exhibit.6

The hard luck of the class belongs to Philadelphia's Johan Rojas, who finished at 2 years, 131 days — two days shy of the money. He began the season under an 80-game suspension for a banned substance and ended it on the 60-day injured list after June surgery on his right elbow; the calendar did the rest. Pete Crow-Armstrong had service enough but signed his six-year, $115 million contract in March, removing himself from the exercise; and three men who would have qualified — Edouard Julien, Jared Koenig and James Outman — were dropped from 40-man rosters this week instead.6

One shadow lies over the whole ledger. The collective agreement dies on the 1st of December and a lockout is widely expected, so no man can presently say when the proposed salaries will be exchanged or the hearings sat — the rolls are posted, and the courtroom they feed has no date on its door.6

THE INSTRUCTORS' DESK: FRANCONA SUMMONS AN OLD LIEUTENANT TO MEND THE REDLEGS' ARMS — TORRES OF THE CLEVELAND SCHOOL TAKES THE CINCINNATI PITCHING CHAIR, AND THE FLUSHING MEN SEAT A SECOND HITTING COACH BESIDE THE FIRST

The appointments week — Seattle's new manager Tuesday, Sacramento's new general manager Wednesday, as these columns have recorded — descended Thursday to the coaching rooms. In Cincinnati, Terry Francona reached back to his Cleveland years and hired Joe Torres as pitching coach, the man having spent the past five seasons as the Guardians' assistant pitching coach — two of them, 2022 and 2023, on Francona's own staff — after five more in that organization's minor-league system.7 Torres, 44, was once the 10th overall pick of the 2000 draft, by the Angels, and wandered the White Sox, Dodgers, Rangers and Rockies systems before hanging up the spikes in 2013. The job wants doing: the Redlegs' staff posted a 4.83 earned-run average, third-highest in the majors, the starters at 4.97, and the club fell to 75-87 and the bottom of its division a year after playing in October. The silver in the drawer is Chase Burns, whose 15 wins stood fourth in the National League and whose 185 strikeouts ranked ninth.7

In Queens, meanwhile, the Mets have settled on a second hitting coach to sit beside the first: Johnny Washington, 42, lately the Braves' minor-league hitting coordinator, will share the duty with the incumbent Troy Snitker, by the word of a source the club has not yet confirmed.8 Washington served under the Mets' manager Andy Green once before, on Green's San Diego staffs of 2017 through 2019, and has held the lead hitting job in Anaheim besides; the lineup he inherits finished tied for 23rd in on-base-plus-slugging and tied for 20th in runs. The club has also promoted Mariano Duncan to assistant coach and brought in A.J. Ellis as its strategy coordinator, while the hunt proceeds for a farm director to replace Green himself, elevated from that post when the managing fell to him in June.8 Two coaches to one lineup is an arrangement the deadball desk regards the way it regards the two-headed calf at the county fair — with interest, and from a respectful distance — but the Herald concedes the arithmetic: a lineup tied for 23rd can hardly claim one voice was working.

The national game spent Thursday largely before the bench — its pitches weighed by a jury in one New York courtroom, its pensions reckoned for another — and the Herald observes that of all the parties lately asking the law to settle baseball questions, the only men on the docket who ever got a call right for a living are the five umpires. The schedule ledger, consulted at first light as always, reads as it read yesterday: the first ink of 2027 remains the spring exhibitions of Friday, the 19th of February, now 105 mornings from this one, with Opening Night itself 138 mornings off, on Wednesday, the 24th of March.1 The nearer docket thickens agreeably: the writers begin reading their award verdicts Monday, the rookies called first; the 17th has become a double appointment, the eight qualifying men to answer their tenders and Louisville Slugger to read its silver-bat winners the same day;5 and the collective agreement has 25 mornings left to live — the arbitration clerks, the reader will note above, are already penciling their calendar in the subjunctive.6 Until the morrow's dispatch, keep your scorecards near and your enthusiasms nearer.
