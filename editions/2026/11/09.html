Volume I · No. 124 · Monday, the Ninth of November, Two Thousand and Twenty-Six

No contests this day; the hot stove burns bright.

135 days until Opening Night of the 2027 season — the first pitch of the new campaign, the clubs yet to be named (Wednesday, the 24th of March, 2027).

News Around the League

THE NURSERY DESK: THE SENIOR CIRCUIT'S PUPILS TAKE THE FALL STARS BY SEVEN RUNS TO FOUR — CONFIDAN OF THE RED STOCKINGS' NURSERY CARRIES OFF THE MEDAL, SUSANA OPENS THE EVENING AT THREE-AND-A-HUNDRED MILES AN HOUR, AND THE DECIDING RUN COMES HOME BY A LOST ART, THE SQUEEZE

The hardest-hitting young man in the desert now owns the hardware to match. Yerlin Confidan, an outfielder in the Reds' nursery who has spent the autumn striking balls harder than anyone in the developmental circuit, doubled, drew a bases-loaded walk, and was named Most Valuable of the twentieth annual Fall Stars Game on Sunday night at Salt River Fields at Talking Stick, his National League pupils prevailing over the junior circuit's by 7-4 — the showcase, ten of the game's hundred best prospects on the card, that this paper billed only yesterday morning.2

The radar did the early talking. Jarlin Susana, the Washingtons' right-hander and the Fall League's hardest thrower, opened the game with fastballs of 102.7 and 103.1 mph and finished off Zion Rose — the Royals' outfielder taken sixth overall in the summer's draft — with an 88 mph sweeper, averaging 101.2 mph through a scoreless first inning.2 Kyle DeBarge of the North Stars was the bat of the evening, 2-for-2 with a double, a walk, two runs and two stolen bases, his 104.2 mph single the hardest-struck ball of the night; in the fifth he manufactured a run entirely alone, doubling off the Dodgers' left-hander Luke Fox and breaking for third with a jump so ambitious that the pitcher simply threw the ball away.2 Welbyn Francisca, the Cleveland infielder who leads the league's hitters at .419, added two of the finest defensive plays of the night at shortstop for good measure.2

Fox, to his credit, gathered himself after the DeBarge misadventure and struck out the side — the last of his victims, caught looking at a 93.4 mph four-seamer, being Aaron Parker of the Blue Jays, who had carried off the home-run derby's crown only the night before, as these columns recorded yesterday. The decision came in the eighth: Kevin Bazzell, a Washington catcher who had just erased a base-stealer with a throw of 1.86 seconds from mitt to bag, lined a 100.3 mph double, and Justin DeCriscio of the Mission nine brought him home by the lost art itself — a safety squeeze laid so gently on the grass before the plate that he beat it out for a single. The schoolhouse, the Herald is satisfied to report, remains in good order.2

THE LABOR DESK: 'DO NOT LOCK OUT BASEBALL' — MARTINEZ OF THE THREE CY YOUNGS, AMONG THE MOST PUNISHED MEN OF THE 1994 STOPPAGE, BIDS BOTH HOUSES FIGURE IT OUT FOR THE FANS WITH TWO-AND-TWENTY MORNINGS LEFT ON THE AGREEMENT

Pedro Martinez chose the tenth annual gala of his own foundation, at Boston on Saturday, to say what the sport's two houses have spent the autumn not saying to each other. "I feel for the fans, and I know when it's good, and it's good, baseball is good right now," the Hall of Famer said, by the account of Boston's own Herald — a paper of kindred name and, on this evidence, sound judgment in whom it quotes. "So stop the crap around and get something done and don't lock out baseball for the fans." He owned to being, honestly speaking, pissed — the word is his, and this desk prints it with the century's pardon — and he addressed the appeal to both sides of the table: "Please, for the fans, both sides, figure it out, a good way to get it done, and do not lock out baseball."3

Few men living are better credentialed to give the warning. When the strike of 1994 stopped the season, Martinez was a young arm on a Montreal club that owned the best record in baseball at 74-40 and dreams of a championship that died with the schedule — and the same cancellation left him a single day short of arbitration eligibility, so that the wage his talent had earned waited another year on the calendar's cruelty. "I was one of the most punished players at that moment because I had talent, and I needed to wait until arbitration to make my money," he said. "I might be insignificant, but I lived it before. I experienced that before."3

The calendar he addresses is not a sentimental one: the collective agreement dies on the 1st of December — 22 mornings from this one — with the owners' latest offer, tendered Tuesday last, proposing to delay their salary cap's arrival to 2028 rather than to withdraw it, and the union standing where it has stood throughout. "Back then, there was a need for other things to change, and they changed for the better," Martinez said of 1994. "This time, there's no excuse." The Herald's sympathies on matters of the purse are of long record and want no restating here; it observes only that when a man with three Cy Young trophies and a plaque at Cooperstown says the fans do not deserve to be mistreated, both houses might profitably take the afternoon to listen.3

THE LAURELS DESK: FIVE NIGHTS OF HARDWARE BEGIN WITH THE ROOKIES THIS EVENING — MESSICK'S CANDIDACY CARRIES A SIX-AND-THIRTY-YEAR CLEVELAND DROUGHT, THE WRITERS VOTE A RELIEVER'S PRIZE FOR THE FIRST TIME, AND THE HERALD REMINDS THE READER THAT POLLS ARE POLLS

The silverware season proper opens tonight. The writers' association reads its Rookie of the Year verdicts this evening at 7 o'clock Eastern on the MLB Network — the first of the week's awards, long marked on this paper's calendar. The Managers of the Year follow Tuesday; Wednesday doubles the bill, the Cy Youngs and the writers' new Reliever of the Year prizes read that night while the league stages its own MLB Awards from the Chelsea Theater at the Cosmopolitan of Las Vegas, the Hank Aaron awards, the Rivera and Hoffman reliever medals, the Comeback men and the All-MLB teams among the honors, with the comedian Roy Wood Jr. and Mookie Betts holding the gavels; and the Most Valuable verdicts close the week Thursday.4

Tonight's business first. The National League's rookie case lies among three: JJ Wetherholt of the Mound City nine, whose glove at second base banked 25 outs above average; Carson Benge, the Mets' outfielder, 20 home runs and the most balanced ledger of the trio; and Sal Stewart of the Cincinnatis, whose 112 runs driven in stood third in all the game. The American League's is a two-man argument of the everyday against the every-fifth-day: Detroit's Kevin McGonigle and his .806 on-base-plus-slugging against Cleveland's Parker Messick, who threw 186 and one-third innings to a 2.56 earned-run average — work good enough that he stands among the Cy Young finalists besides, only the sixth rookie ever to finish in that balloting's top three, after Skenes, Fernández, Gooden, Valenzuela and Fidrych. Should Messick prevail tonight, he ends the second-longest rookie drought in the game: no Cleveland man has taken the award since Sandy Alomar in 1990, a wait exceeded only by San Diego's, running since Benito Santiago in 1987.4

The week's other arguments, with the Herald's standing rule beside them. MLB.com's final staff poll of late September made Pete Crow-Armstrong its unanimous choice for the National League's Most Valuable — all 38 first-place votes — and made Misiorowski and Schlittler its unanimous choices for the two Cy Youngs; a poll is a poll, its sponsor is named, and the trophies themselves are decided by the ballot alone, which is read this week and not before. Among the managers, Pat Murphy, lately champion of the world, seeks a third Manager of the Year in succession, while opposite him stand two mid-season inheritors: Don Mattingly, under whom the Phillies played to the fourth-best record in the game after a 9-19 beginning, and Chad Tracy, who steadied the Boston club to its Wild Card berth — and who, should he win, makes the Tracys, father Jim and son Chad, the first father and son ever to take any of the writers' awards. The writers vote a reliever's prize for the first time this year besides; whether their electors and the panel of old relievers who award the Rivera and Hoffman medals can agree on the same arms Wednesday night is precisely the sort of question the Herald keeps its subscriptions for.4

THE EXCHANGE DESK: THE THIRTY FRONT OFFICES REPAIR TO THE DESERT WITH NINESCORE FREE AGENTS AND MORE ON THE BOOKS — THE GENERAL MANAGERS CONVENE AT LAS VEGAS UNDER THE LOCKOUT'S SHADOW, MINDFUL THAT THE LAST SUCH NOVEMBER SIGNED AWAY NEARLY A THOUSAND MILLIONS BEFORE THE FREEZE

The winter's commerce takes a roof this week: executives of all thirty clubs gather at the Aria hotel of Las Vegas for the annual General Managers Meetings — the convening these columns flagged twice last week — with more than 180 free agents on the open market and the agents who represent them working the same corridors, a convenience the telephone winter never affords. The business sits under one long shadow. With the collective agreement expiring December 1, the trade braces for a November of unusual urgency, and the precedent is exactly five winters old: in 2021, with a lockout looming, Seager signed for ten years and $325 million, Semien for seven years and $175 million, Báez for six and $140 million, and the pitchers Scherzer, Ray and Gausman for nine figures apiece — six men and $995 million subscribed before the freeze came down, with eight more at $50 million or better in the same scramble.5

The welcome wagon carries names the Herald's readers met only this past week. Randy Flores, whose removal from St. Louis to the head of Houston's baseball operations these columns have chronicled, attends his first meetings in that chair, as does Taylor Smith, late of the Rays and the Red Sox, now atop the Athletics' operation; among the second chairs come Rob Cerfolio, promoted at St. Louis only Friday as Saturday's columns told, Cam Castro, the Athletics' new general manager out of the champions' development rooms, and Jared Banner of the Orioles, while Anirudh Kilambi of the Washingtons attends his first such meetings in the general manager's office he has held since last December.5

What the corridors will deal in is talk, and the talk has its favorite names. The Gulf Coast club will be asked all week about Drew Rasmussen, whose $20 million wage for next season would be the largest in the franchise's books — the baseball operations chief Erik Neander professes "every intention" of keeping his ace, which is what a man says in either case. Seattle's Dipoto, never bashful in the market, holds a stocked rotation and a bare cupboard of hitting, so the names of Kirby and Hancock will circulate; Abrams of the Capital nine, Marte of the Desert nine, Kwan of the Lake Erie men and Peña of the Houston nine will all be spoken of, chiefly by men who do not employ them. And the eight qualifying-offer men — Arozarena, Lowe, Suzuki, Rogers, Bauers, Morejon, Jeffers and Bednar — have until the 17th at 4 o'clock Eastern to answer their $23.15 million tenders. The industry's consensus holds that all eight decline, and history leans the same way: of the 165 men ever tendered the offer, just 18 have accepted — though the reader will note, as the agents surely do, that four of those 18 came only last winter.5

THE PANTHEON DESK: EIGHT NAMES WAIT ON SIXTEEN VOTES — THE CONTEMPORARY ERA PANEL TAKES UP BILL WHITE, FIRST BASEMAN, BROADCASTER AND PRESIDENT OF THE NATIONAL LEAGUE, WITH ITS VERDICT SET FOR THE SIXTH OF DECEMBER

The Hall of Fame's winter docket has opened its first argument. The Contemporary Baseball Era committee — the panel of 16 that weighs managers, executives and umpires — reads its results on Sunday, the 6th of December, with eight candidates on its ballot, and the columnist Anthony Castrovince spent Sunday making the case for the most varied life among the eight: Bill White, now 92, whom Cooperstown honored only last summer with the Buck O'Neil Award, a distinction seven people in all have held, for his impact on the game beyond its box scores. O'Neil himself received that honor in 2008 and his plaque in the gallery posthumously in 2022; White's partisans would prefer to see the order reversed.6

The case wants no padding, only addition. The player: thirteen seasons, a .286 average, 1,706 hits and 202 home runs, eight All-Star selections, seven consecutive Gold Gloves at first base, third in the Most Valuable balloting of 1964 and a champion that same October with the Cardinals. The broadcaster: the first Black play-by-play man ever engaged by a major-league club, hired to the Yankee air in 1971 and seated beside Phil Rizzuto for the better part of eighteen seasons, his call of Bucky Dent's home run in the playoff of 1978 among the catalogue's treasures. The executive: five years as president of the National League from 1989, the man in the chair when the league added the Marlins and the Rockies in 1993. And the road beneath it all: signed out of Hiram College by the Giants in 1953, the second Black player in the Carolina League, the desegregator of several minor-league clubs, and nearly two years in the Army besides.6

No single line of the ledger, Castrovince allows, carries Cooperstown by itself; the argument is the totality — a professional life touching parts of five decades, each room entered first or nearly first, and the door held open behind him. "Bill had no choice but to accept that job," his old teammate Bob Gibson once said of the league presidency. "Not for himself but for other people." White's own accounting was always simpler: "I demanded to be recognized for what I accomplished. Nothing more." The Herald, which keeps a standing affection for men who did several jobs well in an age that prefers one job done loudly, will have the panel's answer on the first Sunday of December.6

A Monday that opens the winter's busiest week. The writers read their first verdicts this evening — the rookies, as foretold — with the managers Tuesday, the Cy Youngs Wednesday and the Most Valuable men Thursday; the thirty front offices convene meanwhile at Las Vegas, where the league also stages its Wednesday awards theater, so that Nevada holds very nearly the whole apparatus of the game this week; and the courtroom at Brooklyn resumes its own proceedings Thursday, as Sunday's columns noted. The forward ledger, ruled open at first light as the house requires, stands unmoved: nothing on the card before the spring exhibitions of Friday, the 19th of February, 102 mornings from this one, with Opening Night 135 mornings off on Wednesday, the 24th of March.1 The 17th keeps its double appointment eight mornings hence — qualifying answers and silver bats together — the Hall's Contemporary Era panel reports on the 6th of December, and the collective agreement has 22 mornings left to live, a circumstance on which the week's plainest sermon was preached Saturday at Boston, gratis, by a man with three Cy Young trophies. Until the morrow's dispatch, keep your scorecards near and your enthusiasms nearer.
