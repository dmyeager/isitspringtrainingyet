142 days until Opening Night of the 2027 season — the first pitch of the new campaign, the clubs yet to be named (Wednesday, the 24th of March, 2027).

News Around the League

THE LAURELS DESK: RAWLINGS HANDS DOWN ITS SCORE OF GOLDEN GLOVES AND NINE MEN TAKE THEIR FIRST — THE CRIMSON HOSE AND THE DESERT NINE CLAIM THREE APIECE, THE CHAMPIONS GO TWO FOR TWO, AND WALLS SPOILS WITT'S BID FOR A THIRD

The verdict promised in Sunday's columns arrived on schedule: Rawlings handed down the 2026 Gold Gloves last evening, and for the second year running the honors read like a nursery — nine men collected the first of their careers, a near-team's-worth of new silverware.2 The Red Sox and the Diamondbacks split the laurels' lion's share at three apiece. Boston's trio: the third baseman Caleb Durbin, a first-timer who led the league's third basemen with 13 defensive runs saved in his first season in the Hub; the center fielder Ceddanne Rafaela, back to back in center and the first man to repeat there in his league since Kevin Kiermaier in 2015 and 2016; and the right fielder Wilyer Abreu, a winner for the third year running on the strength of those 28 defensive runs saved this paper remarked only yesterday — the most of any man in baseball, by the ledger and now by the trophy case too. Arizona's three: the catcher Gabriel Moreno, a two-time winner and only the second catcher ever to pair a Gold Glove with a batting title in the same season, after Joe Mauer; the shortstop Geraldo Perdomo, whose 16 outs above average finally earned the first of his career; and the right fielder Corbin Carroll, somehow a first-timer himself.2

The contests this paper flagged Sunday resolved as follows. Bobby Witt Jr.'s pursuit of a third consecutive shortstop honor fell short, and so did the challenge of Andrés Giménez: the glove went instead to Taylor Walls, who led all shortstops with 21 defensive runs saved and becomes the first Tampa Bay shortstop ever so honored — the club's first winner at any post since Kiermaier in 2019 — on the very day, the reader will savor, that his employers must rule on his contract clause.2 Steven Kwan's first candidacy in center field fell to Rafaela. Dillon Dingler kept his crown behind the plate for Detroit. Elsewhere in the junior circuit: Christian Walker took his fourth at first base and his first in Houston silks; Seattle's Cole Young, in his second season, became the club's first second-base winner since Bret Boone — whose streak began before Young was born; Cody Bellinger of the Yankees won his second, the first having come as a right fielder with the 2019 Dodgers; Tanner Bibee joined Shane Bieber as Cleveland's only pitching winners; and Ezequiel Duran gave Texas its first utility honor. The Toronto men, who tied for the most finalists in the field with five, came away with empty hands.2

In the elder league the sweetest news went north to Milwaukee, where the lately crowned champions went two for two: Brice Turang took his second glove at second base — his 23 defensive runs saved trailed only Abreu in all of baseball, and he remains the only second baseman in the club's annals to win one — and the 22-year-old Jackson Chourio, errorless the season through in left field, took his first, making them the third and fourth Brewers ever to hold the award in multiple or maiden splendor four days after holding the Commissioner's Trophy.3 Matt Olson of Atlanta won his fourth at first base; his new teammate Mauricio Dubón took the utility honor for a third time, no other man having won it even twice; Alex Bregman became the second Cubs third baseman to win one, the first being Ron Santo; Pete Crow-Armstrong repeated in center on a league-best 26 outs above average, garnish for his 40-40 season at the bat; and Nolan McLean of the Mets became the first rookie pitcher ever to win a Gold Glove — the Flushing nine's first glove at any position since Juan Lagares in 2014.2

THE HERALD LOWERS ITS COLORS: JERRY KOOSMAN, LEFT ARM OF THE MIRACLE METS, IS DEAD AT THREE-AND-EIGHTY — HE THREW THE PITCH THAT ENDED 1969 AND LEAPT INTO GROTE'S ARMS, AND NEW YORK NEVER FORGOT THE SIGHT

Jerry Koosman, perhaps the finest left-handed pitcher ever to wear a Mets uniform, died Sunday at the age of 83.4 For 12 seasons in New York, from 1967 through 1978, he was the other half of the great tandem with Tom Seaver, and the franchise's ledger still carries his name at the top of every left-handed column: 140 wins, 346 starts, 108 complete games, 26 shutouts, 1,799 strikeouts, a 3.09 earned-run average. As a rookie in 1968 he won 19 games with a 2.08 figure and finished second for the league's Rookie of the Year honors only to Johnny Bench — no disgrace in any era.4

It was October of 1969 that fixed him in the city's memory forever. With the Miracle Mets down a game to the Orioles, Koosman played the stopper in Game 2, carrying a no-hitter into the seventh inning of a 2-1 victory; three games later he went the distance in the Game 5 clincher at Shea Stadium and, as the last out settled into history, leapt into the arms of his catcher Jerry Grote — an image the game has never filed away. He added a Championship Series win over the Reds and a World's Series win besides in 1973, a 21-win season in 1976, and the hard-luck badge of a 20-loss season in 1977 carried with a 3.49 earned-run average. The Army had found him before the Mets did: he was signed for $1,600 in 1964 on the recommendation of a Fort Bliss batterymate whose father ushered at Shea Stadium — the cheapest great signing in the franchise's books. Traded to Minnesota after 1978 in the deal that brought back Jesse Orosco, he shares with that man a symmetry no other pair can claim: the two left-handers who stood on the mound for the final out of each of the Mets' two world championships. Over 19 seasons with the Mets, Twins, White Sox and Phillies he won 222 games, struck out 2,556 men, and pitched his last at 42. The club retired his No. 36 in August of 2021, making him then only the third player in its history so honored. "He was a warrior," his old teammate Ron Swoboda once said. "He played the whole game all the time."4 The Herald lowers its colors for the warrior of Shea: may every left arm that follows him in Flushing be measured against his, and may none mind falling short.

THE FAR PACIFIC DESK: SATO, TRIPLE CROWN MAN OF THE CENTRAL LEAGUE, IS POSTED AND MAY TREAT WITH ALL THIRTY CLUBS FROM THIS VERY DAY — ITOH OF THE FIGHTERS FOLLOWS HIM ACROSS, AND THE EASTERN CONTINGENT GROWS TO THREE

The grandest bat of the winter's eastern fleet has weighed anchor. Teruaki Sato, the Hanshin Tigers' slugging third baseman, was made available through the posting process Sunday, and negotiations with all 30 clubs are permitted to begin this very Monday.5 The credentials want little embroidery: the left-handed hitter took the Central League's Triple Crown this season at .306 with 39 home runs and 106 runs driven in, atop the league's Most Valuable Player award he carried home in 2025. He will play next season at 28, old enough that no restriction binds the terms he may command, with Hanshin to collect a release fee scaled to the contract he signs and a 45-day window in which to sign it. The scouts' cavils are two: a 27 percent strikeout rate that invites comparison to Munetaka Murakami — who struck out more and still hit 35 home runs as a White Sox rookie this season — and a question of where he fields, with 262 games of outfield work on his Japanese ledger if third base will not serve. The insider Jon Heyman has linked the Mets, Yankees, Dodgers and Phillies to the man; in a market thin on power hitting, the queue seems likely to lengthen.5

Behind him came an arm. The Hokkaido Nippon-Ham Fighters on Sunday officially posted the right-hander Hiromi Itoh, 29, whose six seasons read 2.92 by earned-run average across a thousand innings, and whose 2025 campaign — a 2.52 figure with 195 strikeouts — won him the Sawamura Award, Japan's answer to the Cy Young.6 This season he gave 172 2/3 innings at 3.18 while walking fewer than five men in a hundred. He joins Sato and the little Seibu right-hander Kaima Taira, whose coming passage this paper logged Saturday, in a contingent from the far shore now three strong — with more sails reported on the horizon.6

THE BAZAAR DESK: THE GATES SWING OPEN AT FIVE O'CLOCK THIS AFTERNOON — SAN DIEGO KEEPS ITS THREE LEFT HANDS AND TURNS TWO RIGHT ONES LOOSE, BOSTON RETAINS ITS SETUP MAN, AND THE DECEMBER CLOCK HURRIES EVERY BIDDER

At 5 o'clock Eastern this afternoon the exclusive courtships end and the winter market opens to all comers, precisely as this paper's ledger has been promising all week. The catalogue is set: Tarik Skubal stands the undisputed first lot, a near-lock by the trade's reckoning to pass Yoshinobu Yamamoto's 12-year, $325 million standard for a pitching contract; Michael King, whose opt-out this paper recorded Friday, ranks second; and third comes Freddy Peralta, whose season turned from a 4.99 earned-run average in a Mets uniform to a 3.25 in Tampa Bay colors after a midsummer trade, then to the Championship Series' most-valuable honors in October.7 Sato, newly posted, ranks fourth, and over the whole fair hangs the first of December, when the collective agreement expires — a deadline that five years ago drove Corey Seager, Marcus Semien, Max Scherzer and three other nine-figure men to sign before the Thanksgiving leftovers were finished. The December clock, in short, may make this a sprinting market rather than a strolling one.7

The last of the option rulings came in under the wire. San Diego will keep all three of its left-handed relievers: Yuki Matsui opted into his $6.5 million season on the heels of a 1.83 earned-run average across 54 outings, joining Wandy Peralta — whose own opt-in this ledger recorded Sunday — and Kyle Hart, whose club option was exercised; the Friars meanwhile declined their mutual options on the right-handers Lucas Giolito and Griffin Canning, and Miguel Andújar declined his own, all three joining the market. The club has until 2 o'clock Pacific today to decide a qualifying offer for the setup man Adrian Morejon, the best-performing reliever of the free-agent class by the past three seasons' ledger.8 And in Boston the Red Sox exercised their $8.25 million option on Garrett Whitlock, whose 1.72 earned-run average and 0.90 WHIP across 54 appearances made him the sturdiest bridge to the ninth inning in the league — a Rule 5 plucking from the Yankees, the reader may enjoy recalling, that has repaid Boston for six years running.9

THE APPOINTMENTS DESK: FLORES TAKES HIS CHAIR AT DAIKIN PARK THIS VERY DAY AND THE MANAGER HUNT OPENS AT ONCE — AUSMUS, THRICE A GOLDEN GLOVER IN HOUSTON SILKS HIMSELF, STANDS IN THE RUNNING

Randy Flores, whose hiring this paper reported Saturday, is introduced to the press this day at Daikin Park, and the Houston club means to waste no time on the vacancy beneath him: with the general managers convening at Las Vegas next week, the hunt for a manager to succeed Joe Espada is already under way.10 The name at the head of the speculation is a familiar one in those parts. Brad Ausmus — who caught more games than any man in the franchise's history across ten seasons, won three Gold Gloves in Houston silks, and handled the celebrated staffs of 2004 and 2005 — was in the offices at Daikin Park on Thursday, by the word of multiple sources, interviewing for the general manager's post by one account, with a chance the club now gives him a look for the manager's chair instead. The credentials cut both ways: three seasons lately as the Yankees' bench coach and close ties to the club adviser Jeff Bagwell on the one hand; a 386-422 managerial record with the Tigers and Angels on the other. He interviewed for this same chair in 2020, when it went to Dusty Baker.10

The new man's own history may widen the field. Flores spent five of his pitching years and ten of his office years in St. Louis, and two of his old teammates there — Albert Pujols and Yadier Molina — have each declared the ambition to manage in the big leagues. Molina has piloted Puerto Rico in two World Baseball Classics, won with his clubs in the Venezuelan winter league, and lately joined the Cardinals as a special assistant; Pujols managed the Dominican side in this year's Classic and led Escogido to both the Dominican league and Caribbean Series titles a winter ago. Whether the old battery of Cardinal loyalties produces Houston's next skipper, the Herald will report as the interviews proceed.10

The paper goes to press this Monday with the bazaar's gates still barred; by suppertime they stand open, and every free man in the game may talk terms with any of thirty clubs. The schedule ledger has been consulted all the same, as it is every morning, and stands white for 142 mornings more, until Opening Night on Wednesday, the 24th of March, 2027.1 But let no reader mistake a white schedule for a quiet one: the gloves came down last night, the market opens this afternoon, and the general managers pack for Las Vegas next week with the December labor clock audible in every valise.10 The Herald's winter desk is fully staffed and entirely unbothered — there is a kind of box score in a contract ledger too, if you squint. Until the morrow's dispatch, keep your scorecards near and your enthusiasms nearer.
