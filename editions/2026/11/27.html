Volume I · No. 142 · Friday, the Twenty-Seventh of November, Two Thousand and Twenty-Six

84 days until the first spring-training game (Friday, the 19th of February, 2027).

News Around the League

THE EXCHANGE DESK: THE MARKET KEEPS THE FEAST WITH ITS SHELVES HALF-CLEARED — SIX STARTERS SOLD AT FIFTY MILLIONS OR BETTER, KING STANDS ALONE AT THE HEAD OF WHAT REMAINS, SEVEN SOUND MID-ROTATION ARMS WAIT BEHIND HIM, AND THE TRADE WINDOW DRESSES WEBB, THE TEXAS DISCONTENTS AND TWO RESTLESS RAYS

The wires carried no new signature on the feast day itself, which makes this the morning for an inventory, and the wire's Thomas Harrigan has drawn one up. The month's bill of sale now runs to six starting pitchers at $50 million guaranteed or better: Skubal's record nine years and $450 million in Queens, Peralta's five and $125 million in Baltimore, Rogers' five and $120 million on the North Side, Taira's five and $80 million in Toronto, Sonny Gray's two and $55 million in the Twin Cities, and Dustin May's three and $52.5 million with the Pale Hose. Foster Griffin's three years and $48 million in Cleveland miss the threshold by a whisker, and the week's remaining purchases — Gausman to Detroit, Bubic to Cleveland, the Rockies' pair of ground-ball left-handers — stand recorded in these columns already.4

At the head of what remains stands Michael King, whom these columns have carried all week as tipped to sign before the week is out. The case for him is the ledger: a 3.15 earned-run average with 436 strikeouts across 432 innings for the Padres over the past three seasons, and 32 starts at a 3.21 this past year — whereupon he declined the last two years of his $75 million paper and walked back onto the market. Behind him waits a deep bench of mid-rotation soundness: seven unsigned men threw at least 200 innings over the past two seasons with both their adjusted earned-run and fielding-independent figures better than the league's common run — Nick Martinez, Matthew Boyd, Casey Mize, Clay Holmes, Tyler Mahle, Nick Pivetta and Michael Soroka — with Pivetta easily the best of them by the inning, though a flexor-tendon complaint swallowed much of his 2026. Ray, Bassitt, Flaherty and Imanaga cleared one bar of the two, and Brandon Woodruff's sample is small but gleaming: 110 innings in two years at a 3.11.4

The demand outruns the supply. The Cubs, the Orioles, the Jays, the White Sox and the Mets are all reckoned still shopping even after their purchases; the Astros, the Athletics, the Braves, the Snakes, the Cardinals, the Capital nine and the Giants could each use an arm; and no want is so pressing as the Padres', whose entire rotation — King, Pivetta, Ray, Mize, Buehler, Giolito and Canning — walked to the market at once. For the clubs that find the shelves too dear, the trade window dresses itself: the Orange and Black are now willing to listen on Logan Webb — a softening of the summer stance, and of a piece with the grander designs these columns noted Tuesday — while in Texas the names of deGrom, Eovaldi, Gore and the shortstop Seager all circulate as the Rangers hunt payroll relief — deGrom, Eovaldi and Seager each hold a no-trade clause, which leaves Gore, a free agent after 2027, the simplest to move. The Tampa men, for their part, insist Rasmussen stays after his $20 million option — the highest one-year wage in the franchise's history — but might yet part with McClanahan or Jax.4

THE LABOR QUESTION: FOUR MORNINGS REMAIN — THE OLD PEACE'S ONE UNDISPUTED INSTRUMENT PAYS ITS YOUNG MEN ON SCHEDULE, CROW-ARMSTRONG DRAWS A RECORD THREE MILLIONS AND A HALF FROM THE PRE-ARBITRATION POOL, AND THE POOL ITSELF SITS ON THE BARGAINING TABLE AT FIGURES MORE THAN A HUNDRED MILLIONS APART

While the principals kept the holiday, the machinery of the old agreement made its last scheduled payment. The figures of the pre-arbitration bonus pool — the $50 million instrument built into the March 2022 labor settlement to carry money to the game's best young men before arbitration can — were finalized by the league and the union midweek, and Pete Crow-Armstrong of the Cubs draws the largest sum ever paid from it: $3,548,994, past the record Paul Skenes set a year ago. The season so rewarded wants no argument — the center fielder was the unanimous Most Valuable Player of the National League at .280 with 45 home runs, 107 driven in and 41 bases stolen across all 162 games3 — and the arithmetic of the program tells its whole purpose in one line: the pool has now paid him $5,097,329 over three years, better than double the $2,282,186 his actual wages came to across the same seasons. His six-year, $115 million extension, signed in March, only begins with the new year.5

The rest of the roll reads like the autumn's honors list, which is the design working. The Milwaukee right-hander Jacob Misiorowski, the National League's Cy Young man, stands second at $3,164,848; the Bronx's Cam Schlittler, his American League counterpart, third at $3,124,038; Tampa Bay's Junior Caminero fourth and Cleveland's Parker Messick fifth. De La Cruz, Chourio, Varland and the Rookie of the Year McGonigle also cleared a million apiece. A hundred players shared the pool in all — the White Sox placed the most men with eight, while the champion Brewers' five split the largest club sum, $5,443,421 — and the cautionary entry is Skenes himself, fallen from first place to 35th and $316,248 on the strength of a down year: the pool pays the season, not the name.5

The Herald prints the ledger with its usual lean, and this week the lean is nearly a straight line: here is the one instrument of the last peace that both parties call a success, and the parties stand more than a hundred millions apart on it. The league's cap-and-floor proposal would raise the pool to $65 million next year and $75 million by 2033; the union asks $180 million next year, climbing by $15 million annually thereafter. That distance — on the mechanism everyone likes — measures the whole quarrel better than any statement either side has issued. The agreement that created the pool has 4 mornings left to live, expiring at one minute before midnight on the first of December, and the league's courthouse maneuver of Wednesday, asking a judge to bless its lockout in advance, stands as yesterday's columns recorded it.5

THE INFIRMARY WARD: THE SURGEON CLAIMS A SEASON ENTIRE — CASPARIUS, SEVEN-AND-TWENTY, LOSES HIS ELBOW LIGAMENT TO THE KNIFE AND ALL OF 2027 WITH IT, AND THE BULLPEN OF THE BOYS IN BLUE GROWS THINNER STILL

A grim bulletin from Los Angeles: the right-hander Ben Casparius underwent reconstruction of the ligament in his pitching elbow on Wednesday — Dr. Neal ElAttrache at the knife — and will miss the whole of the 2027 season. He is 27, and his 2026 was already a lost year in miniature: an 0-1 mark and a 9.64 earned-run average across five appearances with the big club, a 7.84 across 12 games at Triple-A Oklahoma City. The club will remember instead the 2025 edition — 7-5 with a 4.64 and two saves across 46 games of long, flexible relief for a championship staff.6

The loss lands on a bullpen already inventoried in these columns Tuesday: Phillips gone to Atlanta, Vesia to the open market, and the thrice-mended Robert Stephenson signed to minor-league terms as a lottery ticket behind Díaz and Scott. The arithmetic of that room now wants one arm more than it did a week ago.6

THE APPOINTMENTS COLUMN: THE BRONX HIRES AN OLD TORMENTOR FOR THE CHAIR BESIDE BOONE — HYDE, SIX SEASONS THE ORIOLES' MANAGER AND LATELY A TAMPA ADVISER, COMES TO THE YANKEES AS BENCH COACH, CHOSEN OVER HIS OWN BROTHER-IN-LAW

The Yankees have filled the chair at Aaron Boone's right hand with a man who spent six years trying to beat him from the opposite dugout. Brandon Hyde, 53, comes to the Bronx as bench coach, by sources' account to ESPN's Jorge Castillo, succeeding Brad Ausmus, who left this month to manage the Astros. The resume is a full one: six seasons and part of a seventh managing the Orioles, including the 101-win division champions of 2023 and the wild-card club of 2024, before the axe fell 41 games into 2025; a year since as an adviser in the Rays' baseball operations; and, earlier, the bench coach's post with the Cubs under Renteria and Maddon. The footnote is the stuff of a family novel: the Yankees chose Hyde over Joe Espada — his brother-in-law, the Astros' deposed manager and once the Yankees' own third-base coach — and the club still wants a hitting coach besides, James Rowson having departed to run the Giants' offense.7

THE WINTER CIRCUIT: LIVE BASEBALL NIGHTLY WHILE THE STOVE SMOLDERS — THE AUSTRALIAN LEAGUE'S WHOLE SEASON COMES TO THE LEAGUE'S OWN SCREEN FOR THIRTY DOLLARS, FOUR CLUBS AT NINE-AND-THIRTY GAMES APIECE, AND THE TALLEST MAN EVER TO PITCH FOR PAY

For the reader taking stock of a winter that may yet grow quieter still, the league's own screen offers an antipodean remedy: the Australian Baseball League's entire season — regular campaign and postseason alike — now streams on MLB.TV's MLB+ tier for $29.99, the Dominican and Mexican winter circuits riding in the same subscription. The season down under has been running since the 19th of November; its four clubs play 39 games apiece over ten weeks through the 23rd of January, with a best-of-three championship series set for the 29th through the 31st.8

The circuit's alumni book argues for the ticket: Acuña, Cristopher Sánchez, Imanaga and Caminero all passed through it on their way up, as did the Guardians' All-Star second baseman Travis Bazzana, the Australian taken first overall in the 2024 draft. And for the connoisseur of curiosities, the Sydney Blue Sox offer James Boeree, 7-foot-2 and the tallest man ever to play the professional game — summer baseball in December, visible from the armchair.8

THE SANDLOT DESK: EIGHT SURGERIES, ONE HERO, AND A TEXT THAT READS 'WELCOME TO THE NO-HIT CLUB' — A LONG ISLAND BOY OF THIRTEEN, BORN WITH A FUSED RIGHT HAND, HITS A GRAND SLAM AT COOPERSTOWN, THROWS A NO-HITTER ON HIS MOTHER'S BIRTHDAY, AND HEARS FROM JIM ABBOTT HIMSELF

A story for the morning after the feast, told at length by the wire's Anthony Castrovince. Carmine Pagano of Lindenhurst, New York, is 13, and was born with the fingers of his right hand fused — a condition it took eight surgeries, begun before he was two, to shape into three working fingers. His hero, from the time he knew anything of the game, was Jim Abbott, who pitched in the majors with one hand and threw a no-hitter for the Yankees against Cleveland in September of 1993; father and son studied the old films of the famous glove switch and practiced it together in the yard, the father buying himself a wrong-handed glove to make a fair contest of it.9

The bat was the stubborn problem — the right hand could not hold it through a swing — until a strapping accessory called the 9 Strap, built to bind a hitter's hands together through contact, solved in one winter what years of therapy could not. In August, at a youth tournament in Cooperstown, the boy worked a full count with the bases loaded and put the ball off the top of the fence and over: a grand slam, teammates pounding his helmet, the stands by his father's account become one large family. The company behind the strap heard the story and arranged a video call in September between the boy and his hero, who talked with him for half an hour about struggle and what follows it.9

Then, in October, on his mother's birthday, Carmine took the mound in a fall-league semifinal and carried a no-hitter through all five innings of a game ended early by the mercy rule — one walk, three strikeouts, not a hit. He texted the news to Abbott, who had kept in touch since the call, and the answer came back in five words the Herald prints as received: "Welcome to the no-hit club!" The paper's own sentiment desk, consulted on the holiday weekend, rules the item fully verified and entirely restorative.9

The republic fed yesterday, and the market rested with it: no new signature crossed the wires overnight, and the paper's columns turn instead to inventories — the state of the pitching shelves, the young men's pool paying out its record, and a boy on Long Island who joined the no-hit club. The forward ledger was read before any word was set, by the house custom, and stands unmoved: the camps' reporting dates remain unposted, so the count runs to the first rung bearing a date — the spring exhibition openers of Friday, the 19th of February, 84 mornings from this one, with Opening Night to follow on Wednesday, the 24th of March, 117 mornings off.2 The nearer columns: the collective agreement has 4 mornings left to live; Cooperstown's committee of sixteen reports in 9; the Winter Meetings convene at San Diego with the Rule 5 drafting on December 9, 12 mornings from this one; and the writers' envelope waits for the 19th of January, 53 mornings off. Until the morrow's dispatch, keep your scorecards near and your enthusiasms nearer.
