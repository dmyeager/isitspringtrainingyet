Volume I · No. 126 · Wednesday, the Eleventh of November, Two Thousand and Twenty-Six

No contests this day; the hot stove burns bright.

133 days until Opening Night of the 2027 season — the first pitch of the new campaign, the clubs yet to be named (Wednesday, the 24th of March, 2027).

News Around the League

THE LAURELS DESK: THRICE RUNNING — MURPHY OF THE CREAM CITY NINE TAKES THE MANAGERS' PRIZE AS NO MAN BEFORE HIM EVER HAS, AND HEARS THE NEWS FROM A HOSPITAL BED, WHILE VENABLE OF THE SOUTH SIDERS, TWO AUTUMNS REMOVED FROM A HUNDRED AND TWENTY-ONE DEFEATS, CARRIES OFF THE AMERICAN HONOR

Pat Murphy took the call of history lying down, and no man alive has better earned the posture. The Baseball Writers' Association of America read its Managers of the Year on Tuesday evening, and the National League's went where yesterday's columns weighed it: to the 67-year-old steward of the champion Brewers, who thereby becomes the first manager in either league ever to win the award three years in succession — a thing only four men had done even twice running. Murphy, who underwent back surgery in June and has lately had a hip replaced besides, accepted the honor over the television wire from a hospital bed. "I'm really grateful," he said. "I don't necessarily think I'm deserving, to be quite honest with you. It's more a matter of just keeping everything in perspective, but I'm super, super grateful." The writers suffered no such modesty: he was named on all 30 ballots — 20 firsts, seven seconds, three thirds, 124 points — with Walt Weiss of the Atlantas second at 86 and Don Mattingly of the Phillies third at 25, a single point ahead of the Friars' Craig Stammen. Murphy joins the register as the tenth manager with three such awards or more; Buck Showalter, Bobby Cox and Tony La Russa head that table with four apiece, but no collection among the ten came three Novembers running — that distinction is now Murphy's alone. The campaign beneath the trophy wants no restating: 103 victories, the best in all the game for a second consecutive year, and then the 11-2 October march that ended in the franchise's first championship.23

The American League's prize went to the resurrection man. Will Venable, who inherited a White Sox club fresh from the 121 defeats of 2024 — the most any modern nine had ever suffered — was named on 15 first-place ballots and totaled 111 points, holding off Kevin Cash of the Rays at 98 and Boston's mid-season inheritor Chad Tracy at 47, whose father-and-son particular must now wait upon some other November. In Venable's first year the South Siders improved by 19 games and still lost 102; in his second they won 84, took the league's final Wild Card berth, swept Houston from the opening round, and won the first two games of the Division Series at Cleveland before the Guardians came back for the set. He is the club's first Manager of the Year since Ozzie Guillén in the championship season of 2005, a son of the old big-leaguer Max Venable, and himself nine years an outfielder for the Padres, Rangers and Dodgers before the coaching rooms of Chicago and Boston raised him to a chair of his own. He declined, characteristically, to sit in it alone: "You can have the best front office, the best staff; you can support them the best you can. At the end of the day, it's about getting guys that want to go and compete, so I think it really starts with the players." Of the spring that started it: "This group in Spring Training developed a belief in themselves, a belief in each other, and then went out and did it."2

The evening carried a coda from the gracious side of the ledger. When the champagne flew on the 28th of October, a live microphone caught Murphy — handed the final-out ball by Jake Bauers — urging his first baseman to remember his old club, the 2023 Yankees of the selfish reputation, with a profanity attached. Within half a day, by Brian Cashman's own telling, Murphy had tracked down "the lowly New York Yankees" at their offices to say he regretted it. "He took the time on the greatest — within 12 hours, arguably some of the greatest times that he's had walking this planet Earth outside of the birth of his kids — to realize that he said something that he regretted," the Yankee general manager said Tuesday at Las Vegas. "That meant a lot to me." Murphy called Cashman "very gracious and very, very kind," and offered this by way of explanation: "I could have said the Dodgers or the Giants or whoever." The Herald files the exchange under manners, a department the sport does not always keep staffed, and notes that the week's silver bill is only half discharged: the Cy Youngs and the writers' first reliever's prize are read this very evening, with the Most Valuable verdicts to close the books on the morrow.4

THE HERALD LOWERS ITS COLORS: BOB BOONE, CATCHER OF THE QUAKERS' FIRST CHAMPIONSHIP AND MIDDLE LINK OF THE GAME'S FIRST THREE-GENERATION HOUSE, IS DEAD AT EIGHT-AND-SEVENTY — SEVEN GOLD GLOVES, MORE THAN TWO THOUSAND GAMES BEHIND THE PLATE, AND A NAME THE SPORT STILL ANSWERS TO

The game has lost the man in the middle of its first dynasty of blood. Bob Boone, catcher of the Phillies' first World Series championship and patriarch-in-the-middle of baseball's first three-generation family, is dead at 78; his son Bret announced the passing on Tuesday, and no cause was given. "He was a man of great integrity. He was a great dad, husband, and patriarch. It was a privilege to call him my father," Bret wrote. "Baseball lost a great one today."5

The playing ledger is a monument to endurance at the game's most punishing station. Drafted out of Stanford in 1969 as a third baseman and converted behind the plate in the minors, Boone caught for 19 big-league seasons — ten with Philadelphia, seven with the Angels of California, two with Kansas City — and his 2,225 games caught stood as the record in their day, since passed only by Carlton Fisk and Iván Rodríguez. He won seven Gold Gloves, the last of them at 41; made three All-Star teams; and in the October of 1980 led the Phillies at .412 as they beat the Royals for the franchise's first title, catching the final strike when Tug McGraw fanned Willie Wilson. Moments earlier in that same inning came the play Philadelphia never tires of re-running: Frank White's foul pop glancing off Boone's mitt by the dugout and dropping, as if by appointment, into Pete Rose's glove. He later managed both the Royals and the Reds, and the Phillies set him on their Wall of Fame in 2005. "We played in the minors together where he gave up third base so I could play it, only to become one of the best catchers in baseball history," said Mike Schmidt, his teammate of a decade. "We lost a close friend."5

And then there is the house itself. His father, Ray Boone, played 13 seasons and twice made the All-Star team for Detroit; his son Bret was three times an All-Star across 14 years spent largely in Seattle; his son Aaron played 13 seasons — some of them in Cincinnati under his father's management — and today manages the Yankees, who mourned "an exceptional player, respected manager and distinguished executive" and said they see his values carried forward in their own dugout; a grandson, Jake, works in the Mets' front office. Four generations in the game's employ, and the second of them its sturdiest hinge. The Herald lowers its colors for a man who squatted behind the plate past his fortieth birthday and never once made durability look like drudgery — the game asked him to catch, and for the better part of three decades, in one capacity or another, he never stopped.5

THE EXCHANGE DESK: THE SUITORS BOOK PASSAGE TO CALIFORNIA — THE METS WILL SIT WITH SKUBAL HIMSELF NEXT WEEK AND THE DODGERS BESIDES, THE ATLANTAS PRONOUNCE THEIR RIGHT FIELDER NOT FOR SALE WITH SOME HEAT, AND SEATTLE PARTS WITH THE LAST MAN OF ITS REBUILDING

The winter's great sweepstakes advanced from corridor talk to calendar entries on Tuesday. The Mets — installed by the trade as the favorites for Tarik Skubal only yesterday morning in these columns — will fly officials to Los Angeles next week to meet the left-hander and his representatives, and the Dodgers' general manager Brandon Gomes allowed that his club, which rented Skubal's summer services at the cost of three prospects, will take its own meeting. David Stearns declined all comment on the man himself but took direct aim at the theory that he will not pay long for pitching: "Every situation is unique, and it's been also well publicized even since I've been here — we've engaged in extremely long-term negotiations for pitchers and we will do so again. But situation matters." What he would commit to is appetite: "There's no question we're going to add to that group." The object of all this scheduling turns 30 next week and owns, over the past four seasons, a record of 50-20, an earned-run average of 2.47 and the best league-adjusted pitching figure in the game among arms with 300 innings — against which stands the surgeon's ledger, multiple elbow operations since 2023 and a career high of just 195 and one-third innings. The trade still expects him to sail past the $325 million that Yoshinobu Yamamoto's contract presently marks as the water line; whether Stearns, whose richest pitching deal in Queens remains Sean Manaea's three years and $75 million, or his owner's famous purse writes the number is the question the Aria's corridors chew on.6

At Atlanta's table the general manager spent his Tuesday stamping out a fire he insists was never lit. Asked about the speculation that Ronald Acuña Jr. might be moved — talk fed by a miserable October of 3 hits in 24 at-bats, boos at home, and the outfielder wiping the club from his Instagram — Alex Anthopoulos went to the record with unusual relish: "There's not even a hint of smoke to it. There's zero... There have been zero conversations. And I don't mind saying that on the record. Zero. None. No phone calls. Incoming, outgoing." The origin he assigned the rumor — "somebody on his computer, in his basement, put it out there" — is the nearest thing to period invective the meetings have yet produced. Acuña, who turns 29 next month and batted .255 with 20 home runs across an injury-shortened 107 games, had his $17 million option for 2027 exercised the moment the World Series ended. "He's a huge part of our club," Anthopoulos said. "We need him. We need to add to him."7

And in the northwest, an era was shown the door with honors. The Mariners have informed J.P. Crawford, their shortstop of eight seasons and the longest-serving man on the roster, that they do not intend to bring him back — this though Crawford said as recently as May, "I want to be a Mariner for life." He is 31, a free agent for the first time after playing out five years and $51 million, and the last man standing from the club's first true rebuilding; on the season's final day the Puget Sound men pulled him for a defensive replacement so the park could stand and cheer, and the man wept where he stood. The succession is its own winter's work: young Colt Emerson, extended for $95 million in the spring, is the heir presumptive at shortstop but comes off a rookie year wrecked by a wrist that wanted surgery, and the club's whole left-handed lineup wants right-handed ballast besides, with Randy Arozarena expected to decline his qualifying tender and test the water. The Herald observes that rebuildings are usually reckoned by the stars they produce, and that this one may be best reckoned by the complementary piece who became its conscience.8

THE INFIRMARY WARD: THE PINSTRIPES' LEFT ARM IS SWEPT CLEAN OF ITS SPUR AND PROMISED FOR THE SPRING — THE CAPTAIN'S RIB KNITS WITHOUT FURTHER SURGERY, AND BROWN OF THE NORTH SIDERS TAKES A SCREW IN THE NECK AND AN APPOINTMENT WITH THE NEW YEAR

The surgeons' week continues brisk. In New York, Max Fried has had a bone spur removed from his pitching elbow — the joint whose "bone bruise" twice sent him to the shelf this past season, in May and again in August — and the Pinstripes expect their left-hander ready for the spring, whose first exhibitions are listed for February 19.91 Fried, who turns 33 in January and enters the third year of his eight-year, $218 million engagement, posted a 2.80 earned-run average across 20 starts when the elbow permitted; Brian Cashman set the protocol at six weeks of rest before a training program begins in the second week of December. The same briefing carried the winter's most consequential clean bill: Aaron Judge's fractured rib, reviewed by the specialist who has watched over its healing, requires no procedure at all — "He's currently in a good place," Cashman said, prescribing only a winter of rest for the captain whose absence reduced the Yankee attack to 66 games of its best man. "I know one thing — we'll be instantly better if we have a healthy Aaron Judge."9

Chicago's news asks more patience. Ben Brown, the young right-hander whose 1.85 earned-run average over 20 appearances was among the few unqualified pleasures of the North Siders' pitching year, underwent cervical spine surgery Monday in New York — a screw set at the fifth vertebra to stabilize a fracture, the second time in three years his neck has stopped his season. The club expects him throwing early in the new year and game-ready in the first half of 2027. "He's walking around, looks great," said the general manager Carter Hawkins. "We expect to have him back next year." The Herald wishes the young man a dull and uneventful convalescence; a rotation that already buried the seasons of Cade Horton and Justin Steele has earned a little tedium.10

THE INK DESK: THE BENGALS BUY A LATE-INNINGS ARM FOR ELEVEN MILLIONS — PAGÁN CROSSES FROM ONE ROOKIE-LAUREL HOUSE TO THE OTHER, WHILE THE FRIARS, WANTING VERY NEARLY A WHOLE ROTATION, SIGN THE FIRST ARM OF THE MANY THEY WILL NEED

The week's first notable signatures are relievers' and reclamations'. Detroit is in agreement with Emilio Pagán on one year and $11 million, the deal pending a physical — and the clerks note the small symmetry that the club whose rookie took Monday's American laurels has gone shopping in the pen of the club whose rookie took the National. Pagán, who enters his age-36 campaign, spent the past two seasons with Cincinnati pitching to a 3.07 earned-run average across 111 and one-third innings, holding batters to a .185 average over that span, though a hamstring cost him nearly two months of the summer just closed. The fastball still arrives at an average of 94.9 mph, with a cutter beside it; the Bengals, who parted with their twice-laureled ace at midseason and crowned a Rookie of the Year on Monday, have bought themselves some certainty for the eighth inning.11

San Diego's signature is humbler and more telling. The Friars signed the right-hander Ryan Weiss to a Major League contract with a club option for 2028 — a 29-year-old who pitched to a 7.62 earned-run average in nine outings for Houston, was let go to Triple-A Sugar Land, and finished the year there striking out 24 men in his final 19 and two-thirds innings at a 1.37 mark, after two seasons of solid work for the Hanwha Eagles of Korea before that. The context is the point: seven San Diego starters, Michael King, Nick Pivetta and Walker Buehler among them, walked into free agency at the season's end, leaving a rotation of Randy Vásquez and question marks. "You can never have too much, from a starting pitching standpoint," said A.J. Preller, the man now obliged to prove it. "We're going to need multiples." Weiss is the first; the Herald will keep the running count.12

A Wednesday of silver given and silver promised. The managers' prizes are bestowed — one to a hospital bed in Milwaukee, one to the South Side — and the week's bill holds to the schedule this paper printed Monday: the Cy Youngs and the writers' first reliever's prize are read this evening, while the league stages its awards theater at the Chelsea Theater of the Cosmopolitan at Las Vegas at 9 o'clock Eastern, Mookie Betts and the comedian Roy Wood Jr. presiding over the Aaron awards, the reliever medals and the rest of the glitter;13 the Most Valuable verdicts close the books on the morrow, the same day the courtroom at Brooklyn resumes, as Monday's columns noted. The forward ledger, ruled open at first light as the house requires, stands unmoved: nothing on the card before the spring exhibitions of Friday, the 19th of February, 100 mornings from this one, with Opening Night 133 mornings off on Wednesday, the 24th of March.1 The 17th keeps its double appointment six mornings hence — qualifying answers and silver bats together — and the collective agreement has 20 mornings left to live. The desk notes, finally, what the date at the masthead already says: this is the eleventh morning of the eleventh month, Armistice Day by the old reckoning, and a paper set in this type remembers the eleventh hour of 1918 and every man of the game who ever traded flannels for khaki. Until the next dispatch, keep your scorecards near and your enthusiasms nearer.
