News Around the League

THE LEATHER DESK: RAWLINGS SETS ITS PLATINUM UPON THE TWO FINEST GLOVES IN THE LAND — CROW-ARMSTRONG OF THE SIX-AND-TWENTY OUTS SAVED TAKES THE NATIONAL HONOR, BELLINGER THE AMERICAN, AND THE BRUINS AND THE MAPLE LEAF NINE ARE CROWNED THE BEST-LEATHERED CLUBS ENTIRE

The fielding season, which handed down its score of Gold Gloves on Sunday evening as these columns recorded Monday, took its final bow Friday night: Rawlings announced its Platinum Glove winners — the best defender in each league, any position considered — and both medals went to first-time holders. The National League's platinum belongs to Pete Crow-Armstrong, the Cubs' center fielder, and the case was not close by the instruments' lights: his 26 outs above average led or shared the lead in all of baseball for a second season running, his 16 defensive runs saved stood tied for seventh among every man at every station, and his 63 outs above average since 2024 trail exactly one player in the game — a Kansas City shortstop the reader will meet again below.3

The American League's platinum goes to Cody Bellinger, whose season in the Yankee outfield piled up 21 defensive runs saved, tied for the third-most in the majors behind only Wilyer Abreu's 28 and Brice Turang's 23 — company the reader of Monday's columns will recognize as the measuring sticks of the age. Bellinger's Gold Glove this season was the second of his career and his first since the 2019 campaign that brought him the Most Valuable award with the Dodgers; he becomes only the second Yankee to hold the platinum since Rawlings struck it in 2011, after the catcher Jose Trevino in 2022.3 The ornament has a habit of finding the champions' neighborhood besides: the National League's platinum of 2024 belonged to Turang himself.2

The team honors went north and further north. The Cubs were named the National League's best defensive club for the second year running and the third time since Rawlings extended the award to whole teams in 2020, and the margin brooked no argument — 72 outs above average and 115 defensive runs saved, each figure the best in the game by a comfortable street, with Crow-Armstrong and Alex Bregman both carrying individual gold, as Monday's columns told. The Blue Jays took the American League's team honor for the third time, drawing even with the Cubs for the most such awards any club holds.3

THE APPOINTMENTS DESK: THE REDBIRDS FILL THEIR SECOND CHAIR FROM THEIR OWN HALLWAY BEFORE THE MARKET CAN CALL TWICE — CERFOLIO OF THE CLEVELAND SCHOOLING IS NAMED GENERAL MANAGER AT ST. LOUIS, AND THE WEEK'S FASHION FOR DEVELOPMENT MEN GAINS A THIRD SPECIMEN

St. Louis called the press to Busch Stadium on Friday afternoon and named Rob Cerfolio its next general manager, promoting him from assistant general manager of player development and performance. The president of baseball operations Chaim Bloom, who calls this stretch of the calendar "always one of the craziest times of the year," had reason to move briskly: the club lost its assistant general manager and scouting director Randy Flores to Houston's top baseball job only a week ago, an installation this paper has chronicled since, and Friday's promotion shuts the door on any further raids of the front office.4

The new man's ladder is the fashionable kind, climbed entirely through the development rooms. Cerfolio entered baseball as a Cleveland intern in 2015, rose over a decade to that organization's director of player development, and came to St. Louis in October of 2024 among the first and most instrumental hires of the Bloom regime; in the two years since, the Cardinals' farm system has vaulted up the industry's rankings, and the club credits him as an architect of the revival. "The way that he operates and what he's accomplished in the areas that he has overseen really have stood out and make this a very smooth and natural move for us today," said Bloom, who means to widen his lieutenant's scope to every corner of baseball operations: "There's just a drive and relentlessness to how he operates that I think is really important." Cerfolio, for his part, pointed to the man seated at his left: "I trust him as a person."4

The Herald remarked Thursday that two mornings running had handed a development man a top chair — Seattle's new manager Kanzler on Tuesday, the Athletics' new general manager Castro on Wednesday — and the fashion now owns a third specimen inside the week. The reasoning has not changed since these columns offered it: the world's championship was lately won by a club of modest purse that grows its own, and every front office in the game would like the recipe. The difference in St. Louis is the direction of travel — the Cardinals did not go shopping for a development man; they simply declined to let theirs be shopped.4

THE CONTRACTS DESK: WASHINGTON SIGNS ITS WALL-CLIMBING CENTER FIELDER FOR FOUR YEARS AND THREE-AND-TWENTY MILLIONS — YOUNG OF THE IMPOSSIBLE CATCHES IS SECURED THROUGH 2030, BY THE WORD OF A SOURCE, WITH AN OPTION FOR A FIFTH YEAR BESIDES

The Capital nine have moved to keep their outfield's acrobat. Jacob Young has agreed to a contract extension of four years and $23 million, with a club option of $8 million for a fifth season, by the word of a source — the club itself had confirmed nothing by press time. The deal covers Young's three remaining years of arbitration and one season that would otherwise have belonged to the open market, binding him to Washington through 2030 with the option running to 2031, and it is the second extension of the young winter under the president of baseball operations Paul Toboni, who signed the right-hander Cade Cavalli for four years and $47 million in September.5

What Washington is buying is chiefly the glove, and the glove is of the circus kind — the clubhouse calls him Spider-Man, and the tape supports the libel. A seventh-round pick out of the University of Florida in 2021, Young led all outfielders in baseball with 20 outs above average in 2024 and has banked 14 and 11 in the two seasons since; the signature exhibit came on August 23 last, when he covered 93 feet of ground at Miami, went up the wall to rob Heriberto Hernández of a home run, and threw back to the infield to double off a runner for good measure.5

The bat, long the junior partner, improved enough this season to make the arithmetic easy: Young hit .252 with 10 home runs — as many in the one year as his whole career had held before it — drove in a career-high 50 runs, and stole 18 bases in 22 tries. The club around him finished 77-85, better by 11 wins than the season before, behind an attack that ranked third in the game in runs scored; the extension fixes one more star to a young chart that already holds Abrams, Wood, Crews and Lile.5

THE LAURELS DESK: THE OLD PLAYERS HAND WITT THE HEART-AND-HUSTLE MEDAL FOR THE THIRD YEAR RUNNING — NO MAN HAD EVER WON IT THRICE SINCE THE ALUMNI STRUCK IT IN 2005, AND ONLY BIGGIO EVER KEPT HIM COMPANY AT TWO

The Major League Baseball Players Alumni Association named Bobby Witt Jr. the winner of its Heart & Hustle Award on Friday, and the Kansas City shortstop thereby stands alone in the medal's books: three wins, all in succession, where no man had taken it thrice since the alumni established the honor in 2005. The award goes each year to the active player judged to best embody the game's values, spirit and traditions — committees of former players name one man per club, and fans, alumni and active players together vote the winner from the thirty. Witt had shared the two-win shelf with the Hall of Famer Craig Biggio, honored in 2006 and 2007, and he has been Kansas City's nominee in every one of his five big-league seasons. "One of my favorite awards I've gotten," he told MLB Network. "I just try to play the game to my best ability and go out there and give 100% of what I got, each and every night."6

The season beneath the sentiment holds up under any instrument pointed at it. Witt hit .281 with 18 home runs, 33 doubles and 45 stolen bases across 143 games; his 24 outs above average led the American League and stood third in the majors; and he became the first player in the history of the game to log at least 10 home runs and 25 stolen bases in each of his first five seasons.6 The reader of this morning's leather columns has met him once already — his is the lone glovework ledger in baseball that outranks Crow-Armstrong's over the past three seasons.3

The medal joins a queue rather than ends one. Witt stands a finalist for the Most Valuable award beside Alvarez of Houston and Caminero of the Rays, as these columns recorded Tuesday, with the verdict to be read Thursday; he is a finalist besides for a third consecutive silver bat at shortstop, as yesterday's columns told; and his pursuit of a third straight Gold Glove ended only when Walls took the shortstop's leather, as Monday's columns had it.6 As for the man's own schooling, he offered the arithmetic himself: he wore No. 17 in high school for the 15 of Pedroia and the 2 of Jeter, the two men he watched to learn how the game is carried.6

THE HOUSEKEEPING COLUMNS: SEATTLE SEATS A PROFESSOR OF CATCHING BESIDE ITS NEW CAPTAIN, LOS ANGELES PARTS WITH HALF ITS HITTING FACULTY, AND CLEVELAND RECLAIMS A WANDERING SON OFF THE WAIVER WIRE

In Seattle, the new manager Jason Kanzler — whose hiring these columns recorded Wednesday — is getting a right hand: the club is in the process of making Tanner Swanson its bench coach, by the word of a source confirming an earlier report. Swanson is a native of Washington state and a Central Washington University man come home; he spent 2020 through 2026 on the Yankees' major-league staff as their director of catching, taking on the field coordinator's duties besides from 2024, and the receivers under his instruction led all of baseball in framing runs across those seasons. He takes the chair lately held by Manny Acta, one of four coaches let go at the season's end, and the minor-league hitting coordinator CJ Gillman is expected to take the lead hitting post, though the club confirms nothing yet.7

In Los Angeles the parting is the surprise of the day: the Dodgers and the hitting coach Aaron Bates could not agree on a new contract and will go their separate ways, by the word of a source — this after the baseball operations chief Andrew Friedman had said he expected the staff to stand pat for 2027. Bates spent 12 years in the organization and shared the hitting duty with Robert Van Scoyoc for the past four seasons, in every one of which the club ranked among the game's four best in runs scored; this year's edition stood fourth in runs and second in on-base-plus-slugging before the attack went quiet in October against the eventual champions. Van Scoyoc is expected back, and the club is considering hiring a second coach to sit beside him — an arrangement on which the Herald ventured a view only yesterday, concerning a certain two-headed calf at the county fair, and which it now observes the Dodgers have kept profitably these four years. The desk withdraws nothing; it merely notes the calf's yield.8

And on the shore of Lake Erie, a homecoming of the modest kind: the Guardians claimed the infielder José Tena off waivers from Washington on Friday, days after the Nationals designated him for assignment. Tena, 25, began his professional life as a Cleveland signing out of the Dominican Republic in 2017 and debuted with the club in 2023, before the trade that sent him to Washington for Lane Thomas in July of 2024. He returns with a .222 average and 7 home runs from this past season, a bat the measuring instruments say has quickened considerably year over year, and a glove that has stood at every station save catcher, first base and center field; he is out of minor-league options, which makes the claim a commitment rather than a flyer, and the 40-man roster now stands at 37.9

A Saturday of silverware and staff rooms: the fielding year took its final bow in platinum, the Redbirds bolted the front-office door Houston walked through a week ago, and the housekeeping columns ran brisk from the Sound to Lake Erie. The forward ledger, opened at first light as the house rule requires, reads as it read Friday: nothing stirs before the spring exhibitions of Friday, the 19th of February, now 104 mornings from this one, with Opening Night 137 mornings off on Wednesday, the 24th of March.1 The nearer docket wants its pencil sharpened: the writers read their first verdicts Monday, the rookies called before the rest, and the Most Valuable men follow Thursday;6 the 17th remains the winter's most crowded square, the qualifying answers and the silver bats both due that day; and the collective agreement has 24 mornings left to live. Until the morrow's dispatch, keep your scorecards near and your enthusiasms nearer.
