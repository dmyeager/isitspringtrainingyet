News Around the League

THE SKUBAL REGISTER, CLOSED: THE VERDICT IS FLUSHING — NINE YEARS AND FOUR HUNDRED FIFTY MILLIONS, A HALF-HUNDRED PER ANNUM, THE RICHEST CONTRACT EVER WRITTEN FOR A PITCHER, AND THE GREAT LEFT-HANDER IS A MET WITH EIGHT MORNINGS LEFT ON THE AGREEMENT'S LIFE

The register is closed. On Sunday, by the account of sources, Tarik Skubal agreed with the Mets on a contract of nine years and $450 million, running through 2035, with full no-trade protection and no opt-out anywhere in it — the club not yet confirming, a physical still to be passed. The ledger entries write themselves into the record book: the richest contract ever given a pitcher, past Yoshinobu Yamamoto's twelve years and $325 million of December 2023; an average wage of $50 million a year, the highest ever carried by a full-time pitcher, with only Shohei Ohtani, Kyle Tucker and Juan Soto drawing more per season in all the game. The reader of Saturday's register will note the arithmetic of the final hour: the reported offer then standing was nine years and very nearly $400 million, and the figure, when it hardened, had grown by some $50 million. The audited suitors — the Dodgers, the San Diegans, the Torontos, and at least one establishment that never gave its name — go home with their checkbooks intact, and the winter's grandest courtship is concluded with the collective agreement's clock still showing 8 mornings.3

The man the money bought turned 30 on Friday, and the file wants no embroidery. Two Cy Young Awards, 2024 and 2025 — the twelfth pitcher ever to take the honor in consecutive seasons — the first of them unanimous and fastened to the pitching Triple Crown, 18 wins beside a 2.39 earned-run average and 228 strikeouts; the second built on a league-best 2.21 with 241 strikeouts across 195 and a third innings. Over the four seasons since 2023 the figure reads 2.47, with very nearly seven strikeouts for every walk issued. None of it was promised him: a ninth-round selection out of Seattle University in 2018, he has three arm operations in his file — the first a Tommy John reconstruction that cost him the 2017 season, the most recent this past May's removal of loose bodies from the left elbow, a convalescence of barely six weeks. On the second of August, Detroit — hovering at the fringes of contention — sold him west to the Dodgers, and over his final 16 starts of the season, ten of them in Los Angeles, he pitched to a 2.36.3

What Flushing bought is a correction long overdue. The rotation's earned-run average has slid three seasons running — twelfth in the game at 3.91 in 2024, eighteenth at 4.13 in 2025, twenty-third at 4.47 this past year — and the president of baseball operations David Stearns allowed this month that only the young McLean and Scott held guaranteed posts. The club that paid Soto $765 million two Decembers ago has now written the pitching equivalent, and the wire observes that it makes Skubal the sixth Met to earn better than $19 million next season and the third above $42 million. The sobering page of the file is the last one: the final year, at $45 million, will be pitched entire at the age of 38, and only three regular starters that old took a big-league mound in 2026 — though the wager's defenders point to Sale, Wheeler, Scherzer and Verlander as proof that the elite arm ages better than most. Nor, the wire insists, is the shopping done: a first baseman and bullpen help remain on the Flushing list, and possibly another starter besides.34

THE MARKET AFTER THE VERDICT: ONE SIGNATURE UNLOCKS THE ESCROW — THE WIRE EXPECTS THE TOP OF THE CLASS SIGNED INSIDE THE WEEK, FIVE CLUBS CIRCLE SATO, AND THE JILTED SUITORS TAKE INVENTORY OF THEIR PURSES

These columns wrote Saturday that one signature held a dozen in escrow; the signature is now supplied, and the wire's Mark Feinsand reckons the consequence plainly: the bulk of the winter's top twenty or thirty free agents are expected to sign before the collective agreement expires on the first of December, the most of them in the final 72 to 96 hours — a rush driven, he notes, less by the Skubal verdict than by the calendar itself. Five years ago, six men signed nine-figure contracts in the days before the last agreement lapsed; the wire does not expect quite so rich a stampede this time, but the shape is the same. The next tier of arms — Michael King, Freddy Peralta, Clay Holmes, Trevor Rogers, Nick Martinez — offers no one of the Skubal class, so there is no logical consolation purchase; there is merely a healthy market, for the clubs needing pitching outnumber the pitchers. The slugger Teruaki Sato, the eastern market's grandest bat, is expected by industry sources to sign with one of five clubs — the Yankees, the Quakers, the Blue Jays, the Mets or the Cubs, the first three reckoned the favorites — and the posted men of the Pacific shelf, Taira and Itoh and Korea's Si-Hwan Roh, face in effect the same first-of-December date, else they must wait upon another year.5

The jilted suitors, meanwhile, take their inventory. The Dodgers, who rented the great man for a pennant drive and could not keep him, are read by the wire as having preferred a shorter engagement at a steeper yearly wage — the decade-long term is a vow that establishment has made only to Ohtani, Betts and Yamamoto, and nine years for a pitcher of 30 proved its breaking point — and they count seven starters on the premises without reckoning Ohtani among them, which made the pursuit a luxury rather than a necessity. Their winter turns now to a right-handed-hitting infielder, the at-liberty Mauricio Dubón suggested, and to the left-field question these columns examined only yesterday — whether young De Paula's September earns him the post, possibly at the price of a Teoscar Hernández trade. San Diego and the Cubs each need multiple starters and must shop the tier below. And in Philadelphia the wire reads no panic — Dombrowski is not expected to swerve from his plans — though the columnist Will Leitch counsels the Quakers to worry all the same, an aging roster built to win now having just watched the rival in Queens grow considerably better in the near term.54

THE INK DESK: THE CLUB THAT COULD NOT KEEP ITS OWN GREAT LEFT-HANDER BUYS A MENDED ONE — GIL, ROOKIE OF THE YEAR TWO AUTUMNS GONE, COMES TO DETROIT FOR A SINGLE YEAR, FRIDAY'S WASTE-BASKET PROVING SUNDAY'S BARGAIN COUNTER

Luis Gil spent precisely one weekend at liberty. Non-tendered by the Yankees at Friday's stroke of five — the headline casualty of the deadline, as these columns recorded Saturday — the right-hander agreed Sunday with the Tigers on a contract of one year and $2.3 million, with performance bonuses that could add $2 million more, the deal pending a physical and the club not yet confirming; MLB.com's Mark Feinsand first reported the terms. The résumé needs no restatement beyond its headline: Rookie of the Year in the American League in 2024, on a line of 15 wins against 7 losses with a 3.50 earned-run average across 29 starts, the first Yankee pitcher to take the honor since Righetti in 1981. What followed is the reason the price is $2.3 million and not ten times it — a lat strain that swallowed the first four months of 2025, shoulder inflammation that took three months of 2026, nine appearances all year and a final outing in September of four walks in two innings.67

Detroit's logic was stated on the record by its president of baseball operations, Scott Harris: "We need to add innings. I'm not going to run from that" — and, of the market at large, "everything's on the table for us right now." The Bengals' rotation company reads Valdez, Anderson, Jobe, Ryan and the newly extended Melton — whose six-year engagement these columns set down Saturday — with Olson mending from shoulder surgery behind them, and the pitching coach Chris Fetter is handed the winter's classic restoration: a fastball and a slider that have both lost their bite since 2024, and a frame the medical men must keep upright. The Herald cannot resist the weekend's symmetry: in the same hours the club that would not meet its own great left-hander's price watched him sign for $450 million in Queens, it spent $2.3 million on a gamble that a Rookie of the Year still lives in a mended shoulder. And the man arrives in familiar livery, for Gil winters even now with the Tigres del Licey, as Saturday's columns had him — he need not so much as change the cat upon his cap.67

THE FRONT OFFICE WIRE: A CATCHER OF THE OLD GUARD COMES HOME TO THE MILE HIGH NINE — SERVAIS, NINE SEASONS THE MARINERS' SKIPPER, TAKES A SENIOR DESK IN THE DENVER REBUILDING

The Rockies' busy winter added a name of standing on Sunday: Scott Servais returns to the organization where his front-office life began, as senior director of baseball operations, with official announcement of the club's new hires expected shortly. The man is 59 and his card runs the whole breadth of the trade — eleven big-league seasons a catcher, 820 games for the Astros, Cubs, Giants and Rockies, 33 of them in Colorado colors; special-projects work in the Denver shop at his playing days' end under Jerry Dipoto; the Rangers' player-development chair from 2004 to 2010; an assistant general managership with the Angels; and nine seasons managing Seattle, 2016 through 2024, at 680 wins against 642, five winning years and the breaking, in 2022, of a postseason drought twenty seasons old. He spent 2025 an assistant development coach with the Padres, and he keeps a home at Steamboat Springs — "looking forward," he said Sunday, "to helping the Rox any way possible."8

The appointment reads as a piece with the month. The DePodesta-Byrnes administration has bought two starting pitchers from Chicago in a single stroke — the Castillo and Martin freight these columns recorded Friday — added the sidearming aerospace man Lee on Saturday's page, and installed Ethan Katz at pitching coach; Servais arrives as one of several hires across player development, research and the performance sciences, his own expertise lying in the development line. A franchise that lately owned the game's highest earned-run average is assembling grown-ups at every desk, and the Herald, which has watched many a Denver winter produce nothing but altitude, notes that this one at least produces transactions.8

THE ALMANAC: FIVE HUNDRED BY THE LONG WAY ROUND — SCHWARBER, HIS FIVE-AND-FORTY CIRCUIT CLOUTS OF THE SUMMER FRESHLY BANKED, STANDS 115 FROM THE PANTHEON'S DOOR

The wire's Thomas Harrigan opens a file the Herald is pleased to keep beside its own almanac: Kyle Schwarber's march on 500 home runs, which would be among the strangest ever made. The Philadelphia slugger, 33, signed in that city through 2030, holds 385 for his career — 115 shy of the mark — and the oddity is all in the ledger's first half. Through his age-28 season Schwarber had struck 153, fewer at that age than all but two men who ever reached the five hundred: David Ortiz, who stood at 130 and finished at 541, and Rafael Palmeiro, at 132 on his way to 569. Since signing in Philadelphia in March 2022 he has hit 232 — about 46 a season — which is the second-most any man has hit in his first five years with one club, behind only Ruth's 235 in Yankee flannels from 1920 to 1924, and past the old Philadelphia five-year marks of Howard (229) and Schmidt (199). Only ten hitters in the game's history have put up a five-year span of 232 or better, and the company — Ruth, Sosa, Bonds, Rodriguez, McGwire chief among them — explains the raised eyebrow.910

The arithmetic forward is gentler than the arithmetic behind. The season just closed gave him 45 home runs, an .857 on-base-plus-slugging and a second Silver Slugger; to reach 500 inside the contract's four remaining years wants about 29 a season — a figure the summer just banked cleared with sixteen to spare — and Harrigan rates the errand a near-certainty if health allows. The Herald will keep the count in this column as it climbs, and observes only that the game's roll of late bloomers — Ortiz foremost, 58 home runs through age 26 and a plaque in Cooperstown now — has always read like an argument for patience.9

A Monday paper that retires its longest-running ledger: the Skubal Register, opened when the courtship began, closes above at nine years and $450 million, and the market it held in escrow now runs free — the full accounting, with the weekend's other ink, is set down in the columns. The forward ledger was consulted before the first word above was written, as the house custom commands, and reads as it has all week: the camps' reporting dates remain unposted, so the count runs to the first rung bearing a date — the spring exhibition openers of Friday, the 19th of February, 88 mornings from this one, with Opening Night to follow on Wednesday, the 24th of March, 121 mornings off.2 The nearer columns crowd closer than ever: the collective agreement has 8 mornings left to live, and the bargainers sit again this very morning;11 Cooperstown's committee of sixteen reports in 13; the Winter Meetings convene at San Diego with the Rule 5 drafting on December 9, 16 mornings from this one; and the writers' envelope waits for the 19th of January, 57 mornings off. Until the morrow's dispatch, keep your scorecards near and your enthusiasms nearer.
