THE MORNING HORSEHIDE HERALD

"Every Score Set Down, No Deed Unsung"

Volume I · No. 130 · Sunday, the Fifteenth of November, Two Thousand and Twenty-Six

No contests this day; the hot stove burns bright.

96 days until the first spring-training game (Friday, the 19th of February, 2027).

News Around the League

THE PROSPECT FIELDS: PEORIA TAKES THE DESERT'S LAST PRIZE — THE JAVELINAS BREAK SURPRISE 8-3 FOR AN EIGHTH FALL LEAGUE CROWN, THE MOST IN THE CIRCUIT'S BOOKS AND THEIR FIRST SINCE 2018, BEHIND FIVE SCORELESS INNINGS FROM HOLMAN OF THE QUEEN CITY CHAIN

The desert kept its appointment. The Fall League championship that Saturday's columns commended to every restless scorecard hand was decided at the Peoria Sports Complex on Saturday night, and it was the home club's evening throughout: the Peoria Javelinas 8, the Surprise Saguaros 3, and the title — an eighth for Peoria, the most any club has gathered in the circuit's history and its first since 2018 — was denied to the defending champions, finalists now seven autumns running. The occasion extended one of the minor calendar's quieter marvels besides: one of these two clubs has appeared in the title game in each of the last 17 seasons, the last final without either being played in 2008. Carter Graham of Cincinnati's chain supplied the victors' epigraph, shouting to anyone in earshot, "Best team in the nation, baby!"2

Luke Holman, whom Saturday's columns sent to the hill trailing a 0.69 earned-run average, pitched precisely to the billing: five scoreless innings, six strikeouts, 16 swings-and-misses drawn across three offerings — a fastball at 92 to 94 mph, a splitter, a curve — and the game's honors as its best player. The evening's loudest contact belonged to his systemmate Yerlin Confidan, the Fall Stars Game's Most Valuable man, who ripped a single at 116.7 mph and a double at 110.3 in a 3-for-4 night, while Graham added two singles and a walk. Justin DeCriscio of the San Diego chain lined an RBI double, Brandon Eike of the Seattle chain another; Ike Irish, the young Baltimore outfielder whose sacrifice fly had won the semifinal, went hitless yet scored twice, drove in a run and stole a bag; Kannon Kemp threw the 13 hardest pitches of the night across two scoreless innings of relief; and Francis Peña ran his heater to 97.5 mph in the ninth to lock the trophy away.2

The champions' ledger reads like a period of its own: 219 runs scored, the most of any club across the six-week term, and 112 stolen bases in 28 games — 16 clear of the next-nearest club, the circuit having experimented this autumn with a single disengagement per plate appearance. The whole enterprise answered to Danny Worth, a man of parts of six big-league seasons who had never managed at any level before this fall, and who, dripping with the remains of the water cooler, allowed that he might simply retire on top: "We're gonna be watching all these guys on TV for a long time." And the autumn's individual laurel went where these columns said it belonged — Welbyn Francisca of the Lake Erie chain, whose .427 average led the league, was named winner of the Joe Black Award as its Most Valuable player, a consolation carried home in Surprise's otherwise empty luggage.2

THE EXCHANGE DESK: THE FRIARS, SHORT SEVEN STARTERS, GO COURTING EVERY ARM AT LIBERTY — A SITTING WITH SKUBAL IS BOOKED, OLD FRIENDS KING AND PIVETTA ARE EYED FOR REUNION, AND THE DODGERS JOIN A FIVE-CLUB QUEUE FOR LOÁISIGA

San Diego's winter brief is written in a single line of its general manager's: "you can never have too much" starting pitching — and A.J. Preller, who is losing seven starting pitchers to free agency at a stroke, is conducting himself accordingly. The Friars stand among the most active clubs in the starters' market and are leaving no door unknocked: a meeting is booked with Tarik Skubal himself, adding San Diego's name formally to the suitors' register these columns have kept all week; reunions are eyed with Michael King and Nick Pivetta, former men of the Mission both; and the club has expressed interest in Freddy Peralta — the right-hander who rediscovered his best self after the midseason trade to Tampa Bay and carried the honors of the league championship series in October — as well as Nick Martinez, whose career-best year read 31 starts, a first All-Star nod and the Game 1 assignment of the World Series, and who pitched to a 3.45 earned-run average in San Diego colors in 2022 and 2023. The register runs on: Matthew Boyd, Shota Imanaga and Dustin May — the last of whom yesterday's columns called the most democratically courted arm of the winter after Skubal — have all had their doors knocked as well.3

The relief market, meanwhile, nears its first verdict. Jonathan Loáisiga is reported "getting closer" to his next contract, with five clubs in the bidding — the Dodgers, Phillies, Mets, Yankees and Jays — for a right arm that rebounded from two injury-ruined seasons to a 2.42 earned-run average over 67 appearances in Arizona's employ. The Dodgers are also named among the pursuers of Tim Hill, the submarining left-hander lately of the Bronx, whose admirers include the Mets and his former Yankee employers besides — for in this winter's market, as in every winter's, the line for left-handed relief never shortens.3

THE SONORAN QUESTION, A SECOND WINTER RUNNING: THE BIRDS COME CALLING FOR MARTE — BALTIMORE AND ARIZONA ARE IN DIALOGUE ON THE TWICE-SILVERED SECOND BASEMAN, NO DEAL NEAR, AND THE MAN HIMSELF HOLDS THE VETO

The Marte question, which these columns found reopened at Las Vegas on Thursday, has produced its first named suitor. The Orioles have dialogued with the Diamondbacks regarding Ketel Marte — so a source allowed on Saturday evening — though no deal was imminent and neither club would confirm the discussions; the Jays, shopping for hitters generally, are reported among the interested as well. Arizona's steward Mike Hazen has supplied the winter's framing himself: "I just think I need to be open to making adjustments to what the roster looks like."43

The fit at Baltimore is at once obvious and awkward. Obvious, because the Birds drew a collective .628 on-base-plus-slugging from second base this past season, the fourth-worst figure in the majors, while Jackson Holliday, 22, long cast as the second baseman of the future, fought injuries culminating in September wrist surgery. Awkward, because the infield house is already crowded — so crowded that Mike Elias allows the club has spoken with some infielders about taking outfield work this spring. Yet Baltimore has shown exactly this appetite before: a year ago it wanted no first baseman and signed Pete Alonso to five years and $155 million anyway, a purchase that has aged handsomely.4

As for the man, the ledger states the case plainly. Marte is 33, a three-time All-Star and twice a Silver Slugger, a .277 hitter across 1,371 games with 193 home runs and an .815 on-base-plus-slugging; this past season he batted .249 with 22 home runs and a .748 mark in 141 games, around a brief August stay on the restricted list after failing to appear for a game at Fenway Park — an absence of some two weeks, closed with an apology to his manager and his teammates and a quiet, blameless remainder of the season. Four guaranteed years remain on his $116.5 million extension, with a player option for 2031 behind them, and his ten years of service — the last five in one uniform — give him the veto over any destination proposed. The desert may open the question; only Marte can close it.4

THE TRADE WINDS BLOW THROUGH SHORTSTOP COUNTRY — CALLERS RING THE WHITE ELEPHANTS ON WILSON, THE LONE STAR NINE LISTEN ON SEAGER, AND THE FISH FIELD OFFERS ON THREE YOUNG REGULARS AT ONCE

The winter's trade talk has found the game's middle infield. Bob Nightengale of USA TODAY reports that clubs have called the Athletics to inquire after Jacob Wilson — a name that would have seemed untouchable a year ago, when the shortstop batted .311 as a rookie, became the first rookie shortstop ever elected by the fans to start an All-Star Game for the American League, and finished second in the Rookie of the Year balloting to his own teammate Nick Kurtz. The Elephants extended him for seven years and $70 million before the 2026 season, control running through at least 2032; the season that followed is the reason the callers think it worth dialing — a .266 average over just 80 games, three separate trips to the injured list, and a nagging left shoulder that ended his year in mid-August. He turns 25 in March. Elsewhere in the shortstop country, the Lone Star nine are reportedly listening on Corey Seager, while Washington's CJ Abrams is held unlikely to move.3

And in Miami, Barry Jackson of the Miami Herald reports the Fish fielding offers on three young regulars at once: the middle infielders Otto Lopez and Xavier Edwards — for whom the club "would want a ton to even consider moving" either — and the first baseman and outfielder Kyle Stowers. The asking prices have their reasons. Lopez, 28, was a first-time All-Star who led the majors with 191 hits, stole 34 bases and saved 14 outs above average at shortstop; Edwards, 27, batted .280 with a .360 on-base mark and 21 steals, worth three wins above replacement in each of the past two seasons; Stowers, 28, struck 21 home runs with a .790 on-base-plus-slugging through hamstring strains in each leg, a year after 25 and .912. All three are under club control through 2029 — which is precisely why the offers keep arriving, and precisely why Miami need accept none of them.3

THE COOPERSTOWN DESK: EIGHT NAMES GO BEFORE THE SIXTEEN — BOCHY OF THE FOUR TITLES AND MADDON OF THE BROKEN CURSE HEAD THE CONTEMPORARY ERA BALLOT, WITH BAKER, PINIELLA, ALDERSON, SABEAN, TURNER AND WHITE BESIDE THEM, THE VERDICT DUE THE SIXTH OF DECEMBER

The village on Otsego Lake has posted its winter docket. The Contemporary Baseball Era ballot — the committee's turn, this cycle, to weigh managers, executives and umpires — carries eight names: Sandy Alderson, Dusty Baker, Bruce Bochy, Joe Maddon, Lou Piniella, Brian Sabean, Ted Turner and Bill White. A 16-member panel of Hall of Fame players, executives, writers and historians will sit in judgment, twelve votes of the sixteen being required for election, and the results for the Class of 2027 are to be announced on December 6 — 21 mornings from this one.56

Two managers head the bill. Bochy's credentials scarcely require the committee's reading glasses: 2,252 wins, sixth on the all-time roll, with the five men above him — Mack, La Russa, McGraw, Cox and Torre — already enshrined; four World Series titles, making him the sixth manager to win so many and the first to do it for two clubs, the fourth ring fetched from retirement in his first Texas summer. "It's unthinkable for something like this to happen," says the man, 71, with a catcher's modesty. Maddon's case is the romantic's: 1,382 wins across parts of 19 seasons in Tampa Bay, Chicago and Anaheim — one of 35 managers to reach 1,300, and one of only 16 of those to win at a .532 clip or better — three Manager of the Year awards, two pennants, and the 2016 championship that ended the North Siders' 108-year wait, delivered by a man who governed with mantras ("9=8," "Try Not to Suck") and wore No. 70 on his back. His partisans have already minted the campaign slogan: Respect 70.5

The Herald notes, for the completeness of the record, that the remaining six names span the game's whole administration — Baker and Piniella from the dugout, Alderson and Sabean from the front office, Turner from the owner's box and White from the league presidency — and that a committee's arithmetic is harder than a season's. Eight worthies, sixteen electors, and three weeks to the envelope.5

A Sunday of consequence for the almanac: with the desert's trophy handed over, as the lead column records, the last diamond in the land occupied by big-league organizations has gone dark,2 and the American game is now, until the camps open, a matter of ledgers, telephones and ballots. The forward ledger was ruled open before a word was written, per the house custom, and stands unchanged: the camps' reporting dates are still to be posted, so the paper counts to the first rung bearing a date — the spring exhibition openers of Friday, the 19th of February, 96 mornings from this one — with Opening Night to follow on Wednesday, the 24th of March, 129 mornings off.1 The nearer appointments crowd the book regardless: Tuesday the 17th brings the qualifying answers and the silver bats together, as this paper's calendar has long carried; the collective agreement has 16 mornings left to live; and Cooperstown's sixteen electors report their verdict on December 6. The game has stopped playing, but it has by no means stopped keeping score. Until the morrow's dispatch, keep your scorecards near and your enthusiasms nearer.
