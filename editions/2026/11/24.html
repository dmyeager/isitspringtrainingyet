News Around the League

THE INK DESK: THE SPREE FINDS ITS THIRD SIGNATURE IN AS MANY MORNINGS — GOLDSCHMIDT, NINE-AND-THIRTY AND NEVER ONCE BELOW THE COMMON RUN, TAKES ONE YEAR AND EIGHT MILLIONS TO MIND FIRST BASE IN QUEENS, AND THE THREE-DAY BILL STANDS AT FIVE HUNDRED FIFTY-THREE MILLIONS

Less than a day after writing the richest pitching contract in the game's history, the Mets bought the cheapest Hall of Fame case on the market. By the account of a source, the club agreed Monday with Paul Goldschmidt on a contract of one year and $8 million — the club not yet confirming — and the position these columns have watched them shop all week has its tenant. The file on the man is the kind the Herald keeps in its permanent cabinet: a career on-base-plus-slugging of .876 with 390 home runs and 65.1 wins above replacement by Baseball-Reference's reckoning; the National League's Most Valuable Player of 2022 with St. Louis, the eighth consecutive season he finished among the top twenty in that balloting; seven All-Star summons, five Silver Sluggers, four Gold Gloves; and, by adjusted OPS, never once in his whole career a season below the league's average. He has, moreover, not spent a day on the injured list in 12 consecutive seasons — a durability that reads like a misprint in the present age.3

What remains of the great hitter at 39 is a specialist's weapon, and the fit is almost indecently neat. With the Yankees this past season Goldschmidt put up a .967 on-base-plus-slugging against left-handed pitching — a .299/.393/.573 line — beside a .765 overall with 18 home runs, good for a 111 OPS+, and his glove was worth 6 outs above average in 77 games at first base. The incumbent Jared Young swings left-handed and hit all nine of his home runs against right-handers, so the platoon assembles itself. The warnings are entered in the same ledger: a .590 figure against right-handed pitching, a third consecutive career low, and a hard-hit rate of 35.9 percent, his faintest since the tracking instruments were switched on in 2015. The Herald notes also the man's October habit — .262/.347/.533 with eight home runs across 33 playoff games — which a club of the Mets' present designs does not reckon incidental.3

The post he fills has been a sore since Pete Alonso signed in Baltimore last winter. Jorge Polanco, the handpicked successor, lost most of the season to an Achilles that wanted surgery and hit .148 when upright; Young proved a surprise but was concussed in September; Mark Vientos, tendered his contract but shopped all the same — the callers were already ringing on him, as these columns noted Sunday — now reads more expendable still, and Brett Baty is bound for regular work at second base. The president of baseball operations David Stearns had said first base was the only position beyond pitching he meant to prioritize, and the reader of the weekend's columns will note the pivot: the club had been eyeing Ty France, who seeks three years; it bought one year of Goldschmidt instead, and keeps its powder.3

The arithmetic of the spree wants its own line. Between Saturday morning and Monday night the Mets committed $553 million in guaranteed wages to three men — the young Ewing's eight years and $95 million, the great Skubal's nine and $450 million, and now Goldschmidt's one and $8 million, a rounding error riding in the caboose. The winter's quietest position purchase may prove its shrewdest; it is in any case its best-decorated.3

THE FLOODGATES, AS FORETOLD: THE FINEST LEFT ARM REMAINING GOES TO THE NORTH SIDE — ROGERS SIGNS FOR FIVE YEARS AND SIX-SCORE MILLIONS, THE THIRD-RICHEST PITCHING PURCHASE IN THE CUBS' LONG LEDGER, AND AN EMPTIED ROTATION HAS ITS FIRST NEW TENANT

The forecast these columns carried only yesterday — the top of the free-agent class signed inside the week, once the Skubal verdict loosed the escrow — required one day to begin proving itself. On Monday night the Cubs reached agreement with the left-hander Trevor Rogers on a multiyear contract, pending a physical; the pact runs five years at $120 million by multiple reports, with MLB Network's Jon Morosi first to the news. It is the third-richest free-agent engagement ever given a pitcher on the North Side, behind Jon Lester's six years and $155 million and Yu Darvish's six and $126 million, and it answers a need these columns have inventoried all week: the entire season-ending rotation — Boyd, Imanaga, Holmes, Gausman and Peterson, all five — walked to the open market at the season's close. The bill runs past the treasury, too: Rogers having rejected Baltimore's qualifying offer of $23.15 million, the Cubs, a luxury-tax payor, surrender their second- and fifth-highest selections in the 2027 draft and $1 million from the international bonus pool for the privilege.4

The man was the finest left arm at liberty once the great one signed in Queens, and his file reads like a serial with a late redemption. Rogers, 29 as of the 13th of November, was an All-Star and the National League's Rookie of the Year runner-up for the 2021 Marlins before his lower half betrayed him — all but four games of his 2023 lost, a deadline sale to the Orioles in 2024 for Stowers and Norby — and then came the restoration: a 1.81 earned-run average across 18 games in 2025, and this past season, after a wretched 6.84 through ten outings, a 2.90 from June onward across 20 starts, the fielding-independent figure a near-identical 2.89, with 119 strikeouts against 21 walks in 121 innings. The season closed at 4.03 with 157 strikeouts; the career ledger reads 3.88 across 132 starts. The four-seamer sits a modest 93.8 miles an hour yet has held hitters to a .204 average since the start of 2025, delivered from a low, deceptive slot and dressed with a changeup, sweeper, sinker and cutter; and a career ground-ball rate of 42.7 percent now lands in front of an infield that took the team Gold Glove in 2026, with the Platinum Glove man Crow-Armstrong patrolling center. The Cubs mean to add more arms yet, the door left open even to their own departed; the first and hardest purchase is made.4

THE LABOR QUESTION: SEVEN MORNINGS REMAIN — THE LEAGUE GILDS ITS CAP WITH A MILLION-DOLLAR MINIMUM AND BONUSES AT EVERY STATION OF A CAREER, THE UNION COUNTS NOT ONE NEW DOLLAR IN THE PARCEL, AND OFFERS BESIDES TO DIVIDE THE LOCAL AIRWAVES THIRTY WAYS

Monday's bargaining, with the agreement's clock running out, produced sweeteners from the league and a cold audit from the union. Major League Baseball proposed raising the minimum wage from $780,000 to $1 million for 2027 — a $220,000 rise, the largest single increase in the game's history, the old record being $129,500 — with the minimum thereafter growing alongside the proposed cap and floor; a pre-arbitration man logging a full year of service, and less than half of it on the injured list, would bank at least $100,000 more from the bonus program, a raise of 41 percent in all. The league appended two new instruments besides: a centrally funded signing bonus of 5 percent for every arbitration-eligible player who settles without a hearing — some $33 million a year spread across roughly 175 men, by the league's estimate — and a $500,000 bonus each club may award one veteran of six years' service signing a one-year contract of $10 million or less. These ride atop the earlier concessions: the pre-arbitration pool fattened from $50 million toward $75 million, free agency at five years for men of 30 and older — subject to a club's right to hold such a man one further year at the average of the game's 125 highest wages — and the qualifying offer abolished outright. "MLB is trying to reach an agreement with the players by meeting their objectives," said the spokesman Glen Caplin.5

The union's answer was arithmetic, not gratitude. Every gilt edge is affixed to the same cap-and-floor frame — the figures as last proposed being a $258.2 million ceiling and a $180.2 million floor, commencing 2028 — and the interim executive director Bruce Meyer observed that the proposals "don't add a single dollar in overall player compensation," the purse being fixed and merely re-divided, a fact the league itself does not dispute. "Any supposed benefits to certain classes of players," he said, "are simply taken out of the pockets of other players." The union made its own motion at the same table: it formally agreed Monday to the equal division of local media moneys among the thirty clubs — the league's own concept, accepted outside any cap — which would end the territorial exclusivity of the regional networks and the blackouts with them, and it coupled the acceptance to a revenue-sharing design that would raise assistance to the low-revenue markets from roughly $700 million to better than $950 million next year, more than $1 billion over the agreement's life, with rewards of $20 million for a sharing club that reaches the postseason — or misses it with a better record than the worst club that got in — and $10 million for one that merely finishes above .500.6

The Herald's sympathies on matters of the purse are a matter of long record, and Monday asks little of them: one side proposes to pay the poor clubs more for winning and to split the airwaves evenly; the other proposes a fixed pot with prettier partitions, while the ink of Sunday's $450 million signature — the free market's own appraisal of the best pitcher alive — dries on the page beside it. The agreement expires at one minute before midnight on the first of December, 7 mornings from this one, and the expectation throughout the game is that a lockout follows the stroke; the last one ran 99 days.6

THE EXCHANGE DESK: WHAT THE LOSERS DO NEXT — TORONTO'S SECRET BID OF FOUR HUNDRED TWENTY MILLIONS COMES TO LIGHT AS THE MAPLE LEAF NINE TURN TO BATS, SAN DIEGO VOWS EVERYTHING POSSIBLE FOR KING, THE DODGERS CONFESS THEY NEVER BID AT ALL, AND A GRANDER WEBB DESIGN IS WHISPERED AT THE BAY

The Skubal file, closed yesterday, keeps disgorging footnotes. The Athletic's Mitch Bannon reveals that Toronto's losing bid ran to nine years and $420 million — $30 million shy of the verdict — and that the front office, having entered the winter meaning to spend chiefly on its batting, now simply resumes the original plan: the Maple Leaf nine scored the fewest runs in the majors this past season, and the wire links them to Suzuki, Arozarena, Sato, Lowe and Chisholm at liberty and to Arizona's Ketel Marte by trade, with multiple clubs inquiring after the utility man Clement should an infielder arrive. The rotation — Cease, Soriano, Yesavage, Ponce, Arrighetti — is the lesser worry, though the club still monitors Peralta, King and Taira; a fourth name on that watch list, Trevor Rogers, came off the shelf Monday night.7

San Diego's pivot has a vow attached. The new ownership of Kwanza Jones and José E. Feliciano pursued Skubal in earnest, but the rotation's glut of vacancies always argued against sinking the whole treasury in one arm; the club will now, by USA Today's Bob Nightengale, "do everything possible to bring back" Michael King, with a Pivetta reunion entertained besides — the $18 million club option was declined this month chiefly because exercising it would have handed the right-hander a $14 million player option for 2028 — and Peralta and Taira in the field of possibilities, a corner outfielder from among Sato, Suzuki and Boston's shoppable Duran not excluded. The Dodgers' audit, meanwhile, completes itself: ESPN's Alden Gonzalez reports the club wanted its rented champion back but never put an offer on paper at all, and The Athletic's Katie Woo finds them disinclined to move aggressively for pitching, the infield depth reckoned settled by the Lewis purchase these columns recorded Saturday and the left-field question — De Paula or Hernández — standing as examined Sunday.7

And at the Bay, the shop window these columns reported Sunday acquires a grander design. Nightengale relays the reckoning of several executives: the Giants' best chance of unloading one of their weightiest contracts — Adames, Chapman or Devers — may be a package that carries the ace Webb out the door beside it. A trade of that magnitude is doubtful before the agreement expires on the first of December, but the Bay men could line something up for the season beyond; The Athletic has them wanting a very strong return for the workhorse, with rival shops skeptical they will get their price.7

THE PACIFIC DESK: ANATOMY OF THE LION'S FASTBALL — TAIRA, FIVE FEET EIGHT AND NINE-AND-NINETY ON THE GUN, LOOSES THE BALL FROM A HEIGHT ONLY ONE BIG-LEAGUE STARTER GOES BENEATH, AND THE LABOR CLOCK FORECLOSES THE EASTERN MARKET A FORTNIGHT EARLY

The wire's David Adler puts the instruments to the arm these columns have courted all week, and the readings explain the crowded anterooms. Kaima Taira, 27, late of the Seibu Lions and posted at the beginning of November, carries a career 1.78 earned-run average in the Japanese league with 9.4 strikeouts per nine innings, and throws eight distinct pitches; but the engine is the four-seamer, which averaged 95.8 miles an hour this past season, touched past 99, and sat 96 or better in eight of his final ten starts. The marvel is the geometry. Standing 5-foot-8, Taira releases the ball 4.94 feet off the ground — level with Chris Sale for the second-lowest slot among regular big-league starters, only Joe Ryan beneath him — and 6.8 feet out in front of the rubber, more extension than the big-league average from a man shorter than nearly all of them; Sale and Wheeler were the only major-league starters last season to pair a release under five feet with velocity past 95. The flat approach makes the pitch seem to take off as it arrives, and it drew whiffs at 27 percent against a league norm of about 22; it is, moreover, his chief two-strike weapon against both sides of the plate. The suitors on the wire's list — the Mets, the Mission nine, the Maple Leaf nine, the Cubs and the White Sox — are the same establishments whose audiences these columns have carried since the posting.8

The clock beneath the courtship has tightened. Should no new labor agreement exist when the current one dies on the first of December, the league will neither freeze nor extend the 45-day posting windows — which makes that date the effective deadline for Taira, for Itoh, and for the slugger Sato alike, and forecloses the mid-December horizon these columns gave Itoh's clock on Sunday. On Sato the field narrows besides: industry sources, by the wire's Mark Feinsand, now rate the Yankees, the Philadelphias and the Maple Leaf nine the frontrunners — the five-club field of yesterday's columns shorn of the Mets and Cubs — and The Athletic's Ken Rosenthal hears the signature could come very well before Thanksgiving, which is two mornings from this one.7

THE BULLPEN COLUMN: SMALL STROKES FOR THE LATE INNINGS — SUTER MAKES A THIRD LEFT HAND IN THE BRONX, THIELBAR ON THE EVE OF FORTY TALKS HIS WAY HOME TO KANSAS CITY, AND THE DODGERS BUY A LOTTERY TICKET ON A THRICE-MENDED ARM

The day's smaller ink ran chiefly to relief. The Yankees agreed with the left-hander Brent Suter on one year and $4 million, pending a physical — the insider Jeff Passan reporting a club option for 2028 besides — and the profile wants no translation: 37 years old, a season split between the Angels and Atlanta at a 3.36 figure across 91 innings, ground balls at a rate in the 85th percentile and hard contact suppressed at the 94th. He joins Headrick and Hill as the third left hand in the room, and brings the Bronx bullpen bill to roughly $41.4 million this winter across Bednar's accepted qualifying offer, Hill, the returned Loáisiga — whose homecoming Saturday's columns recorded — and now Suter; the steward Cashman's shop, by the Post's Joel Sherman, still deliberates whether to crown the project with Morejon, the market's best reliever, or spread that money across the roster's other wants.910

In Kansas City the signing came with its own testimony. The Royals announced one year for Caleb Thielbar — ESPN setting the wage at $3.25 million — and the left-hander, who turns 40 on the 31st of January, allowed that he and his wife had been looking at moving to the city and he had hoped the club would call. The figures argue both ways: a 4.86 for the Cubs this past season, but a 2.28 on the road against a home ledger battered at windy Wrigley, and the man reckons his fastball plays in Kauffman's thicker summer air. He joins a bullpen that ranked 29th in the game at 5.07 and a staff makeover a week in motion, the Garcia freight having lately brought Miller, Evans and Ferrer to town, as these columns have recorded; the new man calls the club a dark horse that a tough 2026 has let the public forget.1112

And the Dodgers spent the price of a minor-league contract on a memory. Robert Stephenson, who signed three years and $33 million with the Angels before 2024 and has thrown 10 big-league innings since — a Tommy John reconstruction, a year of nerve irritation, then a second elbow operation amid thoracic-outlet symptoms that swallowed his 2026 entire — agreed to minor-league terms, by a source's account to the wire's Mark Feinsand. At his best, with the 2023 Rays, the right-hander ran a 2.35 across 42 appearances; he turns 34 in February, and behind Díaz and Scott a bullpen shorn of Phillips, now a Brave, and of Vesia to the open market has every reason to gamble small on the chance that the best of him survives the scars.13

A Tuesday paper of consequences: the Queens spree finds a third signature, the floodgates foretold in yesterday's columns open on the North Side, and the bargainers' Monday sitting yields gilt the union weighs and finds wanting — the whole of it set down above. The forward ledger was opened before a word was written, as the house custom commands, and reads as it has all week: the camps' reporting dates remain unposted, so the count runs to the first rung bearing a date — the spring exhibition openers of Friday, the 19th of February, 87 mornings from this one, with Opening Night to follow on Wednesday, the 24th of March, 120 mornings off.2 The nearer columns crowd closer by the day: the collective agreement has 7 mornings left to live; Cooperstown's committee of sixteen reports in 12; the Winter Meetings convene at San Diego with the Rule 5 drafting on December 9, 15 mornings from this one; and the writers' envelope waits for the 19th of January, 56 mornings off. Until the morrow's dispatch, keep your scorecards near and your enthusiasms nearer.
