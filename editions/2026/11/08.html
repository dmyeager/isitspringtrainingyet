News Around the League

THE NURSERY DESK: THE UNDERSTUDY STEALS THE SHOW — PARKER OF THE TORONTO SCHOOLING, HANDED A DERBY INVITATION ON SHORT NOTICE, OUT-SLUGS THE AUTUMN FIELD WITH NINETEEN CIRCUIT CLOUTS AND CARRIES THE CROWN TO THE CATCHERS' ROOM

The Arizona Fall League's home-run derby, which these columns flagged Thursday among the week's appointments, went off Saturday night and handed its trophy to a man who was not on the original bill. Aaron Parker, a catcher of 23 in the Blue Jays' system, entered the field as a late substitute for the Dodgers' young prospect Chase Harlan, drew the seventh position of the eight, and proceeded to out-hit every invited guest: he led all entrants with 19 home runs in sum and beat the Cardinals' prospect Tai Peete in the final round by a count of 2 to 1.2

The power is no rumor newly minted. Parker struck a career-best 19 home runs this season — his first campaign of double figures as a professional, and precisely the number, the reader will smile to note, that he totaled across Saturday's rounds — and his autumn work for Mesa includes a drive measured at 451 feet.2 He joins a short and instructive roll of derby champions: the Pirates' giant Tony Blanco Jr. took last year's edition with an exhibition the desert still talks about, one ball leaving his bat at 122.9 mph.2

The main event follows directly: the Fall Stars Game, the autumn circuit's twentieth annual showcase, convenes this very evening at Salt River Fields at Talking Stick, 8 o'clock Eastern, with ten of the game's hundred best prospects on the two rosters and the Rangers' shortstop Sebastian Walcott at the head of the bill — the particulars as this paper laid them out Thursday.3 The nursery columns remind the reader why the exhibition earns its ink: last November's Fall Stars leadoff man was Kevin McGonigle, who spent this past week as a finalist for the Rookie of the Year.3

THE CHRISTENING DESK: BEFORE YOU ARE A YANKEE, YOU ARE A HIGHLANDER — THE BRONX MEN'S TOP FARM CLUB REACHES BACK TO 1903 FOR ITS NEW NAME, AND THE DEADBALL DESK PRONOUNCES IT THE WINTER'S FINEST SIGNING

A piece of news after this paper's own heart arrived Saturday from the anthracite country of Pennsylvania: the Yankees' Triple-A club, these fourteen seasons the Scranton/Wilkes-Barre RailRiders, has rechristened itself the Scranton/Wilkes-Barre Highlanders — the very name the parent franchise wore from 1903, when it came north from Baltimore, through 1912, in honor of its ballpark's perch on the high ground of upper Manhattan. "Before they were the Yankees, they were the Highlanders," said the club president Sean Reilly. "And now, once again, before you're a Yankee, you're a Highlander."4 The desk that still sets its type by the standards of that era declares the sentiment unimprovable.

The announcement was made a festival: an all-day affair at PNC Field with food trucks, live music and a drone show, the proceedings carried live on the Yankees' own network with the broadcaster Michael Kay as master of ceremonies, and the Yankee grandee Tino Martinez and the sometime manager Joe Maddon — a son of nearby Hazleton — among the guests. Highlanders is the fourth identity in the franchise's books, after the Red Barons of 1989 through 2006 (a Phillies affiliate in those days), the plain Yankees of 2007 through 2012, and the RailRiders since; the coming season is the club's twentieth as the Bronx men's top farm.4

The new livery splits its loyalties agreeably between the mountains and the Bronx. The primary mark is a bear in hiking kit shouldering a bat beneath a pinstriped sky — an homage, the club allows, to a genuine bear occasionally sighted in the ballpark's parking lot — with a campfire of bat-logs among the secondary marks, all from the Louisville design house that drew the Hudson Valley Renegades' pinstriped heavens besides; the home uniforms keep pinstripes, a Yankees sleeve patch and an interlocking SWB. The mascot Champ, a bleacher creature by species, stays on. And the clerks note two fine prints: at 32 characters the Scranton/Wilkes-Barre Highlanders own the longest name in professional baseball, and the club plays in neither Scranton nor Wilkes-Barre but in the town of Moosic. The Highlanders take the field March 26 against the Worcester Red Sox, with the home opener set for April 6.4

THE FAR PACIFIC DESK: A GUARDIAN OF THE OTHER OCEAN PUTS TO SEA — TSENG OF THE FIVE-AND-TWENTY SAVES IS POSTED FROM TAIWAN, HIS WINDOW OPEN THIS VERY MORNING AND SHUT TWO DAYS BEFORE CHRISTMAS, IF THE LOCKOUT DOES NOT SHUT IT FIRST

A week to the day since Teruaki Sato weighed anchor, the autumn's eastward traffic gains another vessel. Jyun-Yue Tseng, right-handed closer of Taiwan's Fubon Guardians — no kin to the Cleveland club of that name, whose November is passing in a Brooklyn courtroom elsewhere in these columns — is entering the posting system, and the thirty clubs may treat with him from this very Sunday until December 22.5

The ledger travels well. Tseng is 25, and this season in the Chinese Professional Baseball League he went 5-3 with 25 saves across 48 games, striking out 65 men against 13 walks in 52 and one-third innings. The international stage has seen him twice: in this year's World Baseball Classic he threw hitless innings against South Korea and the Czech Republic and banked a save, his four-seam fastball averaging 93.7 mph against the Koreans with a cutter alongside. The résumé runs back further than most — he pitched in the Little League World Series of 2014, and was the CPBL's Rookie of the Year in 2021.5

The counting-house terms are the standard tariff — a signing club owes his Taiwan employers a posting fee starting at a fifth of the first 25 millions of any major-league contract — but the calendar is the clause to watch. The collective agreement dies on the 1st of December with a lockout widely expected, and the freeze may cut Tseng's negotiating window short by three weeks; the winter's every transaction now runs ahead of that same weather, as the Herald's labor columns have had steady occasion to observe.5

THE COURTROOM DESK: 'THERE WAS NO MORE INFORMATION LIKE THAT' — A COOPERATING BETTOR SAYS THE INSIDE WORD ON CLASE'S PITCHES DRIED UP WHEN THE MIZUHARA SCANDAL BROKE, AND THE WEEK'S TESTIMONY CLOSES ON THE QUESTION OF THE CHICKENS

The pitch-rigging trial of Emmanuel Clase and Luis Ortiz, whose Thursday session these columns reported Friday, closed its week with the testimony of Luis de Aza Baez, a cooperating government witness who told the Brooklyn jury he placed dozens of bets and won thousands of dollars on the velocity of selected Clase pitches in 2023 "because I had inside information." The word came never from Clase himself, he said, but from Enoc Pena, an associate of the pitcher's, often through a group chat titled "Uber Inc." — its members, like the witness, ride-share drivers in Massachusetts at the time. De Aza Baez opened a betting account in his wife's name to raise his limits and folded the pitch wagers into parlays to fatten the payouts.6

The detail that lifts the day's testimony into the historical record is where the well ran dry. When he asked for more such information in 2024, De Aza Baez testified, he was told that because of "what was going on with Ohtani, there was no more information like that" — the season the sporting world learned that millions had moved from Shohei Ohtani's bank account to an illegal bookmaking operation, a theft of nearly 17 millions to which the interpreter Ippei Mizuhara later pleaded guilty and for which he now sits in prison, Ohtani himself cleared of all wrongdoing. One scandal's scrutiny, by this account, starved another.6

The jury was also shown a text message Clase is alleged to have sent Pena on September 30, 2023, speaking of "killing the first two chickens" — instructions, the witness said, to bet on a Clase pitch to an inning's third batter, but only if the first two had been retired; Clase did not appear in that day's game, and the defense maintains that roosters and chickens in such messages often referred to cockfighting, a legal pastime in the Dominican Republic. De Aza Baez acknowledged he will forfeit $69,000 in winnings and testify truthfully in exchange for the government dropping a wire-fraud charge against him. "This is a case about big talk and small bets," said Todd Spodek, counsel to the co-defendant Robinson Vásquez Germosen, after the session. The proceedings resume Thursday; the Herald's practice is unchanged — the allegations are reported as allegations, and the verdict is left to the jury.6

THE INFIRMARY WARD AND THE INK DESK: CARROLL OF THE SONORAN NINE HAS HIS ELBOW SWEPT CLEAN AND IS PROMISED FOR THE SPRING — THE METROPOLITANS KEEP THEIR MENDED WANDERER STOCK FOR ANOTHER YEAR, AND STITCH A SLEEVE FOR KOOSMAN

From the desert comes news of a tidying rather than a repair: Corbin Carroll underwent surgery this week to remove loose bodies from his right elbow, the Diamondbacks confirming the operation Friday, with full recovery reckoned at about eight weeks — which lands him in the new year with a month and more to spare before the first exhibitions of February 19.71 The season behind him was a catalogue of endurance: a .239 average with 22 home runs, 78 runs driven in, 33 doubles and 17 three-base hits — the most in his league — compiled around a hamate operation that cost him most of the spring and a back that nagged him through the second half. Carroll is 26, a three-time All-Star, the National League's Rookie of the Year of 2023, and the holder, since the month's turn, of the first Gold Glove of his career in right field, as these columns recorded when Rawlings read its verdicts.7

In Queens, the ink desk logs a smaller but sweeter signature: the Mets have re-signed the right-hander Robert Stock, who turns 37 this month, to a one-year contract. Stock's 2026 ran to seven starts and a relief turn — 1-3, with a 5.93 earned-run average over 30 and one-third innings — but it held one evening worth the whole ledger: on August 31 at Tampa Bay he threw five and one-third scoreless innings in a 3-2 decision, his first big-league victory since 2019. Numbness in his fingers, a souvenir of March surgery for thoracic outlet syndrome, shelved him in mid-September; the man has pitched for eight big-league organizations, in South Korea and Mexico, and for the independent Long Island Ducks, and his persistence has now out-lasted the ailment's patience.8

The same club sewed a graver stitch: the Mets announced Thursday that their 2027 uniforms will carry a memorial sleeve patch for Jerry Koosman, the left arm of the Miracle Mets, who died a week ago this morning at 83 and for whom this paper lowered its colors on Monday last.8 The patch is a small thing as cloth goes; the memory it rides on is not.

The Sabbath's ledger runs lighter than the week's and none the worse for it: a derby won by an understudy, a christening in the anthracite country that this desk will be savoring for days, a new sail on the Pacific horizon, and a courtroom gone quiet until Thursday. The forward ledger, ruled open before a word of this was set, stands exactly where Saturday left it — nothing on the card before the spring exhibitions of Friday, the 19th of February, 103 mornings distant, with Opening Night 136 mornings off on Wednesday, the 24th of March.1 Nearer at hand the week fills to the brim: the Fall Stars convene at Salt River Fields this very evening;3 the writers begin reading their award verdicts on the morrow, the rookies summoned first and the Most Valuable men to follow Thursday; the general managers convene at Las Vegas in the week now opening, as these columns noted Thursday; the 17th keeps its double appointment, qualifying answers and silver bats alike, nine mornings hence; and the collective agreement has 23 mornings left to live. Until the morrow's dispatch, keep your scorecards near and your enthusiasms nearer.
