News Around the League

THE PACIFIC DESK, SWEPT BARE: THE LAST GREAT LOT OF THE EASTERN SHELF GOES TO WRIGLEY — ITOH JOINS THE CUBS THE SAME DAY SATO AND ROGERS TURN OFFICIAL, AND TAKAHASHI, WHO NEEDED NO POSTING BELL AT ALL, TAKES HIS GROUND BALLS TO THE DESERT FOR THREE MILLIONS

The republic calls the day after its feast a shopping holiday, and the Cubs kept it like a sacrament. On Friday morning the club made official its two $120 million purchases — the left-hander Trevor Rogers for five years, the slugger Teruaki Sato for six — and by Friday night the wires carried a third stroke: the Wrigley establishment has reached agreement with the right-hander Hiromi Itoh, the finest arm left on the eastern shelf, on terms not yet known, pending a physical, the club not confirming. These columns left the man Wednesday as the shelf's last great lot, "lately seen calling in Chicago, his window shorter than ever" — the window has now closed, and it closed exactly where the paper last saw him standing.3

He is 29, six seasons a Hokkaido Nippon-Ham Fighter, and comes recommended less by the radar gun than by the finer instruments: a deep arsenal, a praised feel for spin and the shaping of pitches — the wire reaches, carefully, for comparisons to Yu Darvish — and command of the first order. This past season he pitched to a 3.18 earned-run average across 172 and two-thirds innings, 159 strikeouts against 34 walks and a walk rate of 4.7 percent; the year before, he took the Sawamura Award, the eastern game's answer to the Cy Young, at 2.52 with 195 strikeouts. The fit is the plainest thing about the purchase. The Cubs' pitching shop prizes exactly this blend of command, spin and durability, the defense behind him is stocked with Gold Glove-caliber men, and the rotation wants bodies: all five men who finished the season in Chicago's rotation walked to the market, and of the arms that remain only Assad returns from a season unmarred, with Steele and Garrett mending from elbow surgery, Cabrera from blisters and a leg, and Brown and Horton not expected until the new season is well grown. Rogers and Itoh would front the room; the club plans its introductions early in the week, and the newest man may share the dais.3

The desert, meanwhile, shopped an aisle nobody was watching. The Sonoran nine agreed with the right-hander Kona Takahashi on one year and $3 million, by the account of multiple reports — a man who needed no posting bell, having been posted a winter ago alongside his Seibu stablemate Tatsuya Imai and, when Imai took $54 million from Houston and no offer suited him, gone home on a contract whose opt-outs now deliver him to the open market unrestricted.5 He turns 30 in February and has thrown 1,337 and a third innings for the Lions since debuting at 18, all in the one uniform, at a career 3.30. The fastball sits in the low 90s and the trade is the ground ball — better than half his outs by that route for years, and nearer 60 percent this past season, when he pitched to a 2.54 with a career-best strikeout rate besides. Arizona pencils him into the contest for the back of a rotation fronted by Burnes, Rodríguez, Kelly and Pfaadt, the club hunting its first October since 2023.4 And the reader keeping the eastern ledger may now close it: Sato, Taira, Itoh and Takahashi — the winter's whole Pacific freight, landed inside of four days.

THE INK DESK: TORONTO BUYS A YEAR OF JAZZ — CHISHOLM, EIGHT-AND-TWENTY, TAKES TWENTY MILLIONS WRAPPED IN A LABYRINTH OF OPTIONS, THE MAPLE LEAF NINE DREAM OF SEMIEN'S PRECEDENT, AND THE QUEUE THE HERALD HAS KEPT ALL WEEK RESOLVES IN CANADA

The queue these columns have kept behind Jazz Chisholm Jr. since the GM Meetings — eleven clubs for conversation; the Jays, the Mets and the Nationals for intent — resolves in Canada. The Blue Jays agreed Friday with the second baseman on a contract of one year and $20 million, by a source's account, the club not confirming — this only hours after making official the five-year, $80 million purchase of the right-hander Kaima Taira, whose formal introduction comes Monday at the Rogers Centre.6

The paper is short but the clockwork inside it is not. The $20 million assembles from a 2027 wage of $12.5 million, a $6 million bonus, and a $1.5 million buyout of a player option for 2028 set at $16 million — and should the man decline that option, it transforms into a club option at $40 million, so that the two years together could pay him $58.5 million. Which is to say: Toronto has bought a year of upside and priced every outcome of it. The upside is real. He is 28, coming off a down Bronx year — .231 with 19 home runs and a .711 on-base-plus-slugging — that still carried 41 stolen bases, a strong glove and his customary voltage; he plays second chiefly, third and the outfield at need. The Toronto desk reaches at once for the house precedent: Marcus Semien, who came north on one year off a down season and struck 45 home runs with an .873 on-base-plus-slugging in 2021. That is a high bar, but motivation should want for nothing, with the open market waiting again as early as next winter.6

The addition firms an infield without unseating anyone so much as redeploying him: Ernie Clement, whose 2026 began hot and faded, is read not as demoted but as loosed upon all four infield posts and perhaps a corner of grass besides, with the platoon arithmetic — Chisholm long the better hitter against right-handers — writing the daily lineups. The want that remains is the one the whole league knows. No club in the majors scored fewer runs last season, the outfield is still open for business, and Suzuki remains the clean fit these columns have carried for days, with Arozarena likewise at liberty. A year of swagger is a fine start; the batting order wants a broadside yet.6

THE YOUTH DESK: QUEENS PAPERS ITS MORNING STAR THROUGH 2035 — EWING, TWO-AND-TWENTY, TAKES EIGHT YEARS AND FIVE-AND-NINETY MILLIONS, HIS FIVE-AND-TWENTY STOLEN BASES THE GRANDEST ROOKIE HAUL IN METROPOLITAN ANNALS

The Metropolitans, whose winter has run to the grandest purchases — Skubal's record $450 million among them, as these columns have chronicled — spent Friday securing the smallest wage on their books. The center fielder A.J. Ewing, 22 years old and seven months a big-leaguer, finalized an extension of eight years and $95 million, and with it the club holds him through 2035 — four seasons past the free agency the current rules would have granted him after 2032, with arbitration due after 2028. The Herald notes the timing without comment, save to observe that "the current rules" is a phrase with 3 mornings left on its own contract.7

The season that earned the paper was the bright spot of a dim year at Flushing: a May debut, then a .252 average with 11 home runs, 43 driven in and a .700 on-base-plus-slugging across 120 games, a sharp eye at the plate, a strong finish after a summer slump — and 25 bases stolen in 33 tries, a franchise record for a rookie. He made 96 starts in center field, took the corners and even second base at need, and with Benge in right and Soto in left he completes an outfield the club means to grow old slowly. The team around its three prize rookies — Ewing, Benge and the pitcher McLean — finished 74-88 and last in its division for the first time in 23 years, a record $358 million Opening Day payroll notwithstanding: perhaps the plainest argument yet printed that pennants are bought by the year but built by the decade. Ewing was a fourth-round selection in 2023, out of a high school in Springboro, Ohio.7

THE EXCHANGE DESK: THE WEEK THE WIRE GAVE KING RUNS TO ITS LAST GRAINS — SAN DIEGO IS CALLED 'MOST FOCUSED' ON ITS OLD ACE'S RETURN, THE MOUND CITY HUNTS THE MIDDLE SHELF, HALF A DOZEN CLUBS RING FOR VESIA, AND WOODRUFF, RISING FOUR-AND-THIRTY, DECLARES HE IS NOT DONE

Michael King remains at liberty, and the week the wire allotted him — these columns have carried Morosi's prophecy of a signature "before the week is out" since Wednesday — is down to its last grains with the pen still dry. The likeliest desk only grows likelier: the Mission nine, whose entire rotation save Vásquez walked to the market at once, are reported by the wire's AJ Cassavell to want their old right-hander home, and the Post's Jon Heyman calls them "most focused on" King — a man who has himself spoken warmly of a return — with the All-Star Nick Martinez, 3.10 for the Rays this past season, under consideration besides. The same club is tied to both Arozarena and Suzuki — ESPN's Passan has San Diego as perhaps "the favorites" on the latter, having lately hosted him — and its surplus of catchers draws the Pinstripes, who are thought to want Fermin or Campusano as right-handed company for Wells.8

The Mound City nine, quiet while the market roared, are reported shopping the middle shelf in earnest: the Post-Dispatch's Goold has them checked in on Imanaga, Bieber and Mize, and Morosi — whose King prophecy stands above, at hazard — adds a fourth name, saying he would believe "Nick Pivetta or a comparable starting pitcher" signs in St. Louis in the days to come. The left-handed relief market stirs behind Morejon meanwhile: some half a dozen clubs, by The Athletic's Kuty, are ringing for Alex Vesia — a 2.64 across 72 games for the Angelenos last season, 74 strikeouts in 58 innings on a fastball that rises out of an over-the-top slot, and since 2021 a 2.67 that stands tied for fourth-lowest among the game's qualified left-handed relievers.8

In Chicago the morning's shopping does not end the list: the Bruins are still expected to add starting pitching beyond Itoh, and The Athletic's Sharma and Mooney observe that Sato's six-year paper makes the club's deep shelf of position prospects — six of its top eight — into trade capital, with the wire naming the familiar sellers: Webb available by the Bay, the Pinstripes long on arms and short on bats, Texas listening on deGrom, Eovaldi and Gore, the Gulf Coast club on McClanahan and Jax — the same window this paper dressed only yesterday.8 And from Milwaukee, word that the oldest storyline on the shelf intends another chapter: Brandon Woodruff, 34 next season and healthy entering a winter for the first time since 2022, declares "I'm going to keep playing" — talks open with clubs, "the Brewers being one of them." The man took a single week's rest after pitching the final inning of the World Series' Game 3 rout and standing a champion two games later; he throws now off a mound built at his Mississippi home, his newborn son nearby, and the Herald — which carried his small, gleaming ledger in yesterday's inventory, 110 innings at 3.11 across two mended years — wishes the shoulder a quiet winter.9

THE INK DESK, SMALLER TYPE: THE DESERT TRADES FOR A FIRST-OVERALL GLOVE, SENDING TAWA TO PITTSBURGH FOR DAVIS — AND THE CUBS HAND COUNSELL ANOTHER 'OUT-GETTER' IN THE SOFT-CONTACT ARTIST YARBROUGH

Arizona's Friday ran to volume. Besides the Takahashi signing above, the desert establishment traded for its backup catcher, sending the utilityman Tim Tawa to Pittsburgh for Henry Davis — first overall in the whole draft of 2021, out of Louisville, and still only 27. The bat has never answered the billing: a .172 average and a .585 on-base-plus-slugging this past season, a third consecutive year below .200, though with career highs in home runs (10) and runs driven in (38). The glove is another matter entirely — across the past two seasons no regular catcher in the majors has thrown out base-stealers at a better rate, 35.6 percent, ahead of Contreras and Bailey, and only Contreras betters his 3.54 catcher's earned-run figure — and it is the glove Arizona bought, to stand behind Gabriel Moreno, the league's reigning batting champion. Tawa, an 11th-round man of the same draft, gave Arizona 178 games at every post but shortstop, catcher and the mound, batting .240 this past season; he crosses to the Smoky City men in exchange.10

And late Friday the Cubs added to the bullpen a man built to Counsell's specification: Ryan Yarbrough, 34 and left-handed, agreed on one year, terms undisclosed, the club not confirming. The manager famously keeps no titles in his relief room — "out-getters," he calls them all — and Yarbrough's whole career is the job description: 57 relief appearances and ten starts for the Pinstripes across the past two seasons at better than two innings an outing, a 3.69 this past year across 63 and a third innings, and contact so soft the instruments nearly refuse to grade it, the 100th percentile of the whole league in both average exit velocity and hard-hit rate and the 96th in barrels, off a five-pitch spread. Nine seasons, five clubs, a 4.18 in 253 games: the role has never had a name, and he has never needed one.11

THE WINTER CIRCUIT: THE FIRST SWING BACK — CASAS, A YEAR AND A HALF IN THE SURGEONS' LEDGER, RETURNS IN DOMINICAN FLANNELS AND CLEARS THE BASES AT SANTO DOMINGO

The winter circuits these columns commended to the armchair only yesterday wasted no time repaying the endorsement. At Estadio Quisqueya Juan Marichal in Santo Domingo on Friday night, Triston Casas of the Red Sox — who ruptured the patellar tendon of his left knee on the 2nd of May, 2025, and then lost the whole of 2026 besides to abdominal and wrist complaints in the mending — stepped in for his first plate appearance with the Tigres del Licey, his first in that league since the winter after his big-league debut in 2022, and with the count 0-2 put a changeup from the Gigantes' Bryan Rodriguez over the right-field barrier with the bases full: a grand slam, and a 5-0 lead before the first inning was done.12

The sentiment writes itself; the ledger is colder. Casas is 26, owns a career .241 average with a .348 on-base figure, a .452 slugging mark and 45 home runs across 952 plate appearances, and returns to a Fenway establishment that has meanwhile installed Contreras at first base and signed Jake Bauers this very month — which is why the wire ranks him as likely trade matter even as he rounds the bases. The Herald, for one morning, declines that arithmetic and simply marks the scene: a year and a half and better of surgeons' schedules, one pitch out of the hand, and the full tour of the bases at the end of it.12

No rest in Saturday's ledger: the nation made a shopping holiday of the feast's morrow and the ballclubs kept it faithfully — the eastern shelf sold out to its walls, Toronto wagered a year on Jazz, Queens papered its morning star through 2035, and a first baseman a year and a half in the mending announced himself with one swing at Santo Domingo — the whole of it set down above. The forward ledger was read before any word was set, by the house custom, and stands unmoved: the camps' reporting dates remain unposted, so the count runs to the first rung bearing a date — the spring exhibition openers of Friday, the 19th of February, 83 mornings from this one, with Opening Night to follow on Wednesday, the 24th of March, 116 mornings off.2 The nearer columns: the collective agreement has 3 mornings left to live; Cooperstown's committee of sixteen reports in 8; the Winter Meetings convene at San Diego with the Rule 5 drafting on December 9, 11 mornings from this one; and the writers' envelope waits for the 19th of January, 52 mornings off. Until the morrow's dispatch, keep your scorecards near and your enthusiasms nearer.
