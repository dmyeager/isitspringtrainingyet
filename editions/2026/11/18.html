Volume I · No. 133 · Wednesday, the Eighteenth of November, Two Thousand and Twenty-Six

No contests this day; the hot stove burns bright.

93 days until the first spring-training game (Friday, the 19th of February, 2027).

News Around the League

THE DAY OF ANSWERS RENDERS ITS VERDICTS: TWO YESES AND FIVE NOES AT THREE-AND-TWENTY MILLIONS THE QUESTION — BEDNAR STAYS A YANKEE AND JEFFERS A TWIN, WHILE AROZARENA, LOWE, SUZUKI, ROGERS AND MOREJON TAKE THEIR CHANCES WITH THE TOLL

The seven answers owed by four o'clock arrived on schedule, and the ledger closed by evening: two yeses, five noes. David Bednar remains a Yankee and Ryan Jeffers a Twin, each taking the certain $23.15 million for 2027; Randy Arozarena, Brandon Lowe, Seiya Suzuki, Trevor Rogers and Adrian Morejon declined, and go to market carrying the draft-pick toll — joining Jake Bauers, who answered early and in Boston's ink, and whose old employers in Milwaukee duly collect a selection between the first round's end and the compensation round for their trouble.9 History held nearly to form: of the 157 men tendered qualifying offers before this winter, just 18 had ever accepted, roughly 11 in a hundred, and this year's eight produced two.2

The Bronx's yes was the closer's. Bednar, 32, whom the general manager Brian Cashman calls "a cool customer," stays installed in the ninth inning of the bullpen that was the club's difference-maker this past season — and it is the second winter running that a Yankee has taken the tender, the outfielder Trent Grisham having done so a year ago. The books justify the keeping: since his arrival from Pittsburgh in July 2025 the man has pitched to a 2.39 earned-run average with 45 saves across 86 appearances, and this past season he saved 35 games — the most by any Yankee since Chapman's 37 in 2019 — with a 2.47 figure over a career-high 69 and a third innings, surrendering no run in 37 of his final 43 outings. With the submariner Hill already re-inked, as Monday's columns recorded, the winter's declared first priority is being answered largely from within.3

Minnesota's yes came with a flourish of loyalty. "I didn't want to close the chapter on the Minnesota Twins," said Jeffers, the best catcher on the market, whose reported suitors included both New York establishments; he returns instead to the only organization he has known, off a .256/.353/.461 season in 369 plate appearances around a broken hamate bone, and the rulebook holds a quiet sweetener besides — a man may be tendered but once in his career, so his next trip to market carries no toll at all.4 The ripples spread at once: Carson Kelly and Tyler Stephenson become the best catchers at liberty, and Morejon stands alone as the prize of the relief shelf — a man who means to stay in relief work, with a San Diego reunion reckoned strong and the Yankees, Mets, Cubs and Giants in the mix besides.2

THE SILVER BATS FIND THIRTEEN NEW HANDS, THE MOST IN TWENTY YEARS — TWO ROOKIES TAKE SILVER TOGETHER FOR ONLY THE THIRD TIME SINCE THE AWARD WAS STRUCK, THE ASTROS AND CUBS CLAIM THE TEAM PRIZES, AND THE TENTH BAT ELUDES TROUT ANOTHER YEAR

The silverware promised for Tuesday evening arrived on the hour, and the engraver met more new names than he has in twenty years: thirteen of the twenty silver bats went to first-time winners, and two of those to rookies — Kevin McGonigle of Detroit at the utility station and Chase DeLauter of Cleveland in the garden — only the third time since the award was struck in 1980 that two freshmen have taken silver in one year, the prior company being Ichiro and Pujols in 2001 and Nokes and Santiago in 1987. McGonigle collected his beside the Rookie of the Year laurel he already owns, and the Bengals claimed three bats in all, the catcher Dillon Dingler and the outfielder Riley Greene joining him.5

The American roll besides: Pete Alonso, whose 43 home runs led the league, takes a second bat in his first Baltimore year; Chase Meidroth becomes the first White Sox second baseman ever to win one; Junior Caminero, 23, adds silver to a pennant; Bobby Witt Jr. takes a third at shortstop at all of 26; Arozarena, Greene and DeLauter hold the outfield; and Yordan Alvarez — lately laureled the league's Most Valuable, as these columns have recorded — takes the designated hitter's prize off a season that led every qualified bat in the game in average, on-base and slugging alike.5 In the National: Gabriel Moreno of Arizona, whose .311 made him the fifth catcher ever to win a batting title and the first since Buster Posey in 2012 — the same Posey whose name went to the writers only Monday, as yesterday's lead recorded; Matt Olson and his 41 home runs; Lowe, the first Pirate so honored since McCutchen in 2015; Elly De La Cruz, a 30-30 man at last and owner of the best on-base-plus-slugging of any qualified shortstop; Alex Bregman, in his first North Side year; the outfield of Pete Crow-Armstrong — the unanimous Most Valuable, seventh member of the 40-40 fraternity, league-best .943 on-base-plus-slugging — with James Wood and the 22-year-old champion Jackson Chourio beside him; Kyle Schwarber and his 45 home runs at designated hitter; and Bauers, the utility man, adding a silver bat to his October laurels. The team prizes went to the Astros, whose 226 home runs led their league, and the Cubs, whose 850 runs led the whole of the Majors.5

The pursuit this paper carried through the week ended a rung short: Mike Trout, a finalist in the American outfield for what would have been a tenth silver bat — a shelf where only Piazza, with ten, and Bonds, with twelve, keep house — watched the three outfield prizes go to Arozarena, Greene and DeLauter, and the old master must wait another winter.65 The day's finest crossing of ledgers, though, belongs to the almanac: Arozarena and Lowe each took silver within hours of declining a certain $23.15 million — two men betting on themselves with fresh hardware under the arm — and the reader may judge the spirit of the wager by the lead column above.

THE INK DESK: THE WINTER'S FIRST STARTING ARM LANDS ON THE SOUTH SIDE — MAY SIGNS WITH THE PALE HOSE FOR THREE YEARS AND FIFTY-TWO MILLIONS AND A HALF, AND DETROIT MAKES ITS PAGÁN PURCHASE OFFICIAL AT ELEVEN AND A HALF

The starters' market has its first signature. Dustin May — an arm these columns lately called the most democratically courted of the winter after Skubal himself — has agreed with the White Sox on a contract of three years and $52.5 million, by the account of a source, the club not yet confirming; it is the first starting-pitching strike of consequence this offseason.79 The buyers know what they are purchasing: a right-hander of 29 whose career has run from celebrated Dodger prospect and champion of 2020 through the surgeon's whole catalogue — the Tommy John operation, an esophageal tear, a lost 2024 — to two healthy seasons since, the latest a career-high 145 and two-thirds innings split between St. Louis and Milwaukee, where he was fetched at the deadline and served the eventual champions in October as starter and reliever alike.7

The fit is a club on the rise and short of arms. The South Siders won 84 games this past season, entered the playoffs as the sixth seed, swept the Astros in the Wild Card round and won the first two games of the Division Series — with Sean Burke employed as very nearly the lone starter — before Cleveland took three straight; depth was the plainest need on the premises. May joins a rotation picture of Burke, Davis Martin, Luis Castillo, the young Noah Schultz and Grant Taylor, the last moving from the closer's post to starting work in 2027, and the steward Chris Getz is not finished: Freddy Peralta, Clay Holmes and Patrick Sandoval are among the free agents of interest, and the club eyes Kaima Taira, the 27-year-old posted from Japan off a 1.36 earned-run average that led his league.7

Detroit, meanwhile, put its ink to paper. The Tigers made official a contract of one year and $11.5 million, with another million obtainable in bonuses, for the veteran closer Emilio Pagán — who declined his $10 million Cincinnati option on Halloween, whereupon the club president Scott Harris, by his own telling, "picked up the phone and called his agent immediately." The second half of Pagán's season recommends him: back from a hamstring strain in July, he converted all 19 of his save chances with a 1.88 earned-run average and 34 strikeouts against 5 walks, and he inherits the ninth-inning post lately held by Kenley Jansen. The Bengals may count themselves doubly fortunate this morning, for one of the market's few proven closers took himself off it at four o'clock, as the lead column attests.8

THE EXCHANGE DESK: TRADE WINDS BLOW STRAIGHT THROUGH THE LABOR CLOUDS — TEXAS LISTENS ON GORE, THE DURAN MARKET DRAWS ITS MAP, THE YANKEES RING AROZARENA'S PEOPLE, AND THE MEGILL DRUM BEATS A THIRD MORNING RUNNING

The industry's operating theory this winter holds that men with a single year of control left will be hard to move, the 2027 season being clouded by the collective agreement's uncertainty — and the telephones ring regardless. The Athletic reports Texas fielding interest from multiple clubs on the left-hander MacKenzie Gore, acquired from Washington only last offseason at a price of five prospects and now entering his final year of arbitration; the costly veterans Seager, deGrom and Eovaldi are discussed as well, though their wages make heavier freight. Logan Gilbert of Seattle, Nick Lodolo of Cincinnati and the Cream City pair of William Contreras and Trevor Megill draw inquiries besides — the Megill drum having now beaten in these columns three mornings running — while Rasmussen, Ryan and Alcantara sit in the same expiring class but behind doors their clubs have signaled shut.9

In Boston the Duran question has advanced from whether to where. The Bauers purchase filled the last seat in the Fenway garden — Anthony, Rafaela and Abreu hold the outfield, with Bauers and Willson Contreras to rotate between first base and the hitter's chair — and Jarren Duran, 30, coming off a lean .627 season but worth 13.5 wins by FanGraphs' ledger across 2023 to 2025, grows dearer with each arbitration winter. The map of suitors, as MassLive draws it: Houston has shown the heaviest past interest, though a new steward there muddies the reading; San Diego, long an admirer, is doing its background work again; Seattle and Philadelphia make their diligence; and Arizona and the Angels are named as sensible fits. The reader will recall the wire's thirty-club exercise naming Duran for Boston only Monday — the timetable, evidently, advances.9

And the Yankees, their closer question answered, have checked in on Arozarena, the insider Jon Heyman counting them among the clubs to sit with the newly silvered outfielder's camp at last week's meetings. The appeal is arithmetical: only Cleveland and Washington gave more plate appearances to left-handed swingers this past season, and with Judge held to 66 games, the club's right-handed hitters produced a .709 on-base-plus-slugging in sum — while Arozarena, off a 148 OPS+ season of 26 home runs and 26 steals, answers the description exactly. The Orioles and Phillies keep their own interest. As for the winter's grandest courtship, the register merely thickens: at least six clubs are scheduled to sit with Skubal — the Mets, whose Monday audience was reported to have gone very well, with the Padres, Jays and Dodgers also on the list and two mystery establishments besides.9

THE PROSPECT FIELDS: THE FORTY-MAN DOORS SWING SHUT ON RULE 5 EVE — FIFTY-THREE RANKED PROSPECTS IN A HUNDRED AND THIRTY-ONE ARE TUCKED SAFELY ONTO ROSTERS, ALL EIGHT OF THE TOP HUNDRED AMONG THEM, AND THE DECEMBER DRAFTING WAITS ON THE LABOR WEATHER

Tuesday evening brought the quieter of the day's deadlines: by six o'clock every club had set its 40-man roster, and every eligible man left outside the door became fair game for the Rule 5 drafting, appointed for December 9 at the Winter Meetings in San Diego. The almanac notes with a raised eyebrow that the drafting is scheduled for the week after the collective agreement expires, and the wire allows the event could be disturbed if no new accord is reached by then — even the minor calendar, it seems, now consults the labor forecast. The mechanics are unchanged: $100,000 buys a selection, the man must keep a big-league seat the whole season through, and $50,000 offers him back if he cannot.10

The count ran true to recent form: of 131 ranked prospects requiring a decision, 53 were protected, roughly two in five, and all eight eligibles from the game's Top 100 were gathered in without exception — Christian Zazueta of the Dodgers the highest-ranked at No. 33, Washington protecting both Travis Sykora and Jarlin Susana, the Mets adding Jefferson Rojas, and Demetrio Crisantes, Josue Briceño, Nolan Perry and Bryan Rincon tucked away besides — extending a perfect record now twelve years long in which no Top-100 man has ever been left exposed. The Herald notes two names with recent business in these pages: Carter Graham, whose cry of "Best team in the nation, baby!" rang over the Fall League final in Sunday's columns, got his roster spot from Cincinnati, and the champions of Milwaukee found room for the young infielder Jett Williams.10

THE WINTER ISLANDS: VLADDY IS TRADED — COMPOSE YOURSELF, TORONTO, IT IS ONLY THE WINTER LEAGUE — GUERRERO GOES FROM THE TOROS TO LICEY, WHERE HIS FATHER TOOK THE LAST AT-BATS OF A HALL OF FAME CAREER

A headline to stop the Canadian heart crossed the wire Tuesday night: Vladimir Guerrero Jr., traded. The particulars restore the pulse — it is the Dominican Winter League, where the Toros del Este have sent the first baseman's rights to the Tigres del Licey for the relievers Misael Tamárez and Cam Robinson, and the Blue Jays are touched not at all. The island clubs hold a big leaguer's winter rights whether he suits up or no, and Guerrero has not played a LIDOM inning since the winter of 2020-21, when he took the field partly to press his old case for third base; his rights have traveled without him since, once fetching no less a personage than José Ramírez in a 2023 exchange.11

Whether the man himself appears for Licey is unsettled, but the romance of the thing writes itself: his father took the final at-bats of a Hall of Fame career in the Tigres' colors in the winter of 2012-13, at the Estadio Quisqueya in Santo Domingo, and the son now holds the option of batting where the father bowed out. The island season runs through December 22, and the Herald will keep an ear to the water.11

For once the almanac's promises were kept to the hour — answers at four o'clock, silverware by evening — and both accountings stand rendered in the columns above, with the forty-man doors shut and a winter-island curiosity besides. The forward ledger was consulted before a word was written, per the house custom, and tells the story it has told all week: the camps' reporting dates remain unposted, so the count runs to the first rung bearing a date — the spring exhibition openers of Friday, the 19th of February, 93 mornings from this one, with Opening Night to follow on Wednesday, the 24th of March, 126 mornings off.1 Nearer at hand the book thickens rather than thins: the collective agreement has 13 mornings left to live, Cooperstown's committee of sixteen reports in 18, the Winter Meetings convene at San Diego with the Rule 5 drafting on December 9, 21 mornings from this one, and the writers' envelope waits for January 19, 62 mornings off. The stove, in short, is fully lit. Until the morrow's dispatch, keep your scorecards near and your enthusiasms nearer.
