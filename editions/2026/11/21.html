News Around the League

THE FIVE O'CLOCK RECKONING: FOUR-AND-FORTY MEN ARE LOOSED UPON THE MARKET IN A SINGLE AFTERNOON — A ROOKIE OF THE YEAR AMONG THEM, MINNESOTA CUTS THREE AT A STROKE, AND SIX CLUBS KEEP EVERY HAND THEY HOLD

Five o'clock came and went on Friday, and when the hour had passed, 44 men who woke that morning under club control went to bed free agents, their employers having declined to tender them contracts for 2027. Six clubs — the Angels, the Athletics, the Brewers, the Marlins, the Orioles and the White Sox — kept every hand they held; the other twenty-four did their cutting, and the headline casualty wore pinstripes. Luis Gil, Rookie of the Year in the American League only two autumns ago — 15-7 with a 3.50 figure and 171 strikeouts across 29 starts in 2024, the first Yankee pitcher to take the honor since Dave Righetti in 1981 — was let go along with the reliever Fernando Cruz, the body having lately kept none of the promises the arm once made. A lat strain cost Gil the first four months of 2025, shoulder trouble better than three months of this past season, and his nine appearances of 2026 produced a 4.61 figure in 27 and a third innings; the steward Brian Cashman pointed to the depth of his pitching staff, and three seasons of club control went into the waste-basket with the man. The reader of these columns' island correspondence will relish the address on his winter mail: Gil is pitching even now for the Tigres del Licey, the same establishment that holds Vladimir Guerrero Jr.'s rights and the Águilas' new manager in its headlights. Cruz, for his part, goes to market with a partially torn elbow ligament he means to mend without the knife, and a splitter that struck out 139 men in 102 and two-thirds innings over his two Bronx seasons.345

The heaviest single stroke fell in Minnesota, where the front office that spent Wednesday buying Sonny Gray spent Friday subtracting: Bailey Ober, Trevor Larnach and Matt Wallner, all cut loose at once, on the same afternoon the club traded Royce Lewis — of which more in the column below. Ober is the name that answers Thursday's arithmetic, when these pages counted nine rotation names for five posts and reckoned a sale the likeliest relief; the ledger chose the simpler instrument. The big right-hander pitched to a 3.66 figure across 68 starts from 2022 to 2024 but has been below the league's average since, and with Gray returned and both Pablo López and Mick Abel expected back from elbow surgery, his seat was wanted.5 Wallner carries elite raw power — a barrel rate of 16.2 percent across 2023 to 2025 and a ball struck at 116.8 miles an hour — and four years of control, yet never held a steady post; Larnach has hit .255/.341/.431 against right-handed pitching since 2021 and .583 by on-base-plus-slugging against the left-handed kind, a split that finally told.5

The rest of the roll rewards the browser. Seattle cut Luke Raley, whose bat lost its room when the Garcia purchase of Thursday's lead remade the roster, and whose season ended under the surgeon's flexor-tendon repair after an on-base-plus-slugging of .929 through his first 53 games gave way to a .157 batting average thereafter.5 Kansas City let go Kyle Isbel and his 51 outs above average in center field since 2022 — third among all outfielders in that span, behind only Pete Crow-Armstrong and Daulton Varsho. Cleveland parted with the utility man David Fry; Washington with Josiah Gray, an All-Star of 2023 who underwent the Tommy John operation in 2024 and has made five appearances these past three years; Detroit with Matt Vierling and Trey Sweeney; St. Louis with Nolan Gorman, whose 41 home runs and 111 OPS+ across 2022 and 2023 have shrunk to a 66 OPS+ in 85 games this past season; and Toronto with Davis Schneider, who hit a leadoff home run in the fifth game of the 2025 World's Series and posted a 1.008 on-base-plus-slugging as a rookie he never matched again.35 And in Boston the formality was completed: Connor Wong, unseated Wednesday when the Fortes purchase crowded him off the roster — as these columns recorded Thursday — was by five o'clock cut loose altogether.3

THE EXCHANGE DESK: THE FIRST NAME CALLED IN THE DRAFT OF 2017 CHANGES HANDS — ROYCE LEWIS GOES WEST TO THE DODGERS FOR BOBBY MILLER AND A CATCHER OF NINETEEN, AND THE UTILITY POST LATELY VACATED BY ROJAS HAS ITS NEW TENANT

The day's one trade of consequence was struck in the hours before the tender deadline: the Dodgers acquired the infielder Royce Lewis from Minnesota, sending back the right-hander Bobby Miller and the 19-year-old catcher Francisco Espinoza. Lewis, 27, was the first name called in the draft of 2017, a schoolboy out of JSerra Catholic in San Juan Capistrano whose professional life has been a running quarrel with his own lower half — twice only in five seasons has he reached 100 games. The past season was, by that grim standard, his healthiest: a career-high 125 games, a lean .227/.287/.380 at the plate, and work at three stations — third base chiefly, with 82 games at first and 16 at second.6

The fit is plain enough. Los Angeles wanted a right-handed-hitting utility infielder from the day Miguel Rojas retired at the season's close, and Lewis — in his second winter of arbitration, under club control through 2028 — answers the description at every point: second-base work to free Tommy Edman for his roving commission, and rest days bought for Max Muncy and Freddie Freeman when a left-hander has the ball.6

Minnesota's side of the ledger is a wager on pedigree. Miller, also 27, went 11-4 with a 3.76 figure as a rookie of 2023 and was for a season the celebrated heir of the Los Angeles pitching line; injury and erosion have limited him to five big-league appearances since the start of 2025, and he arrives as a reclamation. Espinoza, of the Dodgers' international class of 2024, batted .296 with a .713 on-base-plus-slugging in 32 games of Single-A ball.6 Set the trade beside the afternoon's three non-tenders and the Minnesota roster turned over six names between breakfast and dark — four men out, two in, and a front office whose chairman promised a spending winter, as these pages have recorded, revealed to be pruning with the other hand.

THE LABOR QUESTION: TEN MORNINGS LEFT ON THE AGREEMENT'S LIFE — THE LEAGUE WITHDRAWS ITS DESIGNS ON THE SCHOOLBOY DRAFT, THE UNION ANSWERS WITH A CHARTER FOR THE INTERNATIONAL AMATEUR, AND THE TABLE IS SET AGAIN FOR MONDAY

Friday's bargaining session concerned not the veteran's wage but the amateur's door, and produced the rarest article in the current negotiation: a concession. Major League Baseball withdrew its proposal to strip high-school players of their eligibility for the domestic draft, proposing instead to keep the present entry rules for domestic amateurs — this in recognition, said the league spokesman Glen Caplin, of the union's feedback. On the international side no such comity: the Players Association formally rejected the league's international draft proposal from earlier this year and countered with a charter of its own for the amateur entry process abroad. "The enhanced transparency of the International Draft that we have proposed best addresses the root causes of corruption in the current system," Caplin maintained for the league's part.7

The union's counter-proposal is a document of parts. It would raise the clubs' signing-bonus pools and permit teams to exceed them; lift the bonus-pool exemption from $10,000 to $75,000, fattening the envelopes at the low end of the scale; introduce new bonuses for players as they climb the minor-league ladder; establish clear penalties for club conduct the agreement forbids; and eliminate the pre-agreement arrangements — the handshakes made with boys years before they may lawfully sign — that the union holds to be the system's true corruption. For the smaller markets it offers inducements besides: added pool money within the agreement's term, bonuses for reaching the postseason funded by the taxes of pool-exceeding clubs, and strengthened rewards for clubs that lose free agents. "We are committed to negotiating a system that preserves opportunities for future generations of players, protects young prospects from exploitation, and provides flexibility without further burdening international players with new artificial restrictions like a draft," said the interim executive director Bruce Meyer.7

The Herald, whose sympathies on matters of the purse are a matter of long record, notes simply that one side spent Friday proposing to pay young men more and the other withdrew a restriction it had proposed to impose — a day, on balance, of motion in the right direction. The cap and the floor over which the commissioner and the union exchanged broadsides in Thursday's columns went unaddressed in Friday's accounts, and the clock concedes nothing to the amateurs' progress: the agreement dies at one minute before midnight on the first of December, ten mornings from this one, and the two sides are expected back at the table Monday.7

THE SKUBAL REGISTER: THE FIGURE IS NAMED AT LAST — NINE YEARS AND NEAR FOUR HUNDRED MILLIONS REPORTED FROM THE FLUSHING TREASURY, A FIFTH SUITOR TAKES ITS AUDIENCE BY VIDEO, AND THE WHOLE PITCHING MARKET WAITS ON ONE MAN'S PEN

The register these columns keep on the winter's grandest courtship records its first hard number. By the account of MLB Network Radio's Jim Duquette, the Mets have laid before Tarik Skubal an offer of nine years and very nearly $400 million — terms that would carry the highest average yearly wage ever given a free-agent starting pitcher — and it remains unclear, the wire allows, whether any rival establishment has yet put a figure on paper at all. The audiences, meanwhile, multiply: to the four suitors who have sat with the man in person — the Mets, Toronto, San Diego, and the Dodgers, his employers of this past season, who took him from Detroit at the 2025 deadline — the insider Jon Heyman adds a fifth, unnamed, that held its audience by video on Wednesday — one of the two mystery establishments these columns have carried all week, an in-person sitting possibly to follow. The expectation of a choice by the first of December stands — the very day the collective agreement dies.89

Beneath the register, the whole pitching market stands in queue. Jon Paul Morosi reckons that Michael King and Freddy Peralta — the finest arms at liberty after the great left-hander — will both wait upon the Skubal decision before signing anywhere, and Jim Bowden counts better than six clubs in pursuit of Peralta, both Chicago establishments, the Padres and the Rays among them. One signature, in short, holds a dozen in escrow, and the register stays open.9

THE INK DESK: THE CLUB THAT LOST SKUBAL LOCKS UP ITS NEW ACE — MELTON SIGNS IN DETROIT FOR SIX YEARS AND SIX-AND-FORTY MILLIONS AND A HALF, SAN DIEGO KEEPS ITS 'HO-LY SHEETS' MAN, AND TWO BULLPEN PRODIGALS COME HOME TO THE BRONX AND THE BAY

The day's largest signature belongs to Detroit, and the moral writes itself. The Tigers agreed with the right-hander Troy Melton on an extension of six years and $46.5 million guaranteed, with a club option for 2033 and as much as $98 million obtainable through options and incentives, the deal pending a physical — the longest engagement any Detroit pitcher has been offered since Justin Verlander's seven years and $180 million beginning in 2013. Melton, who turns 26 in about two weeks, was a fourth-round selection of 2022 out of San Diego State; his 2026 opened two months late for elbow inflammation and then announced him entirely, a 7-1 record with a 1.49 figure across his first 14 starts and a closing ledger of 8-5 with a 2.76, the final week spent resting an arm whose tests showed no structural harm. With barely a year of service to his name, the contract buys out every arbitration winter and up to two free-agent years besides.10

The resonance is what elevates the item. This is the organization that could not come to terms with Skubal — two Cy Young Awards notwithstanding — and traded him to Los Angeles at the 2025 deadline; it is an organization that has flinched from long pitching contracts since the Zimmermann affair of the previous decade. That it now binds itself to Melton for six years is as plain a statement as a front office can make about which lesson it drew.10

In San Diego the ink ran warmer. The Padres announced a three-year contract for Gavin Sheets worth $11 million, with player options of $4 million apiece after 2027 and 2028 — the lefty slugger had been entering his final arbitration winter, and chose certainty with escape hatches. The figures since he arrived on a minor-league wage two springs ago: a .736 on-base-plus-slugging and 36 home runs, this past season's .212 earned the hard way, the second half played on a torn plantar plate in the left foot that he will rehabilitate rather than have repaired, by the steward A.J. Preller's account. The man's October requires no footnote at Petco Park, where his pinch-hit two-run homer clinched the Wild Card series against the Cubs and the "Ho-ly Sheets" chants have not altogether died down since.11

And two bullpen prodigals found their way home. Jonathan Loáisiga returns to the Yankees — two years at roughly $18 million with a club option for 2029, pending a physical — after a single Arizona season taken on a minor-league deal: a 2.42 figure across 67 innings and a career-high 67 appearances, a sinker at 98 miles an hour, and ground balls at a rate in the game's 95th percentile. He spent his first eight seasons in the Bronx, and his signing advances the bullpen restoration the steward Cashman called his November priority, Bednar's tender and the submariner Hill's return beside it.12 In San Francisco, Camilo Doval signed for one year and $6.3 million to avoid arbitration — the 2023 All-Star whose 39 saves tied for the league's best, whose road since ran through demotion, a deadline sale to the Yankees, and a 4.30 season split with Pittsburgh, until the club that made him claimed him off waivers in September. The Orange and Black will attempt the restoration where the legend was first built.13

THE EXCHANGE DESK, CONTINUED: SMALL STROKES AND LARGER DESIGNS — ARIZONA BUYS A MENDED ARM OFF BOSTON, THE CUBS GATHER A CATCHER FROM THE WAIVER WIRE, DENVER ADDS AN AEROSPACE ENGINEER WHO THROWS SIDEARM, AND THE METS SHOP FOR A FIRST BASEMAN WHILE ATLANTA HUNTS A SHORTSTOP

The smaller strokes of the day filled a ledger page of their own. Arizona bought the right-hander Johan Oviedo from Boston, sending back the 22-year-old left-hander Luke Dotson with a player to be named or cash; Oviedo, 28, made 32 starts with a 4.31 figure for Pittsburgh in 2023 before the Tommy John operation took his 2024 entire and an elbow sprain most of his 2026, and he arrives as insurance for a Sonoran rotation thinned by the departures of Gallen and Soroka to the open market — Burnes, Kelly, Nelson, Rodriguez and Pfaadt stand as the five.14 The Cubs claimed the catcher Joey Bart off the waiver wire from the White Sox and signed him for 2027 — the 29-year-old hit .214 across three clubs and a broken hand this past season, and pairs with Amaya now that Carson Kelly is at liberty — and fetched the reliever Braxton Roxby from Washington for cash, 60 strikeouts in 40 Triple-A outings recommending him; the steward Carter Hawkins allowed that his club must "take risks" to reach its goals.15

Denver kept buying — its second strike in two days, after yesterday's columns recorded the Castillo-Martin freight arriving from Chicago — taking the reliever Chase Lee from Toronto for cash considerations, and the Herald submits Lee's card as the day's finest reading: a walk-on at Alabama who took his degree in aerospace engineering, then taught himself into a sixth-round pick, now delivering sliders and sweepers from a sidearm angle, 5-1 with a 4.60 figure in 40 big-league games. The veteran Jimmy Herget was designated for assignment to make the room, and by five o'clock cut loose altogether.163 The Giants, for their part, sent the infielder Christian Koss to the Rays for Cole Stokes, 21, a twelfth-round selection of the summer's draft out of Florida State who has yet to throw a professional pitch.13

The speculative shelf restocked briskly besides. The insider Heyman reports the Mets — Bauers having gone off the board to Boston, as these columns have recorded — turning their eye to Ty France, 32, who hit .295/.363/.502 with 21 home runs in 133 games for San Diego and whose glove at first base has been worth 14 fielding runs over the past two seasons, tied for second at the position. The incumbent arithmetic argues the case: the Flushing first-base company of Young, Vientos and Baty combined for 18 home runs, an 85 wRC+ and the worst WAR figure in the game at the station, while Jorge Polanco, signed to be Alonso's successor, played two games at first and hit .148 in 37 games in all.9

And in Atlanta the shortstop question has aged into a crisis openly admitted. Since Dansby Swanson's leaving after 2022, Atlanta shortstops rank last in the game by wRC+ and by FanGraphs' wins alike; Ha-Seong Kim hit .116 in 51 games and is at liberty, as is Mauricio Dubón, who started 62 games at the post. With J.P. Crawford the best of a thin free-agent shelf, the steward Alex Anthopoulos allowed on a podcast that his club has discussed trades, and Heyman supplies the names worth the discussing: Corey Seager of Texas, Zach Neto of the Angels, and Jacob Wilson of the Athletics. The Giants, meanwhile, hunt rotation help behind Webb and Roupp, their only two certainties — Morosi has them on Matthew Boyd, 35, an All-Star of 2025 and lately of the Cubs' at-liberty class as yesterday's columns noted, and on Nick Pivetta, 33, whose career-best 2.87 of 2025 gave way to eight elbow-shortened starts this past season.9

Forty-four men put to the street at the stroke of five, the first name of the 2017 draft traded west by sundown, and a bargaining table that for one Friday moved instead of merely glaring — the Saturday columns hold the whole of it above. The forward ledger was opened before a word was written, per the house custom, and tells its patient story: the camps' reporting dates remain unposted, so the count runs to the first rung bearing a date — the spring exhibition openers of Friday, the 19th of February, 90 mornings from this one, with Opening Night to follow on Wednesday, the 24th of March, 123 mornings off.2 The nearer columns press close: the collective agreement has 10 mornings left to live, with the bargainers back at the table Monday;7 Cooperstown's committee of sixteen reports in 15; the Winter Meetings convene at San Diego with the Rule 5 drafting on December 9, 18 mornings from this one; and the writers' envelope waits for the 19th of January, 59 mornings off. Until the morrow's dispatch, keep your scorecards near and your enthusiasms nearer.
