140 days until Opening Night of the 2027 season — the first pitch of the new campaign, the clubs yet to be named (Wednesday, the 24th of March, 2027).

News Around the League

THE LABOR DESK: THE OWNERS MOVE THE CAP'S BIRTHDAY TO 2028 AND SWEETEN EVERY FIGURE — FREEDOM AFTER FIVE YEARS FOR MEN OF THIRTY, A MINIMUM WAGE OF A ROUND MILLION, THE QUALIFYING TARIFF MARKED FOR ABOLITION, AND BOTH PENNANT WINNERS FOUND BENEATH THE PROPOSED FLOOR

The owners returned to the bargaining table Tuesday with their salary cap wearing new figures and a later birthday. The league's latest proposal to the players' union would have the cap-and-floor system begin not in 2027 but in 2028, the second year of a new agreement, with the floor set at $180.2 million and the cap at $258.2 million — roughly $9 million and $13 million higher, respectively, than the figures first proposed for 2027.2 The league's stated reason for the delay is the market now open for business: some clubs already carry 2027 commitments near or above the proposed ceiling, while others would need to raise their payrolls dramatically to clear the proposed floor, and the league says a year's grace avoids disrupting the winter underway. "The projected salary cap and floor for 2028 continues to provide more money for Major League players while leveling the playing field," the league's spokesman Glen Caplin said, "so that every fan believes their team has a fair chance to win it all."2

The sweeteners attached would arrive in 2027 regardless. The league says it will accept the union's May proposal to cut the reserve period to five years for players who have reached 30 — the first reduction below six years since the system was fixed in 1976, and one that would have freed 48 percent of this past season's five-year men a winter early. It would accept the union's proposal to abolish the qualifying-offer system entire — the very tariff under which eight men were tendered $23.15 million apiece only Monday, as these columns recorded. The minimum salary would climb to $1 million for men with two years' service, the largest single increase in the wage's history, with the pre-arbitration bonus pool fattened from $50 million to $65 million besides; and come 2028 the whole enterprise would turn to an even split of baseball revenue between the clubs and the players, with media monies centralized.2

Now the weighing, which this paper performs with its sympathies long on record. By the league's own arithmetic, twelve clubs finished 2026 below the proposed floor and would be obliged to add a combined $636 million in payroll, while eight stand above the cap and would shed $528 million. The reader is invited to run a finger down the list of the twelve — Marlins, Guardians, Cardinals, Rays, Nationals, White Sox, Twins, Rockies, Pirates, Athletics, Reds and Brewers — and to pause at two of them, the Rays and the Brewers: the clubs that contested the World's Series one week ago both stand beneath the wage line the proposal declares the minimum of respectability.2 A plan sold as giving every fan a fair chance to win it all must reckon with the fact that the fair chance just played five games in late October on two purses the plan itself calls too thin. The union's answer had not reached the wires by press time; the present agreement dies on the first of December, 27 days from this morning, and the Herald will keep the ledger open and the handkerchief in its drawer.

THE APPOINTMENTS DESK: SEATTLE HANDS THE HELM TO A MAN OF SIX-AND-THIRTY WHO NEVER MANAGED A GAME AT ANY STATION — KANZLER OF THE CUBS' NURSERY BEATS OUT ESPADA AND BECOMES THE GAME'S SECOND-YOUNGEST SKIPPER

The Puget Sound men have their new captain, and the choice runs clear outside the box: Jason Kanzler, 36 years old, lately three seasons the director of the Cubs' player development, was announced Tuesday morning as the 22nd manager in the franchise's history — a first-time skipper not merely in the big leagues but at any level of organized play.3 He becomes the second-youngest manager in the game, behind only Washington's Blake Butera, who is 34, and the third man to hold the chair under the president of baseball operations Jerry Dipoto, now entering his twelfth season. He succeeds Dan Wilson, whose tenure ended immediately after Game 162 of a season the club counts among its most disappointing. "Our goal in this process was finding a manager with the ability to challenge all of us to improve," Dipoto said. "Jason has the mindset, expertise, communication skills and intensity to help create a championship standard here in Seattle."3

The resume is short on gray hair and long on ascent. A 20th-round draft pick of the Twins in 2013 whose playing days topped out at High-A, Kanzler climbed through the Houston organization across five seasons — hitting coach in the low minors, then minor-league hitting coordinator, then two years on the big-league staff in 2022 and 2023, the first of them ending with the Commissioner's Trophy under Dusty Baker — before the Cubs handed him their farm system.3 The final round came down to two men, and the reader will savor the other name: Joe Espada, the Houston manager whose vacated chair the Astros are even now hunting to fill, as this paper reported Monday. Espada and Kanzler were colleagues on those Houston staffs; the pupil, by the industry's reckoning, has taken the chair first. "This team has an immense amount of talent in all facets of the game," the new man said, "and my coaching staff and I are excited to access and unlock this team's full potential."3

THE BALLOT BOX DESK: TEIXEIRA OF THE 409 CIRCUIT CLOUTS IS ELECTED TO CONGRESS FROM THE ONE-AND-TWENTIETH DISTRICT OF TEXAS — THE SWITCH-HITTER TRADES FIRST BASE FOR THE HOUSE FLOOR, DOWN THE OLD PATH BUNNING BLAZED

The Republic gave its Tuesday to the ballot box, and among the returns came a baseball name of the first magnitude: Mark Teixeira, Republican of Texas, has won election to the United States House of Representatives from that state's 21st Congressional District.4 The ledger he carries to Washington wants no padding. Across fourteen seasons from 2003 through 2016 the switch-hitting first baseman batted .268 with 409 home runs and 1,298 runs driven in, made three All-Star teams, won five Gold Gloves and three Silver Sluggers, and collected a world's championship with the 2009 Yankees.5 There is a neat symmetry in the geography: it was Texas that drafted him fifth overall in 2001 out of Georgia Tech and Texas where he broke into the league in 2003, and it is Texas that now returns him to public life.5

The Capitol has seated ballplayers before, and the path is honorably worn. Jim Bunning threw a perfect game in 1964, pitched his way to Cooperstown, and sat in both chambers of Congress for Kentucky — the House first, then two terms in the Senate. Teixeira arrives by the same door, though the Herald notes the positional novelty: the pitchers have historically handled the nation's business, and it is not every Congress that gains a first baseman, a position whose whole trade is receiving what others throw and keeping a foot on the bag while doing it. The gentleman from the 21st District may find the skills transfer directly.

THE EXCHANGES DESK: THE WINTER'S FIRST TRADE SENDS A SOUTHPAW OF SIX FEET SEVEN TO THE MOUND CITY — THE PINSTRIPES TENDER BEDNAR BUT LET CHISHOLM WALK UNTAXED, AND TAMPA BAY SWEARS ITS ACE IS NOT FOR SALE

The winter's first trade is on the books, and St. Louis made it. The Cardinals announced Tuesday the acquisition of the left-hander Eduardo Rivera from Boston in exchange for the minor-league right-hander Juan Carlos García.6 Rivera is a specimen worth the squint: 23 years old, 6 feet 7 inches of San Juan, Puerto Rico, who opened his big-league career this April with 7 scoreless innings across his first two appearances and proved very nearly unhittable to his own kind — left-handed batters managed one hit in 15 tries against him in the majors, and across 355 left-handed batters faced in his entire minor-league career he has never allowed a home run. His 2026 in the minors read 7-2 with a 2.59 earned-run average and 71 strikeouts in 55 2/3 innings between Portland and Worcester, and he entered the season ranked the No. 18 prospect in the Boston system. García, the price, is an 18-year-old from Montemorelos, Mexico, with two seasons of Dominican Summer League work behind him.6

In the Bronx, the other half of Monday's qualifying-offer ruling emerged. The Pinstripes tendered their closer David Bednar the $23.15 million — his 35 saves this season tied for third in the league, and his ledger since arriving from Pittsburgh in July of 2025 reads 45 saves at a 2.39 earned-run average — but they extended no such offer to the second baseman Jazz Chisholm Jr., who therefore walks to the open market with no draft-pick tariff hung about his neck, a considerable sweetening of his candidacy.7 Bednar has until the 17th to answer; he was one of six Yankee relievers to reach free agency at once, and the club's bullpen, second in the majors at a 3.13 earned-run average, is suddenly a thing to be rebuilt.7

And in St. Petersburg, the question this paper opened Sunday — pay the ace or trade him — received the front office's answer, for whatever a November answer is worth. "Every intention right now is thinking about Ras anchoring our rotation," the president of baseball operations Erik Neander said Tuesday of Drew Rasmussen, whose $20 million option the club exercised Saturday. "We want to have Ras back. It's hard to think that we'll be closer if Ras isn't a part of this team."8 The reader with a long memory will note, as the beat men did, that Neander spoke in nearly the same register about Tyler Glasnow three years ago and traded him to Los Angeles two months later; the Herald records the intention and will watch the deed.8

THE INFIRMARY WARD: CARTER OF THE ARLINGTON MEN GOES UNDER THE SURGEON'S HAND AT FOUR-AND-TWENTY — A HIP REPAIRED, AND THE FIRST BELL OF 2027 LIKELY TO RING WITHOUT HIM

From Texas comes word that the center fielder Evan Carter underwent surgery last week to repair the labrum of his right hip, addressing an impingement besides, and is unlikely to be ready when the 2027 season opens.9 The news lands hard on a career that began at altitude: Carter debuted at 21 in September of 2023 and won a World's Series ring in his first autumn, but the seasons since have been a wrestle, and his 2026 read .194 with 9 home runs and 17 stolen bases across 391 at-bats.10 He is 24 — young enough that a mended hip may yet return the player of that first October — and the Rangers' outfield plans for the spring must now be drawn without him. The Herald wishes the young man a clean knitting and a patient calendar.

Election Day gave the paper its strangest crossover since the champions played Rockefeller Center: a first baseman bound for Congress, and the box score of the thing certified by the returns rather than the official scorer. The schedule ledger was consulted this morning all the same, as it is every morning, and the entry stands unchanged from Tuesday's dispatch: the first ink of 2027 remains the spring exhibitions of Friday, the 19th of February, now 107 mornings off, with Opening Night itself 140 mornings distant on Wednesday, the 24th of March.1 Nearer at hand the docket thickens: the writers begin reading their award verdicts Monday, the rookies called first,11 the eight qualifying men have until the 17th to answer their tenders7 — a species of paperwork that may prove the last of its kind, if Tuesday's proposal at the bargaining table has its way2 — and the owners and the union now bargain with 27 mornings left on the agreement's life. Until the morrow's dispatch, keep your scorecards near and your enthusiasms nearer.
