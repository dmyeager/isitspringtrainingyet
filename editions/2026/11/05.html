Volume I · No. 120 · Thursday, the Fifth of November, Two Thousand and Twenty-Six

No contests this day; the hot stove burns bright.

139 days until Opening Night of the 2027 season — the first pitch of the new campaign, the clubs yet to be named (Wednesday, the 24th of March, 2027).

News Around the League

THE APPOINTMENTS DESK: PUJOLS COMES TO HOUSTON'S DOOR TO ASK FOR THE MANAGER'S CHAIR — ENSBERG AND URUETA JOIN THE QUEUE, AND THE NEW MAN FLORES FINDS HIS OWN ADDRESS BOOK AT THE HEAD OF IT

The Houston vacancy draws its grandest caller yet: Albert Pujols, 46, arrived in town late Wednesday and is scheduled to interview for the manager's chair at Daikin Park this Thursday, by the word of sources the club declines to confirm.2 The playing credentials occupy their own wing of the museum — twenty-two seasons, 703 home runs, 686 doubles, 2,218 runs driven in, eleven All-Star summons, three Most Valuable Player awards and two world's championships — and the interviewers may be asked to forgive the detail that no man has hit more home runs against the Astros than their caller, among them a three-run, go-ahead blow in the ninth inning of Game 5 of the 2005 Championship Series at Houston. What he has never done is manage a big-league game; what he has done is manage winners elsewhere, piloting Leones del Escogido to the 2024-25 Dominican winter crown and the Dominican national side to a third-place finish at this year's World Baseball Classic.2 He interviewed with the Angels and the Padres a winter ago and watched those chairs go to Kurt Suzuki and Craig Stammen; he remains under contract with the Angels even now.

Behind him the queue forms. Morgan Ensberg, 51, who spent seven of his eight playing seasons in Houston silks and gave the 2005 pennant club a .283, .388 and .557 season with 36 home runs — fourth in the Most Valuable voting — is expected to interview this week, and unlike Pujols he carries a managing ledger: seven seasons across Double-A Montgomery and Triple-A Durham with four postseason berths, plus turns in the Arizona Fall League and Venezuelan winter ball. Luis Urueta, bench coach of the Rangers, will be interviewed besides, by the report of MLB Network's Jon Morosi.2

The reader who has followed these columns will remark the pattern. The man conducting the hunt is Randy Flores, the franchise's 15th general manager, introduced at Daikin Park only Monday as this paper reported — and his candidates read like his own address book: Pujols was his Cardinals teammate from 2004 through 2008, and Ensberg his teammate at Southern California in the middle nineties. Flores has said he is "looking to find someone that I can trust and can communicate with," and he appears to be taking the sentence literally.2 Brad Ausmus, whose candidacy this paper logged Monday, remains in the frame, the manager's post having been discussed when he interviewed for the general manager's job that went to Flores. The calendar presses: Flores packs for the general managers' meetings at Las Vegas next week, and the club would plainly prefer to pack a skipper alongside.2

THE HIRING HALL: THE WHITE ELEPHANTS RAID THE CHAMPIONS' SHOP FOR A GENERAL MANAGER — CASTRO OF THE MILWAUKEE PITCHING WORKS, ONCE A THROWING TRAINER, TAKES THE SECOND CHAIR IN SACRAMENTO

The Athletics on Wednesday named Cam Castro their general manager, and the pedigree tells where the game's envy points: Castro, 36, arrives from Milwaukee, where he spent the past two seasons as senior vice president of player development and performance — which is to say the Elephants have gone shopping in the world champions' shop.3 He will serve as the right hand of Taylor Smith, himself installed as president of baseball operations just over two weeks ago. The ladder behind him is pitching from bottom rung to top: a throwing trainer at Driveline Baseball in 2016, a Brewers minor-league pitching coach by 2019, then the organization's pitching coordinator and director of pitching development, and from 2022 its vice president of player development, a member of Matt Arnold's inner circle. "One of the best development minds in the game," Smith called him.3

The Herald notes the week's fashion with approval: that makes two mornings running that a development man has been handed a top chair, Seattle having given its manager's office to the Cubs' farm director Jason Kanzler only yesterday, as these columns recorded. The fashion is no mystery — the champions contested and won a World's Series on one of the game's thinner purses, as yesterday's labor columns had occasion to note, and every club would like the recipe. Castro inherits a task of size: the Athletics finished 64-98, want for pitching above all else, and play one final season at Sutter Health Park in West Sacramento before the Las Vegas ballpark is expected to open its gates for the 2028 season.3

THE RUMOR DESK: BALTIMORE HANGS A PRICE ON EVERY MAN SAVE ALONSO — HENDERSON OF FIVE-AND-TWENTY MAY BE HAD FOR A RANSOM, ARIZONA SETS MARTE BACK UPON THE TABLE, AND PASSAN CROWNS RASMUSSEN THE WINTER'S LIKELIEST MOVER

From Baltimore comes the winter's boldest storefront. The Orioles, by the report of Andy Kostka of the Baltimore Banner, will entertain trade offers on every player on the roster save Pete Alonso, as they hunt upgrades for the rotation and the outfield.4 Alonso, the reader will recall from Tuesday's columns, was the five-year, $155 million signing of last December. Every player includes the shortstop Gunnar Henderson, 25, though the asking price there is understood to be of the sort that ends conversations rather than opens them; his play has receded from its Most-Valuable-caliber heights of 2024, and the Birds would evidently sooner be overpaid than persuaded.4

Arizona, meanwhile, has put Ketel Marte back upon the table, by the account of MLB.com's Steve Gilbert, the Snakes having missed the playoffs narrowly in each of the past three seasons. The table has a peculiar geometry: Marte is signed through 2030 with a player option for 2031, and as a ten-and-five man he carries a full no-trade clause — the club may shop him, and he may decline to be sold. The sides moved past an August unpleasantness, an unexcused absence from a game at Fenway Park, but the listing itself tells the reader which way the front office leans.4

And in St. Petersburg the other shoe hovers audibly. ESPN's Jeff Passan ranks Drew Rasmussen, 31, the likeliest trade candidate in the game this winter — one season from free agency at $20 million, the richest single-year average in the franchise's books — this set against the vow of the president Erik Neander, printed in these columns only yesterday, that the club has "every intention" of Rasmussen anchoring the 2027 rotation.4 The Herald wrote then that it records the intention and will watch the deed; the deed, it develops, has already acquired bookmakers.

THE FAR PACIFIC DESK: THE MEASURING INSTRUMENTS RENDER THEIR VERDICT ON SATO — A BAT AMONG THE GAME'S SWIFTEST, AN APPETITE THAT CHASES NEARLY EVERYTHING, AND ONE SUITOR ALREADY SAID TO COOL

Since Teruaki Sato weighed anchor Sunday — the posting this paper recorded Monday, with all thirty clubs free to treat with him — the trade has busied itself with the question of what, precisely, is crossing the water. MLB.com's David Adler has now published the measuring instruments' answer, drawn from Japan's licensed tracking data, and the figures read like a strongman's calling card: an average exit velocity of 93.3 mph, which would stand roughly among the ten best in the majors and in line with Shohei Ohtani's 2026 mark; a top strike of 117.0 mph, a reading only a handful of big-league men produce in a season; a bat swung at 74.6 mph, inside the game's top 50 and nearly identical to Munetaka Murakami's; a hard-hit rate of 55.3 percent; and a barrel rate of 18.4 percent that would trail only Murakami's own.5 For the eye that prefers a single exhibit, there is an August drive measured at 480 feet.5

The asterisk is appetite. Sato swung at 53 percent of the pitches he saw last season and chased better than a third of those outside the zone; the Japanese baseball authority Yuri Karasawa reckons him a freer swinger than nearly any notable NPB hitter of the past five years. His countryman Murakami — who struck out and walked in heaps and still hit 35 home runs as a White Sox rookie this season, the most ever by a Japanese hitter in his debut year — represents the opposite church: monkish selectivity, violence only upon the chosen pitch. Sato strikes out less, walks less, and makes more contact, particularly against the breaking and off-speed stuff; Adler calls the chasing "playing with fire" against major-league pitching, and the projections split the difference, reckoning him at .247 with 31 home runs and a bat some 12 percent better than the league average in his first campaign.5

The market, meanwhile, moved a square. The Mets — one of the four clubs the insider Heyman linked to the man at his posting, as this paper noted Monday — are now said by The Athletic to be less inclined to pursue him, their winter fixed instead upon starting pitching, with Tarik Skubal the declared object of desire; Bo Bichette's decision to exercise his $42 million player option for 2027 has helped settle the club's plans besides.4 The queue for Skubal thus lengthens precisely as foretold — the Cubs' ardor was Tuesday's news in these columns — and the first lot of the fair looks dearer by the morning.

THE NURSERY DESK: THE AUTUMN CIRCUIT NAMES ITS FALL STARS FOR SUNDAY EVENING — TEN OF THE TOP HUNDRED BOUND FOR SALT RIVER FIELDS, WALCOTT OF THE TEXAS SYSTEM AT THEIR HEAD

The Arizona Fall League named its Fall Stars on Wednesday, and the twentieth annual showcase convenes Sunday evening at Salt River Fields at Talking Stick, 8 o'clock Eastern, with the MLB Network carrying the pictures.6 Ten of the game's hundred best prospects dot the rosters, six of them on the American League side: the Rangers' shortstop Sebastian Walcott, ranked No. 8 in the game and the bill's headliner, joined by the Angels' Arjun Nimmala, the Rays' catcher Nathan Flewelling, the Royals' Angeibel Gomez, the Yankees' Dax Kilby and the Orioles' Ike Irish. The National League counters with the Braves' Tate Southisene, the Phillies' Aidan Miller, the Nationals' right-hander Jarlin Susana and the D-backs' Demetrio Crisantes. Two more of the hundred — Detroit's Zyhir Hope and the White Sox's Caleb Bonemer — were named to the honor but will not play, their Fall League terms already ended; and the Guardians' Welbyn Francisca, who leads the circuit at .409, supplies the local menace. The autumn league's home-run derby precedes the whole affair on Saturday.6

Why the exhibition merits the Herald's ink, the week's own columns attest: a year ago Kevin McGonigle led off the Fall Stars Game for the American side, and this week the writers named him a finalist for the Rookie of the Year, as this paper recorded Tuesday. The autumn circuit is where next November's short lists take their batting practice.6

A morning of chairs, this one: a grandee of 703 home runs asks for Houston's, a champion's apprentice takes Sacramento's, and the rumor columns hang a price on half of Baltimore. The schedule ledger, consulted at dawn as it is every dawn, holds steady: the first ink of 2027 remains the spring exhibitions of Friday, the 19th of February, now 106 mornings from this one, with Opening Night itself 139 mornings off, on Wednesday, the 24th of March.1 The nearer docket wants no padding — the writers begin reading their award verdicts Monday, the rookies first; the eight qualifying men have until the 17th to answer their tenders; the general managers convene at Las Vegas in the week ahead;2 and the collective agreement has 26 mornings left to live, the Herald's sympathies filed where they have always been filed. Until the morrow's dispatch, keep your scorecards near and your enthusiasms nearer.
