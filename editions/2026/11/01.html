News Around the League

THE RETENTIONS DESK: TAMPA BAY KEEPS ITS ACE AT A WAGE OF TWENTY MILLIONS, A SUM NEVER BEFORE PAID ON THE BAYFRONT — AND THE QUESTION OF A TRADE ARRIVES IN THE SAME ENVELOPE

The first great ruling of the options ledger this paper has kept all week came down Saturday night, and it kept an ace in harness. The Rays exercised their $20 million club option on the right-hander Drew Rasmussen — the decision this ledger noted Friday as still pending — and thereby committed to the largest single-season salary in the franchise's history, whose previous high-water mark was the $15 million paid Charlie Morton in 2019.3 The figure was earned the hard way: the option, written into his January 2025 extension at $8 million, carried escalators the pitcher maxed out entirely with the finest season of his career — 16 wins against 5 defeats, a 2.67 earned-run average and 174 strikeouts across 172 innings and 30 starts, the American League's best WHIP at 0.91 — followed by an October in which he opened with three gems and closed by taking the ball in the fifth game of the World's Series on three days' rest, the first such assignment of his life, and allowing one run on two hits over four innings of a losing cause. Since 2022 his 2.74 earned-run average stands fourth among all pitchers with 500 innings, behind only Tarik Skubal, Blake Snell and Chris Sale.3

The celebration is tempered by arithmetic. A club that has never paid any man $20 million must now decide whether to pay this one or trade him, and the pitcher knows it — asked after the fifth game whether he hoped to remain, he answered "I hope so," then added, "There's a business element to this, and I understand that." His manager Kevin Cash was less equivocal: "He's a stud. That guy is a stud." With Freddy Peralta and Nick Martinez both departed to the open market, the St. Petersburg club's winter turns on what it does with the arm it just retained.3

THE OPTIONS LEDGER, THIRD DAY: SAN DIEGO TURNS PIVETTA LOOSE AND KEEPS A LEFT-HANDER, BALTIMORE PARTS WITH ROBERT, CINCINNATI'S CLOSER WALKS AWAY FROM TEN MILLIONS OF HIS OWN ACCORD, AND THREE RANGERS TAKE THEIR LEAVE

Elsewhere the ledger ran heavy with departures. The Padres declined their $14 million option on the right-hander Nick Pivetta, by the word of a source, though the club has not confirmed it and professes interest in signing him back at a friendlier figure.4 The elbow wrote this entry: Pivetta, entering his age-34 season, made only eight starts in 2026 around a flexor strain that cost him nearly five months, and the long stay on the infirmary list triggered a clause converting what had been his own option into the club's. He pitched well on his return — a 1.45 earned-run average over his final four starts, then two honest October outings — but with Michael King already opted out and Casey Mize, Robbie Ray and Walker Buehler all free besides, the San Diego rotation is suddenly mostly vacancies. The left-handed reliever Wandy Peralta, for his part, exercised his $4.45 million option to stay.4 In Baltimore the Birds declined their $20 million figure on the outfielder Luis Robert Jr., as this ledger foresaw they might — the 29-year-old played all of three games in their colors after an August waiver claim before a hamstring ended his season, and he departs with a $2 million buyout, the club's door pointedly left ajar for a cheaper reunion. Only the first baseman Ryan Mountcastle's $7.5 million clause remains on the Baltimore docket.5

The day's genuine surprise came from Cincinnati, where the closer Emilio Pagán declined his $10 million player option and walked into the market at 35.6 The Queen City nine had watched him save 25 games this season at a 3.38 earned-run average — and, after returning from a spring hamstring strain on July 1, convert every one of his 19 chances at a 1.88 figure over his final 30 appearances — and must now hunt a ninth-inning man in a winter when Raisel Iglesias, David Bednar and Adrian Morejon are likewise at large. The third baseman Eugenio Suárez, whose second tour in Cincinnati yielded 26 home runs but a .212 average, and the reliever Pierce Johnson had their mutual options declined the same afternoon.6 And in Texas, the designated hitter Joc Pederson, the catcher Kyle Higashioka and the right-hander Jakob Junis all declined their mutual options with the Lone Star nine.7 The whole settling of accounts must finish within five days of the Series' end, and come Monday at 5 o'clock Eastern the market opens to all comers.69

THE LAURELS DESK: A SCORE OF GOLDEN GLOVES IS HANDED DOWN THIS VERY EVENING — TURANG OF THE CHAMPIONS STANDS AMONG THE FINALISTS, WITT PURSUES A THIRD IN SUCCESSION, AND KWAN SEEKS A FIFTH FROM A NEW PASTURE

The fielding season receives its verdict tonight: the Rawlings Gold Glove winners are unveiled this evening at 8:30 Eastern, with three finalists standing at every position in each league.8 The electorate is the game's own bench — each club's manager and up to six coaches, barred from voting for their own men, supply three-quarters of the reckoning, with the Society for American Baseball Research's defensive index the remaining quarter. The Blue Jays and Cubs lead the field with five finalists apiece.8 Milwaukee's interest is direct: Brice Turang, lately seen turning the final out of the World's Series, is a finalist at second base. Tampa Bay's shortstop Taylor Walls — whose contract clause, it happens, still awaits the club's ruling — is a finalist for the second straight year.8

The contests worth watching: Bobby Witt Jr. of the Royals pursues a third consecutive honor at shortstop, where Andrés Giménez — thrice a winner at second base — now challenges from his new post; Steven Kwan of the Guardians, four years running the winner in left field, stands for the first time as a center-field finalist, having manned the middle pasture more often than the corner this season; Wilyer Abreu of the Red Sox arrives with 28 defensive runs saved, the most of any man in baseball by a wide margin; and Dillon Dingler of the Tigers defends his crown behind the plate. The Herald, by its standing rule, hands out nothing before the ballot does — the names of the anointed in tomorrow's columns.8

THE BAZAAR DESK: THE WINTER MARKET OPENS ITS STALLS ON THE MORROW — SKUBAL THE PRIZE OF THE FAIR, THE QUALIFYING OFFER SET AT THREE-AND-TWENTY MILLIONS AND CHANGE, AND THE LABOR CLOCK TICKING TOWARD DECEMBER

With the options ledger closing, the shape of the winter fair is plain, and the grandest stall belongs to Tarik Skubal. The left-hander, twice the American League's Cy Young man,2 split his season between Detroit and Los Angeles and arrives at the market bearing a ledger of 50 wins against 20 defeats and a 2.47 earned-run average across 103 starts since 2023.9 The sums under discussion strain the type: the largest pitching contract on record is Yoshinobu Yamamoto's twelve years and $325 million, and the highest annual wage the $43.33 million shared by Max Scherzer and Justin Verlander — marks the trade expects Skubal to pass and to press, respectively, with the Mets, Dodgers, Tigers, Blue Jays and Cubs all named among the likely bidders.9

The almanac fills in around him. The qualifying offer is fixed this year at $23.15 million, and the week ahead brings the usual parlor game of who receives one — Randy Arozarena and Seiya Suzuki all but certainly, each all but certain to decline. The general managers convene at Las Vegas from November 9 through 11, where the winter's first trade winds customarily begin to blow. And over the whole bazaar hangs the first of December, when the collective agreement between the clubs and the players expires.9 The Herald will watch that negotiation with its customary interest — and when the pleading of empty purses begins, as it reliably does, this paper will be found consulting the ledger rather than the handkerchief.

THE FOOTLIGHTS DESK: THE SUDS MEN PLAY ROCKEFELLER CENTER — YELICH, TURANG AND MURPHY CROSS WITS WITH A DRUNKEN UNCLE ON THE SATURDAY BROADCAST, TWO-AND-FORTY YEARS AFTER UECKER HELD THE SAME STAGE

The champions' victory tour reached television's oldest vaudeville house Saturday night, when Christian Yelich, Brice Turang and the manager Pat Murphy appeared together on Saturday Night Live's Weekend Update segment, charged with convincing the returning character Drunk Uncle — the comedian Bobby Moynihan, back at the Update desk — that a team named for beermaking had truly won the World's Series.10 The sketch found time for an old wound: "Hey, wait a minute," Moynihan said, eyeing the Milwaukee captain. "You're Pete Davidson, right?" — a jab at a resemblance that has trailed Yelich since his Miami days.10

The Herald notes the echo in the rafters. The club's last man on that stage was Bob Uecker himself, who hosted the second episode of the program's tenth season on October 13 of 1984, in the full flower of his beer-advertisement and Tonight Show celebrity.10 Forty-two years later, the franchise he voiced for half a century sent three world's champions up the same elevators — one more small ceremony, after Friday's parade and the coin in the clubhouse man's pocket, in an autumn that keeps finding ways to toast the old announcer.

The clocks fell back overnight, and so the game's first Sunday of winter arrives an hour longer than the Saturday before it — a cruel arithmetic, the reader will note, since the extra hour contains precisely as much baseball as the other twenty-four. The schedule ledger has been consulted this morning all the same, and stands white for 143 mornings more, until Opening Night on Wednesday, the 24th of March, 2027.1 But the paper is not without appointments: the Gold Gloves are handed down this very evening,8 the market throws open its gates Monday at 5 o'clock,6 and the first hardware of awards season will keep these columns warm straight through to the truck that leaves for the training camps. An hour gained and 143 to wait — the Herald suggests spending the found one on the archive. Until the morrow's dispatch, keep your scorecards near and your enthusiasms nearer.
