Volume I · No. 49 · Wednesday, the Six-and-Twentieth of August, Two Thousand and Twenty-Six

Reporting 15 contests from the day prior.

The Game of the Day

SIXTEEN INNINGS OF FAMINE, THEN A FEAST AT THE STROKE OF MIDNIGHT: WALDSCHMIDT, SUMMONED FROM THE BENCH, ENDS IT WITH ONE SWING — ARIZONAS 5, NORTH SIDERS 4

Four runs in arrears entering the eighth, without a tally in two evenings, the Sonoran nine collect three against the Chicago bullpen and then send a rookie to the plate cold — whose two-run blast wins the game, draws Arizona within a single game of the National's last chair, and closes the strangest day of Torey Lovullo's summer

For sixteen consecutive innings the Arizona bats had gone without a run — nine of them in Monday's shutout, chronicled in these pages, and seven more on Tuesday evening while the North Siders' Holmes spun a spell of his own: seven innings, two hits allowed, five strikeouts, and not a Phoenix runner permitted past his post. The visitors, meanwhile, had banked their evening early — Happ's three-run blast in the fourth, his three-and-twentieth, and a Busch single in the sixth had the ledger four to none, and the desert crowd had every reason to expect a second consecutive famine.

Then Holmes departed, and the spell went with him. In the eighth, against the reliever Palencia, Moreno doubled home a run and Perdomo singled home two more, and four-to-none had become four-to-three inside a quarter hour. In the ninth, with Fernandez aboard and the pitcher Webb on the hill, Lovullo turned to his bench — and here the manager confessed afterward to a comedy worthy of the vaudeville stage. Twice he asked his hitting men, Mather, Easley, and Denker, whom to send; twice they begged another minute's deliberation; at the last they pronounced their verdict: send Waldy. Ryan Waldschmidt, the rookie, batting for Barrosa with the game moving as Lovullo put it at warp speed, took his instructions, stepped cold to the plate, and deposited a two-run blast into the seats — his third home run of the season.

So ended a day Arizona will not soon forget: begun with its manager tendering an apology to his opposite number (the protocol desk relates the particulars below), and concluded with five runs in its final six outs against a club six games clear of it in the standings. The arithmetic is the sweetest part — the Sonoran nine now stand a single game behind San Diego for the National's final wild-card chair, and the two clubs' fortunes turned on the same midnight hour, as the rest of the card relates.

News Around the League

THE PROTOCOL DESK: LOVULLO TENDERS AN APOLOGY, AND THE LEAGUE PRONOUNCES THE SPYGLASS LAWFUL WHEN WORKED FROM SECOND BASE

The shouting that enlivened Monday's contest in Phoenix has ended in a handshake. The reader will recall that during Arizona's seven-to-none defeat, the Diamondbacks' bench took loud exception to the Cubs' third-base coach Quinton Berry, who was observed relaying to Chicago runners at second base when the Arizona starter Kelly meant to throw his changeup — the runner then wiring the intelligence onward to the batsman. Manager Torey Lovullo, his pitching coach Kaplan, and his bench coach Banister hollered at Berry to knock it off; Chicago's bench coach Flaherty hollered back; and a fine cross-field row was had by all. Lovullo, believing a rule forbade such traffic, carried the question to Michael Hill, the league's senior vice president of on-field operations, and received his answer Tuesday: there is no such rule. Espionage conducted in plain view from the bag is honest labor, as it has been since the deadball days, and the remedy — as every catcher of the old school knew — is to change the signs, not to petition the authorities. Lovullo, to his credit, took the ruling like a sportsman and conveyed his apology to Craig Counsell. That evening Arizona beat Chicago anyway, by means entirely above board, as the lead column attests.

THE INFIRMARY WARD: JUDGE GRADUATES FROM THE TEE TO LIVING PITCHING, SCHMIDT PLOTS A RETURN BY WAY OF THE BULLPEN, AND BUXTON'S HIP SOUNDS ITS ALARM ONCE MORE

From the Bronx, encouraging bulletins: Aaron Judge, whose confinement to the batting tee this paper noted Monday, on Tuesday took his swings against overhand pitching in the indoor cages beneath Yankee Stadium — the first such since his right rib began mending — and ran the outfield besides, his throwing now stretched to a hundred feet. His manager Boone, nodding toward the dugout stairs, offered the medical opinion of the age: he looks good. In the same camp, Clarke Schmidt faced hitters in a live session and stands close to a rehabilitation assignment, the club minded to return him by way of the bullpen for the stretch drive.

The news runs darker in Minnesota, where Byron Buxton departed Tuesday's contest in the seventh inning after chasing a line drive into the right-center gap and pulling up in evident discomfort. The complaint is the familiar one — the right hip that has troubled him since May and twice lodged him on the injured list — and the deceleration required of an outfielder in full flight remains its cruelest test. He is to be examined again Wednesday, and the Twins hold their breath.

THE MILESTONE DESK: STEWART STANDS ONE SHY OF A HUNDRED, ALONSO GATHERS FOUR HITS IN PURSUIT, PETERS STALKS A CLUB RECORD, AND THE CROWN OF THREE GROWS HEAVIER

The hundred-mark watch narrows to a single tally. Sal Stewart of Cincinnati, who stood two short when yesterday's paper went to press, homered in the ninth inning at San Francisco — his eight-and-twentieth — and now sits at nine-and-ninety runs batted in, one stroke from the century. The Cardinal pair who lurk behind him, Burleson and Walker, were held at six-and-ninety apiece by an evening in which their entire club managed one run. In the American, Pete Alonso of the Baltimores put up four hits and four runs batted in at St. Louis, his one-and-thirtieth homer among them, and stands at nine-and-eighty — second in the league's table, now but two tallies behind Yordan Alvarez, whose crown of three grows heavier by the morning: the Houston colossus tripled and drove in his one-and-ninetieth in the Bronx, and leads the American in average at .323, in home runs at six-and-thirty, and in runs batted in, all at once. And on the South Side, the rookie Tristan Peters has now homered in four consecutive games — one shy of the club record of five, set by his own teammate Murakami earlier this summer — of which feat the rest of the card has more to say.

THE ANNALS DESK: NINE PITCHES, THREE VICTIMS — BRADLEY WRITES A PAGE NO TWIN HAD WRITTEN BEFORE

In the fifth inning at Sacramento's grounds Tuesday night, Taj Bradley of the Twins accomplished what no Minnesota pitcher is known to have accomplished in the franchise's long and pitching-proud annals: the immaculate inning — three batsmen retired on strikes, nine pitches, not one wasted. Cortes went down swinging at a splitter that dove beneath the zone; Serven chased a curveball to the same fate; and Bolte, the leadoff man, was frozen by a fastball of 99.8 miles per hour laid on the black at the bottom of the zone. The catcher Caratini confessed afterward that he called the final fastball simply because it was the best pitch, and history could look after itself. That the feat came in a losing cause — the Athletics took the game, four to two — is the sort of joke the pastime reserves for its finest single innings, and the Herald records it here so the annals need not blush.

THE WILLIAMSPORT DESK: MISS RASMUS OF ALABAMA REWRITES THE GIRLS' LEDGER

From South Williamsport, where this paper's attentions lingered all weekend, word of a legend still growing: Kinley Rasmus, the twelve-year-old two-way player out of Phenix City, Alabama — daughter of the former big-league pitcher Cory Rasmus and niece of the outfielder Colby — has set a new record for hits by a girl in the Little League World Series, five and counting, as her nine pulled out a four-to-two victory over the Soundview club of Tacoma to advance toward the tournament's final stages. This on the heels of Monday's work, when she tied Mo'ne Davis's mark of fourteen strikeouts, the most ever recorded by a girl in the Series — the pair of them numbering among just nine girls ever to take the hill at Williamsport. The Herald tips its cap, and suspects it will not be the last time it sets her name in type.

The Rest of the Card

THE APPOINTMENT BY THE PACIFIC IS KEPT: SKENES SPOTLESS THROUGH SIX, KING MATCHES HIM SEVEN, AND CRUZ SETTLES IT IN THE NINTH — BUCS 1, FRIARS 0

This desk directed all eyes to San Diego at Tuesday's close, and the evening repaid the fare. Paul Skenes, whose recent labors had gone unrewarded, surrendered a leadoff double to Tatis on an oh-and-two pitch — a drive measured at 110 miles to the hour — and then nothing whatever: six scoreless innings, three hits, four strikeouts, not a walk, his first spotless start since the twelfth of May. San Diego's King answered with seven blank innings of his own, and the game arrived at the ninth a double cipher — whereupon Oneil Cruz lined his seventeenth home run off the closer Miller for the only run of the night. Montgomery locked the door for his sixth save, the Friars were held to three hits entire, and the Missions have now dropped two one-run decisions running to Pittsburgh — with Arizona, as the lead column relates, drawn to within a single game of their chair.

FIVE RUNS DOWN IN ANAHEIM, AND RAMÍREZ DECLINES THE VERDICT: CLEVELAND NINE 8, ANAHEIM NINE 6, IN TEN INNINGS

The Cleveland nine trailed five to none after two innings — a two-run Grissom homer, a Moore blast, and small-arms fire besides — and a lesser club would have consulted the timetable for the morning train. Instead: José Ramírez, vintage issue — three doubles on the night, five runs batted in: the two-run double in the fourth that began the climb, the equalizing double in the eighth after Grissom's second blast had restored the Anaheim lead, and at last the two-run double in the tenth that decided it. It stands as Cleveland's largest comeback of the season and their first from four or more runs down since August of last year; manager Vogt pronounced the affair a heart-pumper, which the Herald certifies as clinically accurate. The streak runs to six, Smith collected his five-and-thirtieth save, and the final wild-card chair remains Cleveland property for another morning.

NINE RUNNING, AND THE LAST OUT MADE BY TELEGRAPH RELAY: MISSOURIANS 5, CANADIANS 3

The hottest club in the land carried its business abroad and never broke stride. Lugo gave five innings of one-run ball, Salvador Perez supplied the go-ahead single in the fifth, and the veteran Scherzer — now one-and-seven on his sorrowful ledger — absorbed the defeat. Toronto made it interesting with two in the ninth, whereupon the game ended as prettily as any this season: Lukes's two-out knock into the gap was cut off by Collins, wired to Witt, and forwarded to Tolbert at second base, where Lukes was tagged out attempting his advance — a nine-game winning streak sealed by relay post. It is Kansas City's longest run since 2017, and no club in either league is presently hotter.

GASPER STRIKES TWICE, AND DURAN EMPTIES THE BASES IN THE ELEVENTH: HUB NINE 7, FLORIDIANS 3

A close-run affair ran long and then burst. Mickey Gasper homered twice in solo fashion, Miami answered each time, and the clubs traded single tallies again in the tenth — Boston's on a passed ball, the Floridians' on a wild pitch loosed even as the batsman struck out, which is the sort of bookkeeping that keeps official scorers honest. In the eleventh the Hub nine loaded the bases and Jarren Duran cleared them entirely, a grand slam to right-center — his eighteenth homer, four runs batted in — and the count of consecutive Boston victories runs to five, the win to the reliever Gamboa.

THE WEST'S CROWN CHANGES HEADS A THIRD MORNING RUNNING: ASTROS 9, AMERICANS 7, IN THE BRONX

A proper slugging match, thrice tied and turned about as often. Yainer Diaz struck two home runs, the second the equalizer in the eighth, whereupon Yordan Alvarez — the crown of three upon his brow — lashed a triple to put Houston in front and Paredes singled him home for margin. García Jr. homered, doubled, and drove in three for the Americans, and Chisholm brought them within one, but Diaz's single in the ninth restored the cushion and Hader closed the accounts for his nineteenth save. The arithmetic: Houston, at the break-even line of six-and-sixty apiece and with Espada restored to the bench as this paper related only yesterday, reclaims sole possession of the West's crown by half a game over Texas — the third different head to wear it in three mornings.

PETERS MAKES IT FOUR EVENINGS RUNNING, AND THE FIRST INNING DOES THE REST: SOUTH SIDERS 11, TEXAS NINE 7

Six runs in the opening frame off deGrom settled the general question, leaving only the particulars. Chief among them: the rookie Tristan Peters, late of the barnstorming Savannah Bananas, who doubled home two in that first-inning cannonade and later homered — his fourth consecutive game with a home run, a big evening at the plate that left him flirting with the cycle, and now one game shy of the club record of five straight as the milestone desk relates. Antonacci tripled and Vargas and the Montgomerys kept the queue moving; for Texas, Foscue and Burger and Jansen all homered in a losing cause. The Rangers thereby surrender the West's crown they had held for a single day.

EIGHT IN THE FIFTH AND NO MERCY IN MOUND CITY: BIRDS 13, REDBIRDS 1

The Baltimores fell upon St. Louis with a violence rarely seen outside the prize ring. Pete Alonso had four hits inside five innings — a three-run homer, his one-and-thirtieth, among them — and four runs batted in; Encarnacion-Strand homered and drove home three; and the fifth inning alone produced eight Baltimore runs, a parade to the plate that the St. Louis bullpen will see in its sleep. Bassitt cruised to the win, Church's homer supplied the Cardinal run, and St. Louis has now dropped four straight — their hundred-watch pair, as noted above, going hungry.

THREE AT THE FIRST KNOCK, ONE AT THE LAST: TOMAHAWK NINE 4, DODGERS 3

Atlanta greeted Glasnow, making his first start since May, with three first-inning runs — Dubón and Harris singling home a pair and Albies adding a sacrifice fly — though the big right-hander steadied thereafter and the champions drew level by the fourth on singles by Thomas, Feduccia, and Call. There matters stood until the eighth, when Ozzie Albies singled home Olson with the run that decided it; Iglesias closed for his eight-and-twentieth save. A heavyweight engagement between the National's two best records, taken by the home club — and the two nines are appointed to resume hostilities this very evening.

LINDOR SAVES IT WITH THE LEATHER, WINS IT WITH HIS LEGS: METROPOLITANS 3, CREAM CITY NINE 2, IN TEN

The shortstop of the Metropolitans compressed a season's worth of service into two innings. With the bases choked and two out in the ninth of a tied game, Francisco Lindor raced to his right and took Hamilton's soft liner off the turf with a full-length dive, saving the go-ahead runs; in the tenth he crossed with the winning tally when Morel's fielder's-choice bounder was compounded by Hamilton's answering throwing error. Lindor had earlier homered, his thirteenth, and Robert Jr. added his tenth; Yelich's ninth-inning double had briefly rescued Milwaukee. The Brewers, still owners of the game's best record at one-and-eighty wins, have nonetheless dropped two running in Flushing.

CLARK ATONES INSIDE THE SPAN OF AN INNING: DETROITS 4, RAYS 1

The young center fielder Max Clark botched a bad-hop liner in the fourth — the sort of carom that changes direction as if bribed — and was still cursing himself when he stepped in against the left-hander Seymour an inning later. His answer: a go-ahead two-run homer into the right-field seats, the second of his major-league life and his first at Comerica Park, struck with such absorbed attention that he carried his bat nearly to first base. Lee added a late insurance single, Jansen closed for his seventeenth save, and the Tampa men — beaten while the Americans were also losing — hold their lead in the East at three games and a half.

KIRBY FURIOUS, AND THE SOUND MEN KEEP CLIMBING: PUGET SOUND MEN 4, QUAKERS 1

George Kirby was all business by the Sound: seven and two-thirds innings, six hits, a single run, nine strikeouts, with Nola — seven strikeouts of his own — the hard-luck loser. Cal Raleigh, a mere evening removed from the three-homer performance this paper celebrated at length, homered again, his eighteenth, and Canzone's two-run single in the seventh furnished the cushion before Muñoz sealed his four-and-twentieth save. The Quakers have now lost two straight since their nine-game streak was buried, and Seattle has won two straight in the climb back toward the wild-card row.

GOODMAN'S FIVE-AND-THIRTIETH TRAVELS WELL, AND THE CAPITAL GOES HUNGRY: MOUNTAIN MEN 3, CAPITAL NINE 1

Hunter Goodman struck his five-and-thirtieth home run — third in the National's table, behind only Schwarber's nine-and-thirty and Olson's six-and-thirty — and Norby doubled home another in the same fourth inning. Lile answered at once with his seventeenth, but Washington found nothing further, and a fifth-inning Colorado run scored on a wild pitch stood as garnish. Romano closed for his thirteenth save; the Capital nine have now lost eight of their last ten, and the Mountain men, whatever their station in the standings, have avenged Monday's thirteen-to-three drubbing in the same yard.

AN IMMACULATE INNING WASTED, AND THE ELEPHANTS TAKE THE EVENING: ATHLETICS 4, TWINS 2

Carlos Cortes opened the scoring with a two-run homer in the second and Sacramento added two more in the sixth on White's double and Walton's single, which proved sufficient against a Minnesota attack held to four hits. The Twins' consolations were historic rather than arithmetical — Bradley's nine-pitch masterpiece in the fifth, for which see the annals desk — and their anxieties are medical, Buxton's hip having ended his evening early, for which see the infirmary ward. Jump took the win, Alvarado the save, his fourth.

CAVANAUGH EARLY, DEVERS LOUD, AND STEWART'S NINETY-NINTH ARRIVES TOO LATE: FRISCO NINE 3, REDLEGS 1

The Frisco nine did their scoring at the bookends of the evening's first half — Cavanaugh's two-run single in the opening frame and Devers's eight-and-twentieth homer in the fifth — and the pitching did the rest, Cincinnati held to four hits, the win to the young Molina and the save to Seymour. The Redleg run arrived in the ninth by way of Sal Stewart's homer, likewise his eight-and-twentieth, which carried him to nine-and-ninety runs batted in and the doorstep of the century, as the milestone desk relates. Cincinnati has dropped three straight; the Frisco men have quietly won two.

The arithmetic desk reports the American's furniture rearranged yet again. The West's crown, which sat upon two uneasy heads Monday morning and upon Texas alone on Tuesday, this morning passes to Houston by the half-game — the third head to wear it in as many editions. The final wild-card chair remains Cleveland's, its tenant at seven-and-sixty wins against six-and-sixty defeats and winner of six running, with Texas one game astern, Baltimore two and a half, and Seattle, Minnesota, and Toronto three. In the National the chairs read: the Cubs comfortable, Philadelphia at two games' cushion, and San Diego clinging to the last seat with Arizona now a single game behind and Miami four. Tonight the card again runs to the full fifteen engagements, all verified against the schedule: the rubber match in the desert goes at 3:40 by the Eastern clock, the Bucs seek a sweep of the Friars at 4:10, the Cleveland nine and the Anaheim nine resume at 4:07, the Houstons and the Americans take up the crown question again in the Bronx at 7:05, the Royals carry nine straight into Toronto at 7:07, and the champions and the Tomahawk nine stage the second act of their collision at 7:15. Until the next dispatch, keep your scorecards near and your enthusiasms nearer.
