The Game of the Day

A MINUTE'S OVATION FROM THE CITY THAT TRADED HIM, THEN TWO HOMERS AGAINST ITS COLORS: RUTSCHMAN RETURNS TO CAMDEN YARDS: RED SOX 6, ORIOLES 5

One month to the day after the deadline deal that sent the ultimate Oriole north, Adley Rutschman stands in at his old harbor to a standing ovation of nearly a minute — then strikes towering solo blasts in the first and the sixth, Roman Anthony's ninth-inning drive supplies the winning run, and Chapman survives a two-run answer to close a seven-homer evening

Baltimore spent the better part of a minute Thursday evening telling Adley Rutschman what he had meant to it, and Rutschman spent the rest of the night showing the city what it had let go. The first man taken in the whole of the 2019 draft, an Oriole and nothing else his entire professional life until the third of August — when a deal struck hours before the deadline sent him to Boston, of all places, a rival within the division — he returned to Camden Yards for the first time wearing the visitors' flannels. The club played a tribute upon the videoboard; the grandstand rose and stayed risen for close to a minute as he dug in; and the man himself called it what it was: "Just to feel that love was a really cool, emotional moment, and really special." Then he compartmentalized, in his own word, and before the first inning was done had put the baseball over the fence.

That first-inning blast was his tenth of the season, and the sixth-inning copy his eleventh — two towering solo shots from a catcher who had missed the three previous games entire with the hyperextended right elbow this paper's ward has been tracking, an ailment the club concedes will want managing to the season's end. "That's a heck of a way to make a return," said Boston's interim manager Chad Tracy, who had watched the ovation from the dugout and pronounced the home club's gesture first-class. Between Rutschman's bookends the contest swung properly: Pete Alonso answered the first blow with a sacrifice fly, and in the fourth Luis Robert Jr. and Alonso struck homers in the same frame — the latter's three-and-thirtieth, the man standing joint first in the American in runs driven home at four-and-ninety — to put Baltimore ahead. Boston drew level on Isiah Kiner-Falefa's seventh-inning single, went ahead on Nick Sogard's two-run drive in the eighth, and banked the run that proved the difference when the rookie Roman Anthony opened the ninth with his third homer. It was wanted: Christian Encarnacion-Strand struck a two-run blow off Chapman in the home ninth before the closer shut the door on his one-and-thirtieth save — seven baseballs over the fences in all, nine of the evening's eleven runs riding upon them. The win fell to the reliever Miller, the loss to De León.

The standings read the evening two ways. Boston, at six-and-seventy wins, sits securely in the wild-card row's second chair; Baltimore has now dropped three running, and stands a game and a half from the last chair with the scrum above it tightening by the day, as the card below relates. But the arithmetic was not really the story, and everyone in the yard knew it. The story went home wearing road grey, having answered a month of upheaval with the finest kind of correspondence: two letters, both delivered over the wall.

News Around the League

THE LAUREL DESK: AUGUST'S GARLANDS ARE DISTRIBUTED — CAGLIANONE AND CROW-ARMSTRONG TAKE THE PLAYERS' PALMS, AND BOTH ANSWER THE HONOR THE SAME EVENING

The league published its August honors Thursday, and the two headline laurels went to young men who promptly justified them by nightfall. The American's Player of the Month is Kansas City's Jac Caglianone, three-and-twenty years old, whose .411 August average and 1.113 OPS led all baseball — the first Royal so honored since Bobby Witt Jr. in the July of 2024 — and who marked the announcement with two hits and two runs driven in against Miami, as the card below relates. The National's palm goes to the Cubs' Pete Crow-Armstrong, his second of the season, earned with a fourteen-homer August that led the majors and helped Chicago to a record no National League club had ever posted: two-and-sixty home runs in a single calendar month. He celebrated by striking his nine-and-thirtieth of the year to beat Milwaukee, the milestone desk below having more to say on that number.

The pitching palms went to Kansas City's Noah Cameron in the American — a 1.87 August ERA with a one-hit shutout of the Angels in it — and Philadelphia's Jesús Luzardo in the National, whose 1.31 figure and six-and-forty strikeouts in four-and-thirty innings and a third continued the summer this paper has watched him assemble. The freshman honors: the White Sox outfielder Tristan Peters, once of the barnstorming Savannah Bananas and now a first-place club's everyday revelation, took the American's rookie palm with a 1.054 OPS; Cincinnati's Sal Stewart, who leads all baseball at one hundred five runs driven in, took the National's. And the relief awards went to a pair of perfect ledgers: Houston's Josh Hader, unscored upon for the whole of August and a spotless one-and-twenty for one-and-twenty in save chances this season, and Milwaukee's Trevor Megill, eight for eight in August with opponents batting .053 against him. The Herald extends its congratulations, and notes with approval that the hardware desk and the box scores are, for once, in perfect agreement.

THE RUMORS DESK: THE METROPOLITANS' EYES TURN ACROSS THE PACIFIC — STEARNS SEEN IN THE STANDS IN JAPAN AS THE SLUGGER SATO WEIGHS THE CROSSING

The winter's first great courtship acquires a sighting. Teruaki Sato, the left-handed slugger of Japan's Hanshin Tigers — the Central League's Most Valuable Player of 2025, forty homers and a hundred and two driven in that season, and two-and-thirty more homers this year with an OPS above a thousand — may come to these shores through the posting system this offseason, and the wire desks report the Mets, Yankees, Dodgers, and Phillies among the clubs linked to him, per the chronicler Heyman of the New York Post. The freshest evidence is the most concrete: the Mets' president of baseball operations David Stearns was seen in attendance Wednesday at the Hanshin nine's engagement with the Yakult Swallows, where Sato obliged the scout in the stands by striking his two-and-thirtieth homer of the year.

The particulars, all duly hedged as reports: Sato is seven-and-twenty, has manned third base for the bulk of his Japanese career, and might be asked to cross the diamond to first were he to land in Queens — the path already walked by the White Sox's Munetaka Murakami, another Japanese third baseman now stationed at first on the South Side and sitting on nine-and-twenty homers in his first American summer. The desk files the story where it files all September courtship: promising, unconsummated, and worth watching when the posting window opens. The reader will note which clubs' names appear in it — the game's great purses, assembled around one bat — and draw the customary conclusions about where the winter's bidding shall be loudest.

THE INFIRMARY WARD: THE GREAT OHTANI TAKES A NIGHT IN THE PARLOR, CHICAGO'S BROWN IS DONE FOR THE YEAR, AND PITTSBURGH EXPECTS ITS PHENOM BACK THIS VERY EVENING

The ward's ledger opens again in Los Angeles, where Shohei Ohtani was held from the batting order Thursday, one night after four strikeouts and a visible wince told the story his manager then confirmed. Roberts owns that the man is "not 100%" upon the mound either — the catalogue runs to the left knee, the right biceps, and what the skipper calls the overall body — though the club yet hopes he pitches again this season, and his return to the lineup is a day-to-day question. His stand-in atop the order, the ward notes with approval, filled the post to the tune of four hits, as the card below sings.

In Chicago the news is harder: the Cubs' right-hander Ben Brown will not pitch again this year. The trouble is a stress reaction in a bone of the neck — the fifth vertebra, by the pitcher's own account — the same affliction that claimed the last three months of his 2024, and a rehabilitation outing of late August brought the discomfort back. "After consulting with doctors, it's just more rest," said the manager Counsell, with the weariness of a man prescribing patience to an athlete. The focus shifts to 2027, and the ward wishes the young man a winter of genuine healing.

Pittsburgh, by contrast, prepares to make a withdrawal from the ward rather than a deposit: the twenty-year-old phenom Konnor Griffin, absent since the fifth of July, is expected to be activated for this evening's opener against the Angels — "He's a huge piece of the offense, defense, baserunning, everything," says the manager Kelly, committing to nothing while telegraphing everything. The surging Buccaneers' account of themselves stands in the card below; the ward merely observes that reinforcements rarely arrive at a better-chosen hour.

THE MILESTONE DESK: CROW-ARMSTRONG STANDS ONE BLOW SHY OF FORTY, A SINGLE HOMER BEHIND SCHWARBER'S CROWN

The number nine-and-thirty deserves a column of its own. Pete Crow-Armstrong's Thursday homer — struck, the trackers certify, off a changeup barely more than a foot above the earth, the lowest pitch he has cleared a fence with all year — brings the Cubs' center fielder within one blow of the forty-homer threshold, a figure no man reaches by accident and few reach at all while also stealing two-and-thirty bases, as he has. Ahead of him on the National's table stands only Philadelphia's Kyle Schwarber at forty even; behind him, Atlanta's Olson at seven-and-thirty and Colorado's Goodman at six-and-thirty. The desk notes besides that Crow-Armstrong ranks among the National's leaders in runs driven in at three-and-ninety and in stolen bases alike, which is why the letters M-V-P attach themselves to his name in every wire dispatch the Herald skims. Three-and-twenty days remain to the season; the arithmetic wants one swing for forty, and the race for the National's home-run crown wants watching every morning between now and the seven-and-twentieth.

THE ALMANAC DESK: CLEMENTE'S DAY IS APPOINTED FOR THE FIFTEENTH, AND THE SEASON'S LAST PAGE FOR THE SEVEN-AND-TWENTIETH

The desk sets down the calendar's fixed stars, each verified against the schedule-keepers at Baseball-Reference. Roberto Clemente Day falls on the fifteenth of September, when all thirty clubs honor the great Pittsburgh right fielder and humanitarian — a date this paper marks each year with particular feeling, the man having been the deadball virtues incarnate a generation after the deadball age. The regular season's final page is the seven-and-twentieth of September, three-and-twenty days from this morning's masthead; the wild-card series open two days later, on the nine-and-twentieth; the division series follow on the third of October, the league championships on the eleventh and twelfth, and the World Series on the three-and-twentieth, with provision made for a seventh game as late as the very eve of November. Between this morning and that last page stand the scrums the desk note below enumerates — three clubs within a game and a half of the American's last chair, three within four games of the National's — and the Herald commends the almanac to any reader tempted to look away for even a weekend.

The Rest of the Card

FIVE RUNS AND NOT ONE OF THEM DRIVEN HOME BY A HIT: BUCCANEERS 5, JINTS 2

Pittsburgh took the set from San Francisco by a method the scorers will be explaining for a week: five runs, and not a single one of them cashed by a base hit. The ledger reads a bases-loaded walk to Oneil Cruz, a bases-loaded plunking of Jake Mangum, a sacrifice bunt turned run by a throwing error, a force-out challenge overturned in Pittsburgh's favor, and Jared Triolo's sacrifice fly — an inventory of small coinage that a bullpen day made stand up, the decision falling to Khristian Curtis, recalled from Indianapolis only that morning, with Montgomery sealing his eighth save. The visitors' whole account was a two-run seventh built on Jung Hoo Lee's double. It is the Buccaneers' fifth series win in their last six tries — "the fight in this team is real," testifies Mangum, and the wild-card arithmetic, though still unkind, has stopped laughing — and the ward above has the gladder news besides: the phenom Griffin is expected back in the lineup this very evening against the Angels.

FOUR IN THE FIFTH SETTLE THE AFTERNOON, AND TORONTO POCKETS THE TIEBREAKER BESIDES: TORONTOS 6, FOREST CITY NINE 3

The set's finale in Cleveland went to the visitors on one crowded fifth inning: Nathan Lukes's two-run double the heavy freight, Vladimir Guerrero Jr. adding a sacrifice fly and Jesús Sánchez a run-scoring single — four runs where the home nine had led. Cleveland's answer, two in the home half on Austin Hedges's single, was the last of it; Ernie Clement singled home the sixth run in the ninth, Miles took the decision, and the loss fell to Bibee. The freight the game carried outweighed the game: the win closes Toronto's account with Cleveland at four games to two this season, and by the tiebreaking rules that head-to-head verdict could prove the season's most valuable coin — the Torontos stand this morning a half-game from the very chair the Forest City nine still hold, with Texas level beside them and Baltimore a game astern. "I loved the way we bounced back," said the manager Schneider, whose club has won six of ten and proceeds to Kansas City tonight.

ALTUVE STRIKES FIRST AND VARSHO TRIPLES THE ARGUMENT HOME: ASTROS 6, WHITE SOX 2

The evening under Houston's roof belonged to the home nine from the second pitch of consequence: Jose Altuve's two-run homer, his fifteenth, before an out had been recorded. Chicago drew level in the second on singles from Rogers and Antonacci, whereupon the third settled everything — Isaac Paredes's run-scoring double, Daulton Varsho's two-run triple, and Christian Walker's single, four runs in the frame. Hunter Brown did the rest: six innings, two runs, seven strikeouts for his fifth victory, the bullpen finishing without incident while Castillo bore the loss. The Space City club has won seven of ten and holds the West by two games over Texas, with the wire desks now openly discussing the American's second seed; the White Sox, twice beaten running, nonetheless keep the Central by three over Cleveland — whose afternoon the card above chronicles — and the season series between these leaders concluded with honors worth counting come tiebreak time.

GAUSMAN AND HENDERSON DUEL AT SEVEN INNINGS APIECE, AND CROW-ARMSTRONG'S THIRTY-NINTH DECIDES IT: BRUINS 2, SUDS MEN 1

The Wrigley argument's fourth and final chapter produced the pitching the first three withheld. Logan Henderson gave Milwaukee seven innings of three hits and seven strikeouts and was beaten; Kevin Gausman — the deadline acquisition doing precisely the office he was acquired for — gave Chicago seven innings of five hits and nine strikeouts and was not. The difference was two runs in the third: Pete Crow-Armstrong reached down for a changeup scarcely a foot off the ground and sent it to dead center at better than a hundred and four miles to the hour, his nine-and-thirtieth of the season, with Carson Kelly aboard. Andrew Vaughn's answering solo blast in the fourth was Milwaukee's entire account, and Rolison closed the ninth for his second save. "Absolutely dominant," ruled Crow-Armstrong of his pitcher, and the arithmetic ruled the set a draw: two games apiece, the Central's gap holding at eight, and the Suds men — beaten with but five hits by a club that managed only three — reminded that the best record in baseball buys no immunity at Wrigley Field. The milestone desk above weighs what the young center fielder's number means; his club merely banks the win and sails for Miami.

JENSEN'S MOONSHOT SAILS FOUR HUNDRED AND FORTY FEET INTO THE ROYAL ANNALS: KANSAS CITY NINE 7, GULF STREAM NINE 3

The friendly rivalry this paper glimpsed Wednesday — the rookie catcher chasing his clubmate's homer count — added its next chapter at Kauffman Stadium. Carter Jensen, who homered twice in Wednesday's losing cause as these pages recorded, tripled in the third and in the fifth struck a go-ahead two-run blast the trackers measured at four hundred forty feet: his two-and-twentieth of the year, his third in two nights, and a figure matching Bo Jackson's 1987 and Carlos Beltrán's 1999 for the second-most ever by a Royals rookie, two shy of Bob Hamelin's four-and-twenty. Jac Caglianone, crowned the American's Player of the Month that very day as the laurel desk relates, answered with a game-tying double and a run-scoring single — "I've got to freaking start hitting homers," the man protested, guarding his one-homer lead in the clubhouse race — and a three-run eighth built on Salvador Perez's and Vinnie Pasquantino's doubles closed the accounts. Wacha took the decision over Alcantara. Miami's portion was a leadoff homer from Heriberto Hernández, his three-and-twentieth, and Agustín Ramírez's solo blow; the Gulf Stream nine thus slip three games from the National's last chair with the season running short, while last-place Kansas City, at three-and-sixty wins, plays September for 2027 — and on Thursday's evidence, 2027 answers.

QUANTRILL THROWS SEVEN CLEAN, AND THE AMERICAN'S BEST ARE BLANKED ON THREE HITS: TEXAS NINE 6, SUNSHINE NINE 0

Arlington handed the league's best record a whitewashing. Cal Quantrill carried a seldom-used slider through seven innings of two hits and five strikeouts — the wire desks report the pitch has quietly become his September weapon — and Tampa Bay finished the night with three hits entire, McClanahan bearing the loss. The scoring gathered by degrees: Foscue's single in the fourth, Duran's in the fifth ahead of Corey Seager's two-run homer, his fourteenth, and a two-run eighth on Evan Carter's triple and Jake Burger's sacrifice fly. The consequences run in two directions at once. The Sunshine nine have now dropped two straight, though their lead in the East holds at three games over the Yankees; the Texas nine, for their part, climb within a half-game of the American's last chair — level in the loss column with the Torontos, whose own afternoon the card above relates — and the same clubs go again at 8:05 this evening with the scrum growing less theoretical by the hour.

DOWN THREE BEFORE AN OUT WAS BANKED, AND EVERY RUN RECOVERED WITH INTEREST: WHITE ELEPHANTS 7, SEATTLE NINE 4

Seattle scored three times before recording an out of its own first inning — Julio Rodríguez's double, Cal Raleigh's single, and a knock from the celebrated debutant Montes, whose arrival the debut desk sang on Wednesday — and had been outplayed thoroughly by the ninth. The visitors' answer ran to fourteen hits: Zack Gelof's leadoff homer to open the game, his sixteenth, Lawrence Butler's two-run single in the fifth, Donovan Walton's go-ahead blast in the sixth, and insurance from Muncy and Jeff McNeil in the late innings, Blewett winning in relief and Harris sealing his fourteenth save. The sixth inning supplied the theatre: directly after Walton's homer, the reliever Vargas ran a cutter up and in to strike Brian Serven, the umpire Hoye gathered his crew and warned both benches, and Seattle's manager Dan Wilson, emerging to ask why, was ejected for the asking — his fourth removal of the season, the most by a Mariners manager since 2022. "I was looking for an explanation," said Wilson afterward, still without one. The Elephants have won two running in the yard of a fading club; the Seattle nine have lost six of their last ten and their manager's patience besides.

THE NATIONAL'S SAVES LEADER IS TOUCHED FOR TWO WITH THE GAME IN HIS HANDS, AND TEOSCAR SENDS THE RAVINE HOME HAPPY: DODGERS 3, MOUND CITY NINE 2

The finale at Chavez Ravine turned on one full-count sinker. St. Louis had led since the first — run-scoring singles from the young Joshua Báez and Ramón Urías building a 2-0 account, twelve hits in all against a Dodger six — and Skubal, touched for ten hits without one of them going for extra bases, had kept the champions within reach at two runs through the middle innings; a fifth-inning wild pitch brought Edman home for their first coin. So it stood at 2-1 into the ninth, when Riley O'Brien — the National's saves leader at five-and-thirty, whose five-and-thirtieth closed Wednesday's account in these very pages — was asked for one more. Edman singled, Betts walked, and Teoscar Hernández crushed the 3-2 sinker to the gap in left-center: both runners home, the game over, the blown save O'Brien's sixth. It was the champions' only hit of the night with a runner in scoring position — they had gone hitless in their previous four-and-twenty such chances across two evenings — and Tommy Edman, keeping the leadoff post while the great Ohtani rested as the ward relates, supplied four of the six hits himself. "Teo came through huge," said Edman, and the tables agree: the Dodgers salvage the set's last game at three-and-eighty wins, while the Mound City nine, restored to level only Wednesday, slip a game under it once more — the season's cruelest denomination of coin, spent twice in two days.

The tables on the fourth morning of September, taken in one turning. In the American: Tampa Bay's lead in the East is three over New York, with Boston seven and a half astern; the White Sox keep the Central by three over Cleveland; Houston holds the West by two over Texas. The wild-card row reads New York and Boston comfortable — and then the morning's best theatre, tighter by a half-game than yesterday: Cleveland's last chair at seventy wins and seventy defeats, with Texas and Toronto a half-game behind, Baltimore a game and a half, and Minnesota three. In the National: Atlanta by four over Philadelphia; Milwaukee, still owner of the game's best ledger, by eight over Chicago; Los Angeles by nine and a half over Arizona — and the last chair Arizona's by half a game over San Diego, with Miami three back and St. Louis four. Tonight's card runs to sixteen engagements — a twin bill in Cleveland among them, the Tigers visiting at 2:10 and again at 7:15 — all verified against the schedule, and the desk directs the eye to four: at 6:40 in Philadelphia the month's handsomest pitching card, Sale and his 2.06 opposing Sánchez and his sixteen victories, the second and third names on the National's earned-run table meeting head-on; at 6:45 in Pittsburgh the Angels arrive as the phenom Griffin is expected back in Buccaneer flannels; at 8:05 in Arlington the Sunshine nine seek their remedy for last night's whitewashing, Martinez opposing Williams; and at 9:40 in San Diego the Yankees at last unveil the Fried-and-Warren piggyback these pages have twice advertised, Buehler standing against it. The night owls are commended to Chavez Ravine at 10:10, where Snell takes the hill for the champions against the capital club. Until the morrow's dispatch, keep your scorecards near and your enthusiasms nearer.
