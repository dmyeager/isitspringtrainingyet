The Game of the Day

THE UNDERSTUDY BRINGS DOWN THE HOUSE — HALPIN'S THREE-RUN THUNDER TURNS THE SUMMIT'S FINAL ACT, AND CLEVELAND STANDS ALONE ATOP THE CENTRAL FOR THE FIRST TIME SINCE INDEPENDENCE DAY: GUARDIANS 6, WHITE SOX 3

Pressed into center field only because the ward had emptied it, Petey Halpin answers Pham's return-day blow with the biggest swing of his life; Messick fans eight, one Smith loses what the other Smith seals, and the half-game lead changes uniforms with ten mornings left to run

For the first time since the morning of the Fourth of July, the top line of the American's Central reads Cleveland — and the swing that turned the column belonged to the one man in the building with no billing on the playbill. Petey Halpin, four-and-twenty years old, is a reserve outfielder by trade; he stood in center field yesterday afternoon only because the invalid list had claimed DeLauter and Martínez for a second consecutive day. In the sixth, his club down a run, Fry and Hedges walked ahead of him and the left-hander Newcomb summoned to face him, Halpin took a 3-1 sinker that its author later confessed was "just middle" and struck it three-and-ninety feet past three hundred to right — a three-run blow, just the fourth hit of his major-league life off a southpaw, and by his own accounting the biggest swing of his baseball life. He tossed the bat aside, stared a long moment at his own dugout, and hop-stepped backward toward first while the park came down around him. "I knew I hit well off the bat," he deposed. "Having that moment and sharing it with the guys in a big game, a big series, it was really cool." In the dugout the winning pitcher confessed to assaulting the water cooler out of sheer joy; the manager Vogt put it plainer: "He's ready for the moment."

The ledger around the thunderbolt deserves its lines. Cleveland's first two runs were a gift of Chicago carpentry — the second baseman Meidroth dropped the tag on a double play that wasn't, in the third, and Adell cashed the mistake with a two-run single ("Maybe keep the other hand on the ball?" offered the Chicago manager Venable, a touch grimly). The visitors' answer was the day's second-best story: Tommy Pham, activated only that morning from the calf strain that had shelved him since the third of the month, struck a three-run homer measured at a dozen feet past four hundred — his first of the year — to put the Pale Hose ahead 3-2 in the fifth. It was the whole of the damage against Parker Messick, who gave six innings with eight strikeouts for his twelfth victory and whose name now rides both the Rookie of the Year and Cy Young ballots-to-come. Ramírez's second double of the afternoon — his first extra-base hits of the month — made it 6-3, and the ninth resolved into the connoisseur's curiosity: the loss charged to Chicago's Smith, the save banked by Cleveland's, the seven-and-thirtieth of Cade Smith's year. "Coming into this series, it was a playoff game for every person that walked into this building," said Messick, and no one in either clubhouse argued.

Now the arithmetic, which is the morning's true headline. Cleveland leads by half a game — but the tiebreaker remains Chicago's, banked Monday as this paper has twice recorded, so the Cleveland nine must finish clear, not level. Nine games remain to them, ten to Chicago, and the road ahead is gentler for the deposed on paper — Tigers, Royals, and Rockies for Chicago, against Cleveland's Athletics, Red Sox, and Royals. The victory also lifts Cleveland a game and a half clear of the West's deadlocked co-leaders for the American's second seed and the first-round bye that rides with it. And tonight offers the strangest wrinkle of all: Chicago hosts Detroit while Cleveland sits idle, an evening on which the deposed can retake a share of the throne without their rival throwing a pitch. "Giving up the division lead, everybody will feel how they feel about it," said Venable. "But I'm proud of the guys and I think we're in a good spot moving forward." Vogt, whose club stands seven-and-seventy wins against seven-and-forty defeats in Septembers since 2022, declined the laurel: "It feels good. It doesn't mean anything yet, right? We've got a job to do for nine more games." The desk has called this fixture the week's own since Monday; it now calls the Central what it has been all along — a coin standing on its edge.

News Around the League

THE INFIRMARY WARD: JUDGE WALKS OFF IN THE SIXTH WITH A TIGHTENED LEG AND SNELL LASTS ONE INNING IN CINCINNATI — TWO PILLARS OF OCTOBER VISIT THE EXAMINING TABLE IN A SINGLE EVENING

The ward's gravest pair arrived within hours of one another. In Minneapolis, Aaron Judge — restored to the Yankee lineup barely a week from three months lost to the fractured rib — felt a stiffness in the lower right leg from the first inning, struck out twice, and was lifted for a pinch-hitter in the sixth of what became the thirteen-inning defeat the card below records. "I tried to kind of play it off and see how long we could go, because these games are important down the stretch," the great man said, "but I thought it'd be the right move to come out." He anticipates no testing; the club will pour two days of treatment into the Thursday off-day and say nothing sooner. "We're not going to speculate one way or the other," said the manager Boone. "We'll just see how he responds."

In Cincinnati the champions' evening went sideways on the second-to-last pitch of a bullpen warm-up. Blake Snell felt the left groin grab, tried the first inning anyway — nineteen pitches, his fastball down a mile and a half — and reported himself to the trainers rather than test a muscle he has strained four times before. "For me to stop, that's a first. Maybe I'm growing up," said the lefty, who had been untouchable since August, four victories and an earned-run figure of 1.04 across six starts. The manager Roberts filed it under "glad it's not worse," but the stakes need no embroidery: "He just can't afford any more setbacks. We've got to make sure that he comes out ready to go in the postseason." The defeat also postponed the West's coronation, as the card relates.

The lesser cots: in Texas, the Rangers' starter Gore fought muscle spasms through four laboring innings — nine-and-sixty pitches, six-and-thirty strikes — before yielding to the extraordinary relief the card below sings; Baltimore's Jackson Holliday went to the ten-day list with inflammation of the left wrist, his replacement answering in the manner the card describes; and Chicago's Montgomery left the summit's finale with a bruised left wrist from a Messick pitch, though the X-rays came back clean and the man is called day-to-day. The ward keeps its lamps trimmed.

THE MILESTONE DESK: TWENTY YEARS AND ONE HUNDRED SEVEN DAYS OLD, AND NO CATCHER SO YOUNG HAS EVER STRUCK TWO IN A GAME — ETHAN SALAS OPENS HIS CAREER'S ACCOUNT WITH BOTH BARRELS IN THE MOUNTAIN AIR

The first home run of Ethan Salas' major-league life left Coors Field at a measured six-and-twenty feet past four hundred, dead to center, on the first pitch he saw in the second inning. The second left three innings later. At twenty years and one hundred seven days, the Padres' boy catcher thereby became the youngest man ever to catch a big-league game and strike two home runs in it — the youngest Padre at any position to do so, the youngest to homer as a catcher since Iván Rodríguez in September of 1991, and the youngest player of any station with a multi-homer game since Guerrero the younger in 2019. The roll of those who managed it younger in the past twenty years reads Trout, Harper, Soto, and one Manny Machado, who now dresses in the same room. "I'm just a kid playing baseball, man," Salas said. "So it's pretty crazy to be in company with such high-caliber guys like that."

The account had opened slowly — hitless in his first fifteen at-bats before Sunday's triple at San Francisco loosened the hinge — but the club promoted him for the glove, not the barrel, and the glove did its own day's work: a shrewd challenge of the machines ended the first inning with two Rockies stranded, and the veteran Ray, throwing to him for the first time, gave six tidy innings and pronounced him "great back there." The manager Stammen took the wider view: "Playing the position that he plays, it makes it doubly tough. He's got a lot thrown at him — and then: 'Oh yeah, you're expected to hit, too.'" The evening's consequences for the pennant arithmetic stand in the card below; the desk here records only that the youngest catcher in five-and-thirty years spent his history-making night grinning like exactly what he claims to be.

THE CLUBHOUSE DESK: TWO DAYS AFTER THROWING ONE HUNDRED ONE PITCHES, THE ACE OF MIAMI ASKS FOR THE NINTH — ALCANTARA'S FIRST SAVE IN NINE SEASONS SENDS THE LAUNDRY CART ROLLING

The manager knew before the game; the starter Gusto pieced it together when he saw the big man lacing his cleats in full uniform in the sixth inning; the rest of the world found out when Sandy Alcantara — one hundred one pitches in Monday's series opener, a single day of rest behind him — strode out of the Miami bullpen to protect a one-run lead in the ninth at Phoenix. Two flyouts, a walk, and a caught-stealing later, the face of the Marlins franchise owned the first save of his nine-season life. He had asked the pitching coach for the assignment before the game, with the closer Fairbanks and the setup man Bender both lost for the year and the bullpen freshly emptied. "I want to die out there for my team," Alcantara said. "That's why I'm here. Every time they give me this opportunity, I will take it."

The wires supply the genealogy: since the save became an official statistic in 1969, only three other men had recorded one on a day they entered leading — or sharing the lead in — all the major leagues in innings pitched: Hershiser in '87, Niekro in '78, Messersmith in '75. Alcantara makes four. The clubhouse afterward gave its stoic ace the rookie's rite — the laundry-cart ride into the showers, beer and shaving cream attending — and the man permitted it, which those who know him rank among the evening's larger surprises. "The dude is an inspiration," said Gusto. "I can't even explain how fired up we were." Even the beaten manager Lovullo tipped a puzzled cap: "That's an elite arm, an elite starting pitcher. I was a little bit confused — but to each their own. They won the baseball game." Miami sits seven and a half from the National's last chair with nine to play, mathematically distant and temperamentally unbeaten.

THE OCTOBER DESK: THE CONTENDERS BEGIN ARRANGING THEIR PIECES — ATLANTA RESTS SALE FOR A WILD-CARD OPENER NOT YET EARNED, AND CROWNED MILWAUKEE GOES HUNTING ROUND NUMBERS

The month's endgame has begun on the blackboards as well as the field. Atlanta — leading the East by four and a half with a magic number of five, the earliest clinching possible Saturday — will not pitch Chris Sale in this weekend's Houston series at all, carrying him instead on six extra days' rest to a final regular-season turn Wednesday against Cincinnati, which in turn sets him up to open a Wild Card series on a rested arm. The ledger justifies the indulgence: the ancient left-hander has made but two starts on regular rest all year and owns a 1.98 earned-run figure across the three-and-twenty starts since the last of them. "It's a little bit of an extended break," allowed the manager Weiss, "but we feel it sets him up for the postseason." The thinness behind him is the worry — López to the shelf, as this paper recorded Tuesday, and the rookie Ritchie schooled at Wrigley in the manner the card below describes.

Milwaukee, its fourth straight Central crown one day old, celebrated by winning again — the particulars stand in the card — and awoke this morning at five-and-ninety victories, two shy of the club's best figure ever, with plain .500 ball across the last ten making the Central's first hundred-win outfit since the 2016 Cubs. The manager Murphy is husbanding arms rather than chasing numbers: Misiorowski was capped at two-and-eighty pitches Tuesday, Henderson at seven-and-eighty last night — his two-and-twentieth consecutive start of three runs or fewer to open a career — and the slumping Harrison auditions for an October role in this very afternoon's finale at Pittsburgh. "We've got work to do," said Murphy, and his closer Megill translated: "We're not resting people. We're playing tooth and nail. I would probably expect nothing less."

THE ALMANAC DESK: THIRTY YEARS AGO THIS VERY NIGHT, THE TORNADO TOUCHED DOWN AT COORS FIELD — NOMO'S NO-HITTER IN THE ONE PARK WHERE NO-HITTERS DO NOT HAPPEN

The almanac asks the reader to set down the standings a moment and mark an anniversary: on the seventeenth of September, 1996 — thirty years ago to this morning's date — Hideo Nomo of the Dodgers threw a no-hitter at Coors Field, the mile-high batting paradise where the great Scully himself had reckoned the feat "impossible." It remains singular: in the near four thousand eight hundred games played at that address since, no man has matched it. The night itself was all improbability — a two-hour rain delay, a cold drizzle, and the whirling "Tornado" windup abandoned for the stretch, out of which the enigmatic right-hander from Japan spun the forkball his old pitching coach Wallace still calls "really two pitches — he would throw it for a called strike and then he'd get a chase." Bichette, the previous year's runner-up for Most Valuable, struck out against it three times. "Those Rockies never saw a split-finger like he threw," says Candiotti, who watched from the same dugout. "These guys now, there are guys with good splitters. But they're not like Nomo's. Not even close. Unreal."

The wires append the desk's favorite grace note, from the man's very debut the year before: mound conference, bases loaded, hundreds of Japanese pressmen watching, and the pitching coach — out of pure instinct — asking his new charge "¿Cómo está?" ("Hey Dave," the catcher Piazza observed, "he's Japanese, not Spanish.") Nomo nodded, answered "Good," and struck the next man out. Thirty years on, with last night's Coors card entry full of a teenager's thunder as the milestone desk sings, the Herald lifts its cap to the night the impossible park stood silent.

THE PIONEER DESK: THE QUEENS DRAW FIRST BLOOD IN THE WOMEN'S LEAGUE CHAMPIONSHIP — MACKAY THROWS FIVE AND TWO-THIRDS FROM THE BULLPEN, AND MO'NE DAVIS SCORES THE TYING RUN

The reader will recall from yesterday's columns that Mo'ne Davis had come to St. Louis to watch her old Monarchs teammate wear a big-league uniform, her own championship business waiting just up the road; that business has now heard from her. In the first championship game the Women's Professional Baseball League has ever played, Davis' Los Angeles Queens beat the first-place San Francisco Firebells seven runs to six — and did it the hard way, down 6-2 after three innings with their starter gone four outs in. The rescue belonged to Jamie Mackay, a power-hitting catcher by trade who had never thrown more than three and two-thirds innings in an outing: she gave five and two-thirds from the bullpen, struck out five, and retired the final nine batters she faced. "I'll do anything to help our team," she said, which the box score renders redundant.

The rally's hinge was the desk's returning heroine: Davis led off the sixth with a double and came home with the tying run on the captain Lansdell's double inside the third-base bag, before the Australian captain Eynon — three singles in three tries already — lifted the go-ahead sacrifice fly. "Baseball is a big game of momentum," said Eynon, the game's named player. The Queens thus lead the best-of-five a game to none, with the second act on the league's bill for this very night. One sandlot in South Philadelphia, the Herald observes, has now furnished a big-league debut and a championship rally in the same week; the man who drags that infield should charge admission.

The Rest of the Card

SEVEN INNINGS OF SCORELESS COMMAND FROM WHEELER, THREE STRIKEOUTS SHY OF TWO THOUSAND — AND THIRTEEN WALKS DRAWN BESIDES: QUAKERS 3, CAPITAL NINE 0

Zack Wheeler gave seven scoreless innings of four hits, facing the minimum across his last three frames and closing his night by blowing a fastball past Crews — the 1,997th strikeout of his career. The Quakers drew thirteen walks off Washington's staff and still stranded thirteen men, Arraez walking thrice and doubling besides; Duran sealed his one-and-thirtieth save, and the Capital nine's four-game winning run, entered in yesterday's columns, is ended. Philadelphia sits half a game behind the Cubs at the head of the wild-card row, with the arithmetic in the October desk above.

FOUR HOME RUNS, A SWEEP OF FLUSHING, AND THE POLAR BEAR'S THREE HUNDRED FIRST — THE BALTIMORES REFUSE TO LEAVE THE RACE: BALTIMORES 7, METS 1

Pete Alonso opened the evening with a four-hundred-ten-foot blast into the second deck — career homer three hundred one, his seven-and-thirtieth of the year, cheered by the Flushing crowd for a third consecutive night — and Jackson, Encarnacion-Strand, and Basallo followed him out, the last pair back-to-back. Bassitt gave six and two-thirds of one-run ball for the sweep, and the Elias bureau notes the same three Baltimore hitters homered in consecutive games for the first time since Davis, Machado, and Trumbo in August of 2016. "Am I surprised that Pete does this? No, because he's literally that good," said Bassitt of his once-and-again teammate. The Baltimores have won three straight and sit two and a half from the American's last chair — alive, if breathing thin air — while Jackson's thunder covers the wrist-sore Holliday, as the ward above records.

THIRTEEN INNINGS IN THE DRIZZLE END ON CARATINI'S SINGLE — THE HIGHLANDERS SQUANDER A LOADED TWELFTH, AND THE GREAT MAN'S EXIT SHADOWS ALL: TWIN CITIES NINE 5, HIGHLANDERS 4

New York was down to its final out in the ninth when Volpe's double off the wall squared the account, and the clubs then traded single runs through the tenth and eleventh like duelists too tired to miss. The turn came in the twelfth — bases full of Highlanders, nobody out, and no runs to show for it, Ramos grounding into a double play with Volpe cut down at the plate — before Victor Caratini singled home Keaschall in the thirteenth to end it. The evening's asterisks: Judge's early exit fills the ward above; Schmidt, activated from his elbow reconstruction that very day — as this paper forecast Monday — threw his first innings of the year; and the pursuit of Tampa Bay stretches to four and a half with the Bronx idle today.

THREE TWO-OUT DOUBLES BEFORE THE FIRST INNING WAS OVER — THE SUNSHINE NINE BANK A FIFTH STRAIGHT AND WATCH THE PURSUIT STUMBLE BY WIRE: SUNSHINE NINE 4, ATHLETICS 3

Caminero, DeLuca, and Simpson strung three consecutive two-out doubles in the first — Simpson's two-hit night lifting his average to .310, level with Houston's Alvarez atop the American's race — and Nick Martinez made the early four stand for his fifteenth victory, the first Ray to the figure since Eflin in 2023. White and Bolte answered with solo thunder before Kelly, Booser, and Sulser (saving on the closer Baker's day of rest) shut the door. With the Yankee defeat arriving by wire an hour earlier, the East's lead swells to four and a half; the house celebration, the wires confide, now features a spinning wheel of forfeits, and last night's commanded DeLuca to hug the manager Cash mid-interview — "the first time he actually brought me in," the outfielder reported with satisfaction.

SCHERZER, TWO-AND-FORTY YEARS OLD, HOLDS THE LINE FOR FIVE — THE CANADIANS STOP DETROIT'S RUN AT SIX AND KEEP THEIR CANDLE LIT: CANADIANS 5, BENGALS 1

The Bengals' six-game surge, sung in yesterday's columns, died at Toronto against the oldest arm in the argument: Max Scherzer, carrying an earned-run figure north of six, gave five innings of one-run ball because his club had no other choice and every need. "Our backs are against the wall," said the future Hall man. "We've got to come out fighting and clawing." Straw's go-ahead single in the fourth and a sixth inning fattened by two Detroit errors did the scoring; Varland sealed his four-and-thirtieth. The ledger stays stern — a game and a half from the last chair, with the tiebreaker now Chicago's, not theirs, since the Central's flip re-sorted the quarry — and the Canadians open at Texas Friday with Cease and Soriano loaded in order.

EOVALDI OUT OF THE BULLPEN TO 'COME TOGETHER' — FOUR SPOTLESS INNINGS, THE LAST ELEVEN MEN RETIRED, AND THE WEST IS A DEAD HEAT: TEXAS NINE 7, HUB NINE 3

With Gore fighting spasms through four labored innings, the Arlington crowd rose in the sixth to a mid-game apparition: Nathan Eovaldi, the co-ace turned emergency fireman, whose four scoreless innings retired the final eleven Boston men and made him the first Texas pitcher to win while throwing four or more spotless frames to end a game since 2007. "It felt almost like a closer coming into the game," said the manager Schumaker. "But it was early — you're just not used to feeling it." Contreras' two-run homer, set up by a shrewd challenge of the machines, was most of Boston's evening; a three-run seventh off the faltering Hub bullpen was most of Texas'. The Texas nine draw dead level with Houston at .500 apiece atop the West — though the Silver Boot tiebreaker is Houston's, nine games to four — while Boston's wild-card cushion holds at five over Chicago.

RAVE AND MASSEY SUPPLY THE THUNDER AND HOUSTON GOES QUIETLY — NOUGHT-FOR-NINE WITH MEN IN SCORING POSITION, AND THE WEST'S THRONE IS SHARED: MISSOURIANS 5, ASTROS 2

John Rave's two-run homer in the fourth and Michael Massey's solo blow in the eighth headed a Kansas City evening that Houston met with a shrug — nought-for-nine with runners in scoring position, one man past second base through seven innings, and a seventh defeat in ten games. Peña's three hits were the local consolation; the veteran Walker, mired at nought-for-nineteen, spoke the clubhouse's creed ("We're in a spot to be in the postseason, and that's what we're fighting for") while the manager Espada compressed the strategy to two words: "Win tomorrow." The Astros share the West with Texas at six-and-seventy wins and losses apiece, tiebreaker in hand, with Atlanta arriving for the weekend.

THE CROWNED CREAM CITY NINE DECLINE THE HANGOVER — FOUR TWO-OUT SINGLES, YELICH'S TWO-STRIKE TURN, AND VICTORY NUMBER FIVE-AND-NINETY: CREAM CITY NINE 5, ALLEGHENYS 4

A day after the champagne, Milwaukee won in its own dogged dialect: four consecutive two-out singles from the order's cellar in the second, then a seventh-inning rally of walk, single, walk, and fielder's choice before Christian Yelich lined a two-strike single the opposite way for the lead. Lowe's two-run homer and Flores Jr.'s solo blow had put the Alleghenys briefly ahead; Uribe and Megill closed the door, the latter's eight-and-twentieth save. The larger designs — Henderson's capped start, the hundred-win arithmetic, Harrison's audition this very afternoon — stand in the October desk above.

CROW-ARMSTRONG STRIKES TWICE MORE AND PASSES SWEET-SWINGIN' BILLY OUTRIGHT — BREGMAN FALLS A SINGLE SHY OF THE CYCLE: CUBS 8, ATLANTAS 4

The chase this paper entered Tuesday at 'level with Billy Williams' lasted exactly one more home game: Pete Crow-Armstrong homered twice — his three-and-fortieth and four-and-fortieth — and tripled besides, passing Williams' 1970 figures outright for the most home runs (and, at one-and-eighty, the most extra-base hits) by a left-handed batter in all the Cubs' annals. It was his tenth career game of ten or more total bases — and his sixth of the summer, tying Cruz's 2019 figure for the most such games in any single season the books have recorded. Alex Bregman doubled, homered, and tripled behind him, then flied out in the seventh with the cycle on the line — "I tried to hit a single. It's stupid. I should've just kept trying to hit a homer," he grinned, before nodding at his teammate: "Pete's on cycle watch when he wakes up in the morning." The rookie Ritchie absorbed the schooling for Atlanta, whose October designs the desk above weighs. The forty-forty ledger stands at seven-and-thirty steals with ten games left; Wrigley waits on three more thefts.

SNELL THROWS NINETEEN PITCHES AND THE CORONATION IS POSTPONED — THE REDLEGS BEAT THE CHAMPIONS' BULLPEN AT ITS OWN LONG GAME: REDLEGS 6, ANGELENOS 2

Needing a victory or a San Diego defeat to seal a thirteenth West flag in fourteen years, the champions got neither: Snell's single inning opened the door — the ward above has his groin and his prognosis — and seven innings of patchwork relief could not hold the line, De La Cruz's two-run homer in the third and Myers' solo blow in the fifth leading a thirteen-hit Cincinnati evening. Stewart and Friedl singled home the insurance while Halvorsen took the loss; Bubic, activated from the sixty-day shelf that very day, threw the ninth. The Angelenos remain nine clear of the Padres with the finale this afternoon — Wrobleski starting, the bullpen game moved up a day by necessity.

TEN INNINGS AT ST. LOUIS AND THE BAY NINE OUTLAST THE SAVE-LEADER HIMSELF — GILBERT'S TENTH SETTLES A GAME THE REDBIRDS TIED TWICE: BAY NINE 6, REDBIRDS 5

Twice St. Louis clawed level — two in the seventh on an error and a wild pitch, two in the ninth on Fermín's sacrifice fly and Bernal's sharp single — and twice was made to pay for the impertinence, Drew Gilbert's two-run homer in the tenth off O'Brien, the National's co-leader in saves, deciding it. Cox stole two bases and singled two home in the seventh — one of them a gift of Walker's errant throw — Kuhnel sealed his eighth, and Saggese's sacrifice fly in the tenth bought the Redbirds back only the one. Two straight now for the Bay nine, playing out an orphaned September with evident professional pride.

NINE RUNS AT DENVER AND THE TEENAGER'S NAME IN THE HISTORY BOOKS — THE PADRES RETURN TUESDAY'S SCORE WITH INTEREST: PADRES 9, DENVER NINE 3

Two nights after the Denver nine snapped their eight-game streak by a 9-3 score, the Padres returned the identical figure with the ledger reversed: Salas' two historic blows — the milestone desk above has the full accounting — headed a thirteen-hit evening, France and Taylor driving two apiece and Harris legging a triple. MARSEE'S ELEVENTH AND A CAUGHT-STEALING FOR THE FINAL OUT — THE FLORIDIANS TAKE THE SERIES AND THE DESERT NINE TAKE THE CONSEQUENCES: FLORIDIANS 4, DESERT NINE 3

Jakob Marsee homered, doubled, stole a base, and drove in two, and Miami led from the second inning and never gave it back — though Arizona's three solo answers, Smith, Moreno, and Arenado in turn, kept the evening honest to its final pitch, when the catcher Mack threw out the pinch-runner Lawlar stealing to end it with the tying run erased. Until the next dispatch, keep your scorecards near and your enthusiasms nearer.
