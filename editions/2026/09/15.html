THE MORNING HORSEHIDE HERALD

"Every Score Set Down, No Deed Unsung"

~ Being a Faithful Daily Chronicle of the National Pastime ~

Volume I · No. 69 · Tuesday, the Fifteenth of September, Two Thousand and Twenty-Six

Reporting 10 contests from the day prior.

The Game of the Day

EIGHTEEN PITCHES, TWELVE OF THEM BEATEN FOUL — A ROOKIE'S WALK CRACKS THE SUMMIT OPEN, AND A SIX-RUN SIXTH GIVES CHICAGO THE SERIES THAT MAY DECIDE THE CENTRAL: WHITE SOX 7, GUARDIANS 3

The fixture of the week delivers its opening argument: Peters outlasts Williams in the longest plate appearance of the major-league season, Teel and Vargas cash the runs behind him, and the White Sox leave Cleveland's own park holding a game-and-a-half lead and the tiebreaker besides

Eighteen pitches. Twelve of them, with two strikes on him, beaten foul by a rookie outfielder who fell behind no-balls-and-two before the sixth inning at Cleveland was a minute old. Tristan Peters against Gavin Williams — the August Rookie of the Month against the big right-hander Cleveland had held back a full week precisely for this series — became, by the count of the bureau's machines, the longest plate appearance in the major leagues this season, tied for the fifth-longest since such things were first written down in 1988, and the equal of Adam Kennedy's 2004 mark for the most pitches ever thrown in a walk. For that is how it ended: ball four, Williams' evening done at a season-high one hundred thirteen pitches, and the floodgates propped open behind him. "It felt like I was up there for like an hour," Peters deposed afterward. "I was exhausted." His opposite number could only tip the cap: "I counted at one point, and I just stopped counting because it was getting up there." The Cleveland manager Vogt, whose club lost the game on the exchange, called it "two very good players going at it — entertaining to say the least," which is a gracious way to describe a mugging.

What followed was the six-run sixth that settled the fixture this desk has called the week's own for three mornings running. Williams had carried a shutout through five despite a bases-loaded escape in the fourth; the relief that inherited his mess found no such fortune. Andrew Benintendi sprinted home when the first baseman Lowe's throw struck Peters on the back of the head — the young man praised by his manager Venable for "getting into the throwing lane," a spring-training fundamental executed at the season's loudest hour — and then the honest artillery: Kyle Teel doubled two across with the bases full, and Miguel Vargas struck a two-run homer, his one-and-thirtieth, a measured two-and-ninety feet past three hundred to left. Six runs in the frame, every one of them chargeable to the ledger of a single walk. The opener Newcomb and the bulk man Sean Burke — five innings, one unearned run, the decision to nine-and-seven — kept the hosts quiet until garbage time, Fry's two-run homer in the ninth the whole of Cleveland's late reply. "It's no secret what's on the line here, so to win the first game is huge," said Burke. "To play in a game like this is a privilege."

The consequences are the sternest of the September. Chicago's hold on the Central widens to a game and a half, and — the connoisseur's item — the victory clinches the season's series between the two clubs, so that a dead heat at the wire now goes to the White Sox. Cleveland, which had rearranged its whole rotation to open this set strong, must now win the two games that remain in it merely to keep the argument alive; the visitors' young left fielder Martínez left the evening clutching a flank besides, as the ward below records. "It's just one game," Williams insisted. "You play 162 for a reason." True enough — but of the 162, the Herald submits, some weigh more than others, and last night's weighed a game and a half plus a tiebreaker. Game two goes at 6:40 this evening, Martin opposing Griffin and his fifteen victories.

News Around the League

THE CLINCHING DESK: TWO MORE TICKETS PUNCHED IN A SINGLE EVENING — THE CHAMPIONS' FOURTEENTH OCTOBER RUNNING TIES THE OLDEST STREAK IN THE BOOKS, AND THE YANKEES' BERTH ARRIVES BY WIRE FROM TORONTO

The postseason's ledger of the admitted doubled itself last night. In Cincinnati the champions dispatched the Reds four-to-one — the particulars stand in the card below — and thereby entered their fourteenth consecutive postseason, drawing level with Atlanta's 1991-to-2005 run for the longest such streak the game has ever kept. The celebration was a handshake line, nothing more; champagne is not uncorked in that clubhouse for formalities. "We don't take it for granted," said Max Muncy, the longest-tenured of them, "but it is the expectation here." The expectations beyond: a thirteenth West flag in fourteen years, held by nine games over San Diego with the tiebreaker banked; a first-round bye, three and a half clear of Atlanta for the second seed; and the game's first three-peat since the Yankees of 1998 to 2000.

Which brings the desk to those Yankees' heirs, whose own ticket arrived by the strangest of couriers. New York beat Minnesota eight-to-three, as the card tells, but the berth itself was sealed in Toronto, where the Blue Jays' ninth-inning stumble against Detroit did the arithmetic for them — a third consecutive October for the Bronx, and the eighth in the manager Boone's nine years. "We've been through a lot this year with injuries, with adversity," Boone allowed amid the clubhouse noise. "Through all the ups and downs, they've been a together group." The pitcher Warren put the house's ambition more plainly: "This is the baseline. This is what we're expected to do." Four clubs now hold October's paper — Tampa Bay, Milwaukee, Los Angeles, New York — and the reader will note the season's first two admissions came by walk-off thunderbolt and twenty-run carnival, the latest two by handshake and by telegram. September distributes its drama unevenly.

THE LAUREL DESK: THE FINAL CY YOUNG CANVASS IS UNANIMOUS TWICE OVER — SCHLITTLER AND MISIOROWSKI, EACH AT 1.95, SLAM THE DOOR ON BOTH LEAGUES AT ONCE

Only yesterday these columns recorded the Bronx chatter drifting from Cy Young toward Most Valuable; this morning the wires certify that the first conversation, at least, is over. MLB.com's final poll of the season — two-and-thirty voters, ranking five deep — returned Cam Schlittler of the Yankees and Jacob Misiorowski of the Brewers as unanimous first choices in their leagues, every first-place vote to both men. The symmetry is the connoisseur's delight: each leads his league with an earned-run figure of precisely 1.95, tied for the lowest among all qualified pitchers in the game. Schlittler has allowed one run or fewer in each of his last seven starts; Misiorowski's two hundred six-and-thirty strikeouts are tied for the major-league lead — with Toronto's Cease, the American's runner-up, whose own two hundred six-and-thirty are a personal high and whose .177 average against is the league's stingiest. The rest of the American's ballot ran Messick, Rasmussen, Gray; the Herald notes with approval that an award poll and a pennant race have rarely shared so many principals. Misiorowski, as it happens, takes the hill this very evening at Pittsburgh — on Roberto Clemente Day, in the great man's own park, as the almanac below explains. The voters may be finished; the season is not.

THE MILESTONE DESK: FLUSHING CHEERS ITS POLAR BEAR IN VISITING ORANGE — ALONSO'S HOMECOMING FINDS HIM ONE BLOW SHY OF THREE HUNDRED

Pete Alonso walked to the visitors' end of a long and familiar hallway at Citi Field last night, dressed for the first time in his life to play against the Mets — and the house that watched him hit two hundred four-and-sixty home runs in its colors, more than any Met who ever lived, declined to treat him as a visitor. They chanted his name at batting practice, chanted it again after the anthem, and rose for a tribute reel before the first pitch; the man tipped his cap to the thunder. "Honestly, it's a little weird," he confessed. "I lived a lot of life here... It's much appreciated." The evening's line was a quiet nought-for-three with a walk, but the ledger around it is anything but quiet: those two hundred four-and-sixty homers as a Met plus the five-and-thirty of this Baltimore summer leave him at two hundred nine-and-ninety for his life — one swing from the three-hundred club — with his hundred runs driven leading all the American besides. The wires note his favorite memory of the old place remains the winning blow he struck against Milwaukee in the Wild Card round of 2024. The new place got the winning blow last night from another hand entirely, as the card below sings, and the Herald suspects Flushing will cheer rather less if number three hundred arrives before the set concludes.

THE INFIRMARY WARD: MIAMI FOLDS MEYER'S TENT FOR THE YEAR, ATLANTA'S LÓPEZ JOINS THE SHELF WITH ELEVEN TO PLAY, AND CLEVELAND HOLDS ITS BREATH OVER A FLANK

The gravest entry is Miami's. Max Meyer, an All-Star in the season's first half — the Marlins' first starter so honored since Alcantara in 2022 — has thrown his last pitch of 2026: the club halted his throwing program and shut him down, the neck strain that has shelved him since July refusing to yield. "He just wasn't able to get past the hump," said the manager McCullough, who chose a sound offseason over a longshot September. The man leaves behind one hundred eleven innings, far his most, and a sweeper the measuring machines rank among the game's best; the ward wishes the arm a quiet winter.

Atlanta's file thickens at the worst hour. Reynaldo López, chased in the third inning at Wrigley as the card below records, goes to the fifteen-day invalid list with discomfort of the right shoulder — "we've got to change course here," said the manager Weiss — and the East leaders, four and a half up with eleven to play, are down to Sale, Mahle, Holmes, and Pérez among starters who figure in October. The rookie Ritchie comes up from Gwinnett for tomorrow's turn, and Smith-Shawver's five innings of relief last night read like an audition for the playoff bullpen. In Cleveland, meanwhile, the young outfielder Angel Martínez winced through a seventh-inning swing and left the summit's opener with inflammation of the right flank — "we pulled Angel out of precaution," said Vogt; "we'll know more tomorrow" — words no club cares to speak with the division on the table. The lesser entries: Toronto's Rudy Martin Jr. to the ten-day list with a hamstring, and the champions' reliever Henriquez shelved with a barking lower back, the once-heralded Bobby Miller recalled to fill the room. The candle burns for all.

THE ALMANAC DESK: THE ONE-AND-TWENTY FLIES ON EVERY BREAST TODAY — ROBERTO CLEMENTE DAY ARRIVES, AS THESE COLUMNS HAVE TWICE PROMISED

Twice this week the almanac has pointed the reader to the fifteenth of September; it is here. Across all thirty parks today the game honors Roberto Clemente — fifteen times an All-Star, three thousand hits exactly, and a man who died on New Year's Eve of 1972 flying relief to earthquake-stricken Nicaragua — with a No. 21 patch on every jersey in the major leagues. At Pittsburgh, where he played the whole of his eighteen years, every Pirate, coach, and manager wears the number entire for tonight's set with Milwaukee; nominees for the Clemente Award, past winners, and players of Puerto Rican blood may do likewise wherever they stand. The year's nominees, one to a club, make a roll the Herald is pleased to print in part: Arizona's Carroll, who as the card below attests spent last midnight authoring a walk-off; Detroit's ancient Verlander, whose homecoming these pages have kept in pencil; Kansas City's Witt; the Mets' Lindor; and Baltimore's Alonso, nominated in his first autumn away from Flushing. The day opens Hispanic Heritage Month across the game. The award itself — for character, for the community, for the game carried beyond the foul lines — is the only laurel in the sport named for what a man does when no box score is kept, and the Herald holds it the equal of any column it prints.

The Rest of the Card

SEVEN SPOTLESS INNINGS FROM THE DEADLINE PRIZE, AND THE TICKET IS TAKEN WITH A HANDSHAKE — TUCKER AND HERNÁNDEZ SUPPLY THE THUNDER: BOYS IN BLUE 4, REDS 1

The clinching evening itself was almost businesslike, which was rather the point. Skubal, the mid-summer prize, gave seven scoreless innings of three hits with nine strikeouts — among his finest work in the new livery — while Kyle Tucker opened the account with his fifteenth homer, a third in five games from a bat warming at the season's best hour, and Teoscar Hernández followed a Tucker walk with a two-run blow, his fourteenth. Kiké Hernández singled home the fourth; De La Cruz's seven-and-twentieth homer off Phillips in the ninth spared Cincinnati the blanking. The fourteenth consecutive October thus secured — the full accounting stands in the clinching columns above — the champions permitted themselves a handshake line and turned to the next errand: Yamamoto opposes Lowder tonight in the set's second act.

A SIX-RUN EIGHTH IN A STEADY DRIZZLE, CROWNED BY THE GREAT MAN'S FIRST THUNDER SINCE THE INVALID LIST — JUDGE'S MOONSHOT FINDS THE SECOND DECK: PINSTRIPES 8, TWINS 3

The evening at Target Field was played in a drizzle and decided in a downpour. Trailing two-to-one into the eighth — Caballero's fourteenth homer the early account, answered when Emmanuel Rodriguez, the debutant these pages sang on Sunday, tripled home both Minnesota runs — New York stacked six in the frame: Caballero and Rice singled home three between them before Aaron Judge turned on a 2-2 cutter from Minter and deposited it in the second deck in left, a vintage moonshot measured at fifteen feet past four hundred. It was the great man's eighteenth homer and his first since returning from three months lost to a fractured rib. "The ultimate goal is to be ready for the postseason," he said afterward — a postseason whose ticket arrived mid-celebration by wire from Toronto, as the clinching columns record. Wells added his twelfth homer, Warren gave five and two-thirds without an earned run, and the pursuit of Tampa Bay holds at three and a half, with the four-game set in the Bronx on the twenty-second looming as the East's last argument.

THE WALK-OFF SWORD, SO LATELY MIAMI'S, TURNS IN THE HAND — CARROLL'S NINTH-INNING BOLT CAPS A RALLY FROM THREE DOWN: RATTLERS 8, GULF STREAM NINE 7

Two mornings ago these pages sang Miami's ninth-inning ambush of the champions; last midnight in the desert the same blade turned. The visitors led seven-to-four after seven honest innings — Sanoja a triple and a homer among three driven, Marsee a homer besides, and Burnes reached for five runs in under five frames — before Jordan Lawlar's two-run homer in the eighth drew Arizona within one. The ninth was cruelty by installments: the closer Fairbanks walked Moreno, threw a single pitch to Perdomo, and left the game hurt; the reliever Ralston inherited the bunt that moved the pinch-runner Troy to second; and Corbin Carroll, who confessed he had spent the pitching change planning the at-bat, got the hung splitter he was hunting and struck it for a two-run, walk-off homer, his one-and-twentieth. "One of those catalyst type of games that pushes you forward," ruled the manager Lovullo. The arithmetic: Arizona holds at two and a half behind streaking San Diego — three in the loss column — with the season's final weekend at Petco still fated for the two of them; a third straight defeat, and the spiral was on. The club also claimed the outfielder Jesús Sánchez off Toronto's waiver wire in the morning, stocking the bench for the stretch. Miami, which shut its All-Star Meyer down for the year as the ward reports, flies home with a hard week's lesson: the walk-off is a lender, and it always calls the loan.

SIX HITLESS INNINGS UNDONE IN A QUARTER HOUR, AND THE NIGHT ENDS ON A THROW THAT CLIPS THE CLOSER'S GLOVE — A FIFTH STRAIGHT FOR DETROIT: TIGERS 6, JAYS 5

Toronto was one out into the seventh inning of a no-hitter and, by the eighth, one mistake from October's exits. Soriano carried the no-hit bid six and a third before Detroit landed five hits around a single out — Dingler's run-scoring single, Max Clark's two-run double, Torkelson's double behind it — and a two-run lead became a four-to-two deficit in minutes. Okamoto's three-run answer, his three-and-thirtieth homer, briefly restored the house's roar; Callahan tied it in the eighth; and the ninth supplied the season in miniature: Guerrero ranged far to his right and threw true, but the ball clipped the covering closer Varland's glove and rolled away, McKinstry racing home with the winning run. "I thought Vlad made a good throw," said the manager Schneider. "We just didn't execute it." Kinley took the decision, Jansen sealed his twentieth save, and the schooling Tigers — winners of five running with the Verlander homecoming still penciled at the calendar's edge — play kingmaker with evident relish. For the Jays the ledger is grim reading: a game behind Cleveland's chair, holders of the tiebreaker over the Clevelands as this paper has noted — but one-and-five against Chicago, so the Central's own argument now bears directly on which club they must catch. The same clubs go again at 7:07 tonight.

FOUR HITS, THE HUNDREDTH RUN DRIVEN, A SEVEN-AND-THIRTIETH THEFT, AND A TWO-AND-FORTIETH HOMER TO STAND BESIDE SWEET-SWINGIN' BILLY: CUBS 7, TOMAHAWK NINE 3

Pete Crow-Armstrong gave Wrigley Field the full catalogue: a two-run double in the second that carried his runs driven to one hundred exactly, a single and his seven-and-thirtieth stolen base in the fourth — the forty-forty ledger, entered in these pages Saturday at four steals wanting, now wants but three — a two-run homer in the sixth, his two-and-fortieth, and a single in the eighth for good measure. The homer is the historian's item: it draws him level with Billy Williams' 1970 figure for the most in a season by any left-handed batter in all the Cubs' long annals. "It's great company to keep," ruled the manager Counsell; the young man, who counts the Hall of Famer a friend, allowed that this was one distinction "I'll buy into." Peterson — a Met, the reader will smile, when Chicago bought this very future in 2021 — held Atlanta to a run into the seventh; Yastrzemski's pinch two-run homer dressed the visitors' line late. The Braves' evening cost them double: López lasted but three innings and went to the invalid list by midnight, as the ward above tells, and the East's lead is pared to four and a half over Philadelphia. The Cubs, for their part, sit atop the National's wild-card row and welcome the same clubs back at 7:40 tonight.

ON THE NIGHT FLUSHING CHEERED ITS OLD HERO, THE ONLY THUNDER THAT COUNTED WAS BALTIMORE'S — HENDERSON'S EIGHTH-INNING BLAST KEEPS THE CANDLE LIT: ORIOLES 2, METS 1

The sentiment belonged to Alonso, as the milestone columns above attest; the decision belonged to Gunnar Henderson. With the account level at one in the eighth, the shortstop turned on a slider from Núñez and sent it seven-and-ninety feet past three hundred over the wall in right-center — his three-and-twentieth homer, his first since the thirtieth of August, and the end of a twelve-game drought of runs driven that had shadowed a difficult year. "I feel like I've been swinging at a lot of the right pitches," he said. "Glad I came through then." Kjerstad's run-scoring double had opened the evening's business; the rookie Tong gave the Flushing nine six fine innings around Ewing's tenth homer, and got nothing for them. Baltimore's Young walked five and stranded eight across five stubborn innings before four relievers closed the door, Garcia sealing it. Two straight now for the obstinate Orioles, three games from the American's last chair — and the wires observe that Henderson, at five-and-twenty, has a hit in eight of his last ten as he plays a hard season out with evident pride.

SIX RUNS BY THE THIRD, THEN A LONG EVENING OF WHITE KNUCKLES IN THE MOUNTAIN AIR — THE EIGHTH STRAIGHT MATCHES THE SEASON'S BEST: SAN DIEGO NINE 8, MOUNTAIN MEN 7

The surge this paper has chronicled through a full week reached eight consecutive victories by the now-customary method: a fortune banked early and defended with the fingernails. Machado opened with his eight-and-twentieth homer, the second inning produced four more — Tatis' two-run blow, his two-and-twentieth, the heaviest — and at six-to-one the evening looked a stroll. Coors Field permits no strolls: Colorado's fourteen hits kept arriving, McCarthy's two-run triple and Carrigg's homer among them, and the margin required Ty France — activated from the paternity list only that afternoon — to single home the seventh run with two out in the seventh, part of a three-hit return. "It's just the time of year where you've got to win," said the new father. "It doesn't matter how you do it." Miller sealed his seven-and-thirtieth save with the tying run aboard. The third wild-card chair stays San Diego's by two and a half over Arizona — three in the loss column, the figure that matters — while the Rockies absorb an eighth straight defeat, their five-and-ninetieth of a long year. The same clubs go again at 8:40 tonight.

TEN MEN UP, TEN MEN DOWN, AND NOT A HIT UNTIL THE SIXTH — THE ROOKIE MATHEWS DEALS, BERNAL STEERS, AND BURLESON REACHES ONE HUNDRED TEN: CARDINALS 2, GIANTS 1

St. Louis' September of auditions produced its finest recital yet. The rookie left-hander Quinn Mathews retired the first ten San Francisco batters, carried a no-hit bid into the sixth until McCray's double broke it, and finished with eight strikeouts across six and two-thirds innings of a single run — crediting his fellow rookie, the catcher Bernal, for the navigation: "I think I shook one pitch in three innings." Bernal steered and hit besides, three hits with a run driven in, while Burleson's run-scoring single carried his league-leading count to one hundred ten. The reliever Bruihl stranded the bases full in the seventh with the night's only batter faced, and O'Brien sealed his seven-and-thirtieth save. The Giants' whole account was Cox's single; a fourth consecutive defeat deepens the gloom of an outfit playing out the string without Devers, whose season, as yesterday's ward recorded, ended on a surgeon's calendar. The clubs meet again at 7:45 this evening.

A PERFECT RELAY SETS THE TONE AND DETMERS BANKS A SEVENTH STRAIGHT QUALITY START — ONE STRIKEOUT NOW FROM TWO HUNDRED: ANGELS 6, PUGET SOUND MEN 4

The first inning at Anaheim told the whole evening in miniature: Crawford aboard, Arozarena's double into the gap, and the relay — Siri to Neto, the shortstop's throw home registered at seven-and-ninety miles to the hour and a shade more — cutting the runner down at the plate. "That relay set the tone," ruled the manager Suzuki, and behind it Reid Detmers did what he has done all the season's second half: six innings, three runs — all on Raleigh's three-run homer — and eight strikeouts for a seventh consecutive quality start. Since the All-Star interval his earned-run figure stands at 1.65, the best in the major leagues, and his strikeout count rests at one hundred nine-and-ninety: one more makes him the first Angel to two hundred since Ohtani in 2022, and the first left-handed one since Finley in 1999. Neto — the club's Clemente nominee, the almanac notes — tripled home a run and scored twice, Siri collected three hits, and the rookie Guzman's two-run single supplied the cushion. Seattle's long season leaks another; the same clubs conclude at 9:38 tonight.

The Tuesday tables, then — with four flags of certification now flying. In the American: clinched Tampa Bay holds the East by three and a half over clinched New York, Boston eight and a half astern of the leaders; the Central is Chicago's by a game and a half over Cleveland with the season's tiebreaker banked besides, as the lead columns sing, and game two of the summit goes tonight; the West, where neither principal played, stands Houston by a single game over Texas. The wild-card row: New York and Boston seated in comfort, the last chair Cleveland's by one over Toronto — who hold the tiebreaker on the Clevelands but not on the White Sox, a distinction that may decide their October — with Texas a game and a half back and Baltimore three. In the National: Atlanta by four and a half over Philadelphia, its rotation thinning as the ward reports; clinched Milwaukee by nine and a half over Chicago; the clinched champions by nine over San Diego. The wild-card ledger: the Cubs at the row's head, Philadelphia a half-game behind them, San Diego in the third chair by two and a half over Arizona — three in the loss column — with Pittsburgh seven back and St. Louis seven and a half. The desk's Monday bill delivered in full: the summit to Chicago, the champions' clinch at Cincinnati, the Yankees' evening ended in a berth, and the desert matter settled by walk-off. Tonight's bill is the full board of fifteen, verified against the schedule, and the desk commends four engagements to the reader's lamp: at 6:40 the summit's second act in Cleveland, Martin opposing Griffin and his fifteen victories; at 6:40 the unanimous Misiorowski takes the hill at Pittsburgh on Clemente Day, every Pirate wearing the one-and-twenty; at 6:45 Sánchez, seventeen-and-five, carries Philadelphia into Washington; and at 7:07 Toronto, wounded twice over, tries Detroit again with the arithmetic tightening at its back. The almanac stands at attention today: Roberto Clemente Day across all the leagues, as the columns above record. Twelve mornings remain to the season's final page on the seven-and-twentieth, and the Wild Card rounds open on the nine-and-twentieth. Until the next dispatch, keep your scorecards near and your enthusiasms nearer.
